# Cleft and ellipsis

Two ways English puts emphasis on one part of a sentence and leaves the
rest out. Both are essential in speech, both are used constantly in
writing, and both are almost invisible to a learner because the plain
version works fine and is only less pointed.

## Cleft sentences

A cleft takes an ordinary sentence and splits it so that one element
becomes the focus. The rest moves to the beginning or the end.

> **I** saw the accident. → **It was I** who saw the accident.
> **The accident** happened in 1994. → **In 1994** was when the accident
> happened.

Four patterns, and each is more emphatic than plain word order.

### *It is/was* + focus + *that/who*

The most common, and the way to emphasise almost anything.

> **It was** the manager **who** decided. *(not the team)*
> **It is** the cost **that** worries me. *(not the schedule)*

Note the agreement: *was* with a past sentence, *is* with a present one.
This is a real error in learner English.

> ✗ *It is the cost that worried me.*
> ✓ *It was the cost that worried me.*

### *What* + subject + *is/was*

Emphasises a whole clause, and is more common in speech than in writing.

> **What** I need **is** time.
> **What** happened **was** that nobody told anyone.
> **What** she said **was** that she would resign.

### Focus first, then the rest

The focus goes to the front without *it* or *what*, and this is the
sharpest of the four.

> **In 1994** the accident happened.
> **A long time ago** we met.
> **This** is what I meant.

### *What* + focus + *is*

Emphasises a noun phrase as the subject of a whole clause.

> **What** matters **is** the cost.
> **What** I want **is** a straight answer.
> **What** happened **is** nobody knows. *(a fragment, and it works)*

<Warning>
A cleft is not the same as emphasis by intonation, and using both at once
overdoes it.

> *It was **the manager** who decided.* — the structure already says
> *It was the manager* that decided. ✓
> ✗ *IT WAS the manager WHO decided!* — three emphases competing

The cleft is for written English and for neutral speech. The capital
letter and the shouted relative pronoun are for a different purpose, and
using them together makes the sentence sound like an argument rather than
an explanation.

One genuine use of both: in a formal document, where the structure is
there to survive being read aloud by someone who cannot see your face.
</Warning>

## Wh-cleft and reversal

A related move puts the question word at the front and leaves the rest
loose.

> **How** do you know that?
> **Why** did you say that?
> **What** happens next?

And the reversal, where the complement comes first.

> A strange thing happened to me. → **This** is what happened to me.
> The problem was the timing. → **Timing** was the problem.

## Ellipsis

Ellipsis leaves out words the reader can recover. It is not a shortcut; it
is a way of avoiding repetition.

**The auxiliary is dropped** when it is understood from the previous
clause.

> *She **will** write, and he **will** **write** too. → *and he will too.*
> *I **have** seen it, and she **has** **seen** it.* → *and she has too.*

**The verb is dropped** after an auxiliary.

> *He **has** never been there, but she **has** **been** there. → *but she has.*
> *I **might** go, though I **don't** really want to.* → *though I don't.*

**A whole clause is replaced** by a so or neither.

> *She sings, and so **do** I.
> *He is lazy, and so **is** she.
> *I did not go, and neither **did** she.
> *He could not swim, and nor **could** she.

**The object is dropped** after a transitive verb.

> *She wrote to him, and he **wrote** **to** her. → *and he wrote back.*
> *I gave her the book, and he gave **her** the letter.*

<Warning>
Ellipsis is only safe when the missing words are genuinely recoverable. A
learner who over-uses it produces sentences whose subject has vanished.

> *I read the first chapter and the second.* — the second what? Read, and
> the *second chapter* is the ellipsis. ✓ This is fine, because the noun
> was mentioned and a chapter can be read.
> ✗ *I read the first chapter and the second one about verbs.* — the
> ellipsis now hides which noun is the object, and the modifier attaches
> to the wrong thing.

The safe rule: the dropped element must be the same word that was used
before, not a new word that could refer to something else. That is the
distinction between ellipsis and ambiguity, and it is the line a learner
crosses.
</Warning>

## Substitution

Where ellipsis drops a word, substitution replaces it with a general word.

| dropped | replaced by | example |
|---|---|---|
| a noun | *one, ones, this, that* | *I have two cars. He has **one**.* |
| a verb | *do, does, did* | *I read it. She **does** too.* |
| a clause | *so, not* | *He is rich. She **is not**.* |
| a whole sentence | *this, that, it* | *It was the worst. **That** was the year.* |

Substitution with *do* is worth noticing, because *do* carries no meaning
at all and exists only to hold the place of a verb.

> *He plays the piano and she **does** too.*
> ✗ *He plays the piano and she plays too.* — the repetition is clumsy
> ✗ *He plays the piano and she does play too.* — acceptable, and the
> *does* adds a hint of contrast

The bare *does* is neutral. *Does* plus the verb suggests "unlike him",
which is why *I do smoke, actually* sounds mildly contradictory.

<DeepDive>
One use of ellipsis is worth learning early, because it is extremely common
in conversation and almost never taught: **negative ellipsis**, where a
negative auxiliary stands alone and the meaning of the whole clause is
carried by the context.

> *Are you coming?* — **No**, I'm not.
> *Does he live here?* — **No**, he doesn't.
> *Will you help?* — **I can't.** / **No chance.**

The reply is a single auxiliary with no main verb, and the reader supplies
the rest. This is the same mechanism as the substitution table above, and
it is the reason a learner who has only learned full sentences finds
ordinary conversation hard to follow: native speakers leave out the parts
the context makes obvious, and the learner is waiting to hear them.

The practical skill is not to produce ellipsis. It is to hear it. When a
listener says "I don't", they mean "I don't think so", or "I don't agree",
or "I don't want to", and which one comes from the question.
</DeepDive>

## Cleft compared with fronting

| device | what it emphasises | example |
|---|---|---|
| plain | nothing in particular | *The manager decided.* |
| fronting | the first element | ***The manager** decided.* |
| *it*-cleft | one element, named | **It was the manager** who decided. |
| *what*-cleft | a clause | **What happened** was a delay. |
| inversion | the first element, and it sounds formal | **Never had** I seen such a mess. |

Fronting is the weakest and is achieved by nothing more than putting a word
first and commaing correctly. The *it*-cleft is the strongest and is
explicit about what is being contrasted. In a long sentence, the difference
between fronting and a cleft is the difference between a hint and a
statement.
