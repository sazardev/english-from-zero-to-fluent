# Formality and register

Register is the set of choices that mark you as a particular kind of
speaker in a particular situation. Speaking to a friend and writing to a
company are different registers, and mixing them is what makes a message
sound either cold or careless.

## The one switch that does most of the work

Pronouns and the words that go with them.

| | formal | informal |
|---|---|---|
| first person | *one*, *we* | *I*, *you* |
| second person | *one*, *you* | *you* |
| contractions | none | normal |
| imperatives | *Please close the door.* | *Shut the door.* |
| requests | *I would be grateful if you would...* | *Could you...?* |
| criticism | *This may be a problem.* | *This is a problem.* |
| agreement | *We* | *you* |

> **Formal.** *I would be grateful if you could confirm receipt of this
> document at your earliest convenience.*
> **Informal.** *Let me know if you get this.*

The formal version is four times longer and says the same thing. In an email
to a supplier, the short version is rude and the long version is
professional. Neither is wrong; they are different registers and the
context picks one.

<Warning>
The most common register error in learner English is **too formal**, and it
has a specific cause: the language the learner studied is the formal
register of English, which is what is printed in books, and they have heard
almost no informal English except from songs and films.

The result is a learner who writes *I would like to inform you that the
meeting has been postponed* to a friend, and who is told they sound strange.

The fix is exposure, not rules. Listen to spoken English for an hour a day
for a month and the register will come by itself, because the rules are
being demonstrated faster than they can be explained.
</Warning>

## Formal to informal, in pairs

| formal | informal |
|---|---|
| *purchase* | *buy* |
| *reside* | *live* |
| *assistance* | *help* |
| *request* | *ask for* |
| *require* | *need* |
| *terminate* | *end* |
| *commence* | *start* |
| *sufficient* | *enough* |
| *approximately* | *about* |
| *endeavour to* | *try to* |
| *individuals* | *people* |
| *obtain* | *get* |
| *inform* | *tell* |
| *regarding* | *about* |
| *prior to* | *before* |
| *subsequently* | *later* |
| *utilise* | *use* |
| *inquire* | *ask* |
| *furnish* | *provide* |
| *endeavour* | *try* |
| *peruse* | *read* |
| *ascertain* | *find out* |

Two of these are almost never used and should be avoided in ordinary
English regardless of register: **utilise** and **peruse**. They were
invented to sound learned, and native speakers use them almost never.

> ✗ *Please **utilise** the attached form.* → ✓ *Please use the attached
> form.*
> ✗ *I **perused** your letter.* → ✓ *I read your letter.* — and *peruse*
> now means to read carefully or examine, and even that is rare

## Hedging, in outline

A hedge softens a claim. Formal and academic English hedge constantly, and
so does careful spoken English, and a learner who does not hedge sounds
either rude or overconfident.

| hedge | effect |
|---|---|
| *may* | possibility |
| *might* | possibility, more tentative |
| *could* | possibility, or a softened demand |
| *tend to* | a pattern, not a rule |
| *appear to* | an impression, not a fact |
| *seem to* | the same |
| *suggest* | the evidence is not conclusive |
| *indicate* | the same, more technical |
| *argue that* | an inference is being made |
| *it is likely that* | probability |
| *broadly speaking* | a generalisation with exceptions |
| *to some extent* | partial agreement |
| *in principle* | theoretically, and not in practice |
| *if any* | conceding there may be none |
| *at least* | a lower bound |

> *The data **suggests** a correlation.* — careful
> *The data **proves** a correlation.* — wrong, and a claim you cannot
> support
> ***It is likely that** prices will rise.* — hedged
> *Prices will rise.* — a prediction, stated as a fact

<DeepDive>
Hedging is the clearest single difference between learner and native
academic writing, and it is a difference of **honesty about evidence**
rather than politeness.

A native writer hedges a claim because a claim has to match its evidence. A
strong claim from weak evidence is not confidence, it is a mistake that has
not been noticed yet, and hedging is the linguistic form of noticing.

The pairs worth learning as a set:

> *The results **show** that X.* — the evidence is direct
> *The results **suggest** that X.* — the evidence is indirect
> *The results **indicate** that X.* — technical, the evidence is
> statistical
> *The results **may indicate** that X.* — and it may be nothing
> ***It could be that** X.* — the weakest form, and sometimes correct

*Suggest* is the workhorse and it is not a politeness form at all. When a
paper says *the results suggest a link*, the honest reading is that there
is a link the authors cannot yet rule out as coincidence, and reading it
as *prove* is a mistake a learner makes in one direction and a native
reader makes in the other.

The practical benefit: hedging is a tool for precision. If you are unsure
how strong your evidence is, there is a form for exactly that strength, and
using it makes you more accurate rather than less direct.
</DeepDive>

## Choosing a register

Ask three questions in order and stop at the first that answers.

**Who is this for, and do I know them?**

> known and informal → *Hey, are we still on for Thursday?*
> known and formal → *Dear Mr Fox, thank you for your message.*
> unknown and formal → *Dear Sir or Madam, I am writing to...*
> unknown and informal → *Hello, I hope this finds you well.* — borderline;
> "hope this finds you well" to a stranger is too familiar

**What is the cost of being wrong?**

> high → formal and hedged. *I am sorry, but I am unable to confirm the
> date at this time.*
> low → informal. *Can't do Thursday, sorry.*

**What does the situation usually look like?** Some contexts are simply
formal: legal, medical, academic, government, anything with a signature
attached.

## Contractions

The rule is simpler than it looks.

> **Contractions in**: speech, informal writing, email, instructions,
> anything addressed to one person, anything meant to be warm.
> **No contractions in**: formal letters, legal and medical writing,
> academic writing, anything where a contraction would look careless to a
> reader.

> ✓ *I'm writing to confirm...* — an email, fine
> ✗ *I am writing to confirm...* — stiff but not wrong
> ✓ *I am writing to confirm...* — a formal letter, correct
> ✗ *I'm writing to confirm...* — informal, and wrong in a letter to a
> company

<Warning>
Four words that are informal in almost every context, and which mark a
learner's English as casual whether or not they mean to be.

**Gonna, wanna, gotta.** Standard in speech; informal in writing; and
**wrong in anything submitted for assessment**.

> ✗ *I am **gonna** send the file.* → ✓ *I am going to send the file.*

**Kinda, sorta.** These hedge, and a hedge in a formal document is a
weakness.

> ✗ *The results were **sorta** significant.* → ✓ *The results were
> slightly significant.* — and the word *slightly* does the same job
> without the informality.

**Stuff, things, guys.**

> ✗ *Please find **the stuff** you requested.* → ✓ *Please find the
> documents you requested.*
> ✗ *You **guys** should come.* → ✓ *You should come.* / *All of you.*

**Kicking off, hanging out, grabbing a coffee.** All fine with friends and
all wrong in a professional email.
</Warning>

## Four things learners get wrong about formality

**Too formal by default.** *I would like to inform you that* to a friend.

**Using formal Latinate vocabulary for no reason.** *Utilise, peruse,
ascertain, pursuant to* — all rare, all worse than the plain word.

**Not hedging in academic writing.** *The results prove...* is a claim
about evidence, and overclaiming is the most criticised habit in research
writing.

**Contractions in a formal letter.** ✗ *I'm writing to...* to a company.
