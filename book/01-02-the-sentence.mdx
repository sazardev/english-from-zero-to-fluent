# The sentence

English sentences are built from a small number of parts in a small
number of orders. Learn the orders and the sentences stop being a
mystery.

## Finding the subject

The subject is the noun or pronoun the sentence is about. You find it by
replacing it with *he*, *she* or *it* and asking whether the rest of the
verb still makes sense.

> **The book on the table** is mine.

*The book on the table* → *it* is mine. Yes. So the subject is *the book
on the table*, not *table*.

> **What she said** surprised me.

*What she said* → *it* surprised me. Yes. So *what she said* is the
subject, and *she* is inside a clause inside the subject.

<DeepDive>
The test matters because the subject is not always next to the verb, and
the error of mistaking a noun for the subject is the origin of a large
family of mistakes.
<br><br>
<i>Liking him was easy.</i> The subject is <i>liking him</i>, not <i>him</i>.
The mistake this produces is the comma: <i>I don't know what does she
mean</i> should be <i>I don't know what she means</i>.
</DeepDive>

## The basic order

| position | what goes there |
|---|---|
| 1 | subject |
| 2 | verb |
| 3 | object, complement or adverbial |

> She **wrote** **a letter**.

Nothing comes between the subject and the verb except an adverb or an
auxiliary.

> She **quickly wrote** a letter. ✓
> She wrote quickly a letter. ✗

The exception that proves it: you can split subject and verb with a
short adverb or a phrase, but only a short one.

> She **often** writes letters. ✓
> She **with enormous patience** writes letters. ✗

## Questions

A question inverts the subject and the first auxiliary, and nothing
else.

| statement | question | what moved |
|---|---|---|
| She **is** working. | **Is** she working? | *is* |
| She **has** finished. | **Has** she finished? | *has* |
| She **can** swim. | **Can** she swim? | *can* |
| She wrote it. | **Did** she write it? | *did* appeared |
| She has a car. | **Does** she have a car? | *has* stayed, *does* appeared |

<Warning>
If there is no auxiliary in the statement, one appears in the question.
<i>She wrote it</i> becomes <i>Did she write it</i> — not <i>Wrote she it</i>,
and the main verb goes back to its base form.
</Warning>

## Negatives

One word, placed before the main verb.

> She **is not** working.
> She **has not** finished.
> She **did not** go.

Three ways to say no, all equivalent in the negative.

| | |
|---|---|
| full | She **does not** work. |
| contracted | She **doesn't** work. |
| with *never* | She **never** works. |

<DeepDive>
<i>Does not</i> and <i>never</i> are not quite the same.
<i>She doesn't drink</i> leaves the door open: maybe she drinks tea.
<i>She never drinks</i> closes it. Use <i>never</i> when you mean the
absolute, and <i>doesn't</i> when you are refusing a specific thing.
<br><br>
The formal register prefers <i>does not</i> to <i>doesn't</i> in writing,
and <i>doesn't</i> in speech. There is no rule about when to contract.
There is a register, and you follow the register you are in.
</DeepDive>

## The adverbial positions

Adverbials of time, place, manner and direction can move, and where they
land changes the meaning.

| position | example | what it means |
|---|---|---|
| before the subject | **Yesterday** she left. | normal |
| mid, after the verb | She left **in 1990**. | |
| mid, after the object | She left the house **at eight**. | |
| end | She left **at eight**. | neutral |
| beginning, fronted | **At eight**, she left. | emphatic, sets the scene |

<Warning>
*Only* certain adverbials move freely. <i>Yesterday</i>, <i>often</i> and
<i>usually</i> go at the start. <i>Yesterday I left the house at eight in
the evening</i> is awkward; it wants a single focus at the end:
<i>Yesterday I left the house at eight</i>.
</Warning>

## The compound sentence

Two independent clauses joined.

| conjunction | meaning | example |
|---|---|---|
| and | addition | I was tired, **and** I was hungry. |
| but | contrast | I was tired, **but** I kept working. |
| or | choice | Stay or go, your decision. |
| so | result | I was tired, **so** I stopped. |
| yet | unexpected contrast | It was cheap, **yet** it broke. |
| for | reason | I stopped, **for** I was exhausted. |

<DeepDive>
There is a rule Spanish does not have, and it catches almost everyone.
If the two clauses are closely connected and short, the comma goes
<b>before</b> the conjunction. If they are longer or the link is looser,
the comma goes <b>after</b> it. In practice the comma almost always goes
before, in British English, and omitting it is a mild error rather than a
wrong meaning.
<br><br>
The exception worth knowing: with <i>and</i> between two verbs, no comma
at all. <i>I read and liked it</i>, not <i>I read, and liked it</i>.
</DeepDive>

## The complex sentence

One independent clause, plus one or more dependent clauses. A dependent
clause cannot stand alone.

| type | word | example |
|---|---|---|
| adjective | who, which, that | *The man **who called** was angry.* |
| adverbial of time | when, before, after | *When it rains**, I stay in.* |
| adverbial of reason | because, since | *I stayed **because it rained**.* |
| adverbial of condition | if, unless | *If it rains**, I stay in.* |
| adverbial of contrast | although, though | *Although it rained**, we went.* |
| adverbial of purpose | so that, in order that | *I wrote **so that she could read** it.* |
| noun | that, whether, what | *I know **that he lied**.* |

<Warning>
<If/whether> is a clause, not a conjunction, and it needs a verb of its
own. <i>I don't know if he comes</i> is wrong; <i>I don't know if he
comes or not</i> or <i>I don't know whether he will come</i> is right. The
Spanish <i>no sé si viene</i> feels complete and is not.
</Warning>

## The order of dependent clauses

When a sentence has several, English has a default order and deviating
from it is possible but must be deliberate.

| position | clause type | why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | before any other | the frame |
| 2 | *although*, *while*, *because* | the background, given first |
| 3 | *which*, *who*, *that* (relative) | modifies a noun just mentioned |
| 4 | *when*, *if*, *so that* | the condition or the time |

> **Although** it was raining, **because** I had an appointment, **which**
> I had made earlier, I left **when** the bell rang.

Read that aloud. The sequence is: background, reason, which-noun, time.
It sounds right because the first clause frames the rest.

## The sentence you should write

Most of this chapter reduces to a procedure.

1. find the subject
2. put the verb after it, with an auxiliary if the sentence needs one
3. add the object or complement
4. add adverbials at the end, and move one to the front only if you want
   it emphasised
5. add dependent clauses after the independent one

Follow that order and your sentence will be English. Deviate from it only
when you are making a point.
