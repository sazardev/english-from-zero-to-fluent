# Reading faster without losing the text

Reading speed is not a skill you develop by reading faster. It is a
by-product of two things: how much vocabulary you have, and how much you
are processing per fixation. Both grow, and the second one grows faster
than most learners expect once they stop translating.

## The number, and what it means

| reader | words per minute | notes |
|---|---|---|
| learner, beginner | 60-100 | reading word by word, translating |
| learner, intermediate | 150-200 | still translating many words |
| learner, upper intermediate | 250-300 | grammar stops being the bottleneck |
| first language reader, easy text | 300-400 | |
| first language reader, dense text | 200-250 | |
| skilled reader, any English prose | 400-600 | literature included |

Measure yourself on text you do not know well, because that is the honest
case. A text you have read before flatters you.

<Warning>
**Do not aim for speed directly.** Every technique for reading faster is a
technique for understanding more per fixation, and a learner who sets a
speed target stops reading and starts scanning, and scanning is not
comprehension.

The number is a measurement of what already happened. A learner at 120
words per minute who understands 95 per cent of the text is doing better
than a learner at 200 who understands 70, and the second learner cannot
tell the difference, because the loss happened below the level of noticing.
</Warning>

## The four things that actually slow you down

In order of how much they cost.

**Translating.** Reading a sentence, converting it into another language in
your head, and then understanding the original. This is the largest cost
by far, and it is a habit, not a necessity. It disappears when a sentence
is understood as a whole before it is understood word by word.

**Looking up.** Not the act, but the interruption. Part 0 says five
lookups per page maximum, and the reason is that each one breaks the
sentence you were in.

**Sub-vocalising.** Saying the words in your head as you read. It is not
unusual and it is expensive; it roughly halves the rate. It is common in
learners who read aloud as children and it stops on its own once silent
reading becomes automatic.

**Not knowing the function words.** *the, of, to, and, that, in, for,
with, is, was*. These are half of a text, and a learner who processes them
individually spends most of the reading on the least informative half of
the page.

## The one technique that matters

**Read in phrase units, not words.**

A trained reader does not see *I read the book* as six separate items. They
see three units:

> *I read the* | *book*

or two, and they take in two or three words as one shape. Everything about
reading speed follows from this one fact, because a phrase is read in one
fixation and a word is read in one fixation.

How to practise it, and this is a real exercise rather than advice.

1. Take a text. Underline every noun.
2. Cover the words, leaving only the function words visible.
3. Try to read it. You cannot, because the nouns are what carry the
   meaning.
4. Do the opposite: cover the function words, read the nouns as a list.
   You can guess a good deal, because the structure is visible in the
   function words.

Step 4 is the point. It proves that the function words carry the
**structure** and the content words carry the **information**, and reading
efficiently means taking in the structure continuously and attending to the
information.

<DeepDive>
There is a reason the function words are worth learning separately from the
vocabulary, and it is about how much of the page they occupy.

In an ordinary English text, roughly half the words are function words:
*the, of, and, to, a, in, that, is, it, for, was, with, as, on, at, by,
from, or, an, be, this, which, has, was, were, not, but, they, we, you*.
They carry almost no information. You cannot delete them all, but you can
delete most of them and still recover a rough idea of the sentence.

A learner who knows 2,000 content words and processes function words one at
a time reads at maybe 90 words per minute and is working hard the whole
time. A reader with the same 2,000 content words and automatic function
words reads at 250 and is not working at all.

The gap between the two is not intelligence or effort. It is whether the
high-frequency words have become automatic, and automatic is a matter of
frequency, not of study. The list of the hundred most common English words
covers about half of ordinary written text, and those hundred are worth
more to a learner's reading speed than the next two thousand words.

This is also why the flashcard chapters in part 9 come early in a
vocabulary build. The top hundred are in the first two chapters of most
decks, and they are the ones that pay back immediately.
</DeepDive>

## Measuring honestly

Pick a passage you have not read. Time it. Then answer four questions.

1. How many words did you have to look up?
2. How many sentences did you have to read twice?
3. Could you summarise the passage in one sentence?
4. Could you not summarise it, and is that because it was hard or because
   you read too fast?

The fourth question is the only one that matters, and it is the one that
distinguishes a reader who is fast and accurate from a reader who is fast
and has lost the argument.

**Words per minute plus comprehension** is the real measure, and a useful
target is to hold 95 per cent comprehension while the rate rises. Speed
gained with comprehension falling from 95 to 80 is a loss, and it feels
like a gain for about a month.

## Increasing rate without losing accuracy

Four methods, in the order to try them.

**Graduated texts.** Read a graded reader at your current level, then one
above, then the next. Each step should feel comfortable and slightly too
easy, because a text that is too hard is a text you will not finish.

**Re-reading.** Read a passage once, then again, then again. The second
read is much faster and much more complete, and this is normal. The third
is faster still. This is the cheapest method and the most reliable, and
re-reading a difficult text four times teaches you more than four different
texts.

**Paragraph-first reading.** Read only the first sentence of each paragraph.
This gives you the structure of a whole page in fifteen seconds, and the
detail comes on the second pass. It is the technique of part 7.4 applied to
ordinary prose.

**Timing a fixed task.** Ten minutes, one text, every day, with a note of
the date and the rate. The trend over a month is worth more than any single
measurement, and it is the only way to know whether a method you are
practising is working.

## Reading what you cannot yet understand

A learner who stops at the first hard word reads very little, and reading
little is why the vocabulary stays small. The techniques for reading
under your level are these.

**Skip and come back.** Mark it, do not stop. A single unknown word in a
sentence of known words is usually recoverable from the sense of the
sentence, and looking it up is slower than guessing.

**Guess from the shape.** English word shapes are informative.
*Un-* is a negative, *-tion* is a noun, a word ending in *-ly* near a verb
is often an adverb, a long word is probably not essential. This works far
better than learners expect and it is how a reader who knows 3,000 words
reads a text with 4,000 in it.

**Read the last line of each paragraph first.** As in part 7.4. It gives
you the point of the paragraph before the difficulty arrives, and the
difficulty becomes detail rather than obstruction.

**Accept that the first read is slow and the second is not.** This is the
single most reassuring fact about reading a difficult text, and it is true
of native readers too. A text that is genuinely at your level takes half
the time on the second reading, and the half you save is the half you
spent reconstructing.

<Warning>
The technique to avoid is **reading every text at the level you cannot
comfortly read**, on the theory that difficulty makes you strong. It does
the opposite: the vocabulary you meet in a text that is too hard is
acquired shallowly and is often never acquired, and the texts you do not
finish are the ones you do not remember.

The correct policy is the opposite of the intuitive one: **most of your
reading should be easy.** Read a great deal below your level, a little at
it, and almost nothing above it. The great deal below builds speed and
automatic vocabulary, the little at it teaches you something, and the
almost-nothing-above-it is there so that you have something to look forward
to.
</Warning>

## Four things learners get wrong about reading speed

**Aiming for speed rather than understanding.** Speed is a measurement,
not a target.

**Translating every sentence.** The largest single cost, and a habit that
stops on its own once you read a lot.

**Stopping at every unknown word.** Five per page, and guess the rest.

**Reading nothing above your level.** The hard texts teach you the least
and get abandoned the fastest.
