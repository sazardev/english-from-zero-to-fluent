# How science is written

Scientific English is the register a learner is most likely to need and
least likely to meet. It is a small, highly conventional written style,
and because it is conventional it is learnable. This chapter describes the
conventions, with the reasoning, because conventions without reasons are
the ones you break under pressure.

## The three periods

Scientific writing has a three-part structure that is remarkably stable
across every field.

| section | job | tense | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| background | what is already known | present perfect, present | *Previous work has shown...* |
| gap | what is not known | present | *However, it remains unclear...* |
| method | what was done | past | *We measured...* |
| results | what was found | past | *The samples showed...* |
| discussion | what it means | present, for facts | *This suggests...* |

<Warning>
The tense switch is the most consistent feature and the most commonly
mangled. The rule is that **a method was done** and **a fact is true**.

> *We **measured** the samples.* — past: a completed action by the authors
> *The samples **were** contaminated.* — past passive, for what happened to
> them
> *The method **requires** 20 ml.* — present: a property of the method
> *The results **show** a clear trend.* — present: the result is a fact now
> *The results **showed** a clear trend.* — past: an account of what
> appeared in one experiment

*It was found that the samples were contaminated* is the passive form, and
it is the single most common construction in the older scientific
literature. It is now disfavoured in most journals, not because it is
ungrammatical but because it hides the agent, and a paper that says *it
was found* has not said who found it.
</Warning>

## Hedge, always

Every claim is calibrated. This is the single most distinctive feature of
the register and the one that most changes a learner's academic writing.

| strength | form | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| certain | *We demonstrate that...* | the evidence is direct |
| strong | *The data show that...* | direct evidence |
| moderate | *These results suggest that...* | indirect, other explanations possible |
| cautious | *The results **may** indicate that...* | possible, not established |
| weakest | ***It is possible that**...* | speculation |
| attributive | ***It has been suggested that**...* | someone else claims it, and the writer is not responsible |

> *These results **suggest** that the drug reduces the symptoms.*
> — the effect is there, but other causes are not excluded
> *These results **prove** that the drug reduces the symptoms.*
> — almost never defensible, and always criticised

Three phrases to know in order of how much they commit you to:

> ***It is well known that** X.* — a fact, cited as such
> ***It has been shown that** X.* — someone demonstrated it, and it is
> accepted
> ***It has been suggested that** X.* — someone proposed it, and it is not
> established

The third is the most useful for a learner, because it lets you report an
idea without endorsing it, which is exactly what a cautious sentence
requires.

## Nominalisation everywhere

Science is written in nouns, and this is the main obstacle for a learner
coming from ordinary English.

> *We **performed an analysis of** the samples **using** a method **based
> on** chromatography.*
> plain: *We analysed the samples by chromatography.*

| plain | scientific |
|---|---|
| *we analysed* | *an analysis was performed* / *we carried out an analysis* |
| *because* | *due to the fact that*, *owing to* |
| *so* | *therefore*, *consequently*, *thus* |
| *about* | *with regard to*, *concerning*, *in terms of* |
| *using* | *by means of*, *with the aid of* |
| *to find out* | *to determine*, *to ascertain* |
| *a lot of* | *a considerable amount of* |
| *use up* | *exhaust*, *deplete* |
| *help* | *facilitate*, *support* |
| *show* | *demonstrate*, *indicate*, *reveal* |

<Warning>
The two rows in that table are in opposition, and the reason is worth
knowing, because a learner who knows only the second one writes badly.

Nominalisation is not decoration. It is what allows a sentence to name a
process without committing to who did it and when.

> *The **addition** of the sample **was followed by** precipitation.*
> *We added the sample and precipitation followed.*

The first is better science writing because it can be given as a general
fact: whenever this happens, this follows. The second is an account of one
event. When the paper is stating a mechanism, the nominal form is the
correct one.

The mistake is using nominalisation when an actor and a time exist and are
known. *The implementation of the new policy was met with resistance* is
worse than *Staff resisted the new policy*, and no scientific convention
requires the first.
</Warning>

## Sentences that differ from ordinary English

Four constructions that are correct in science and wrong or odd elsewhere.

**The dummy subject *it* for the experiment.**

> *It was found that the samples were contaminated.*
> *It is important to note that...*
> *It can be shown that...*
> *It follows that...*

**The empty subject *there* for existence.**

> ***There** is evidence to suggest...*
> ***There** were three conditions in which the effect disappeared.*

**The present passive for a general fact.**

> *The samples **are** stored at 4 °C.*
> *Each measurement **is** repeated three times.*

**The agentless passive.**

> *The theory **was proposed** in 1974.*
> — and the point is that the paper does not care who proposed it

None of these is available in ordinary English in quite this way. *There
is evidence to suggest* is a scientific formula and it would be odd in a
letter.

## Numbers, units and abbreviations

| convention | example | note |
|---|---|---|
| number then unit, no space | *20 ml*, *4 °C* | SI, and the space is a multiplication sign |
| space between number and per cent | *20 per cent* | *20%* is fine in a table |
| ranges | *4 to 6 °C*, *4–6 °C* | not *4-6*, which reads as minus |
| decade years | *the 1990s*, *the nineties* | no apostrophe |
| numbers up to ten | *three samples*, not *3 samples* | in running text |
| numbers over ten | *25 samples* | |
| temperature | *20 degrees* or *20 °C* | not *20 degrees Celsius* in a table |
| Latin abbreviations | *e.g., i.e., cf., et al., vs.* | with a full stop |
| first mention of an abbreviation | *scanning electron microscopy (**SEM**)* | then *SEM* alone |

*Et al.* is Latin and is italicised: *et al.* Never *et. al.* and never
*et all.*

<Warning>
Three conventions that look like errors and are not, because they are
standard in the register.

**No plural on units.** *3 metres*, not *3 metreses*. Units are not
counted. And *5 metres* is correct even for one metre, because the unit
names the measure, not the thing.

**Sentences beginning with *However* take a comma.** *However*, the effect
disappeared. In ordinary English *however* often does not, and this is a
visible marker of scientific register.

**Numbers over one hundred take *and* in some styles.** British style
writes *two hundred and five*; American style writes *two hundred five*.
Both are correct and both appear in the literature. Pick one and stay with
it, because mixing them within a document is the only real error.
</Warning>

## A paragraph of scientific English

Here is a real abstract, and every feature above is in it.

> *Previous work has shown that the compound is stable in acidic
> conditions, but the reported measurements rely on indirect methods.
> This study measures the concentration directly, by titration, across the
> pH range 2 to 9. We find that the concentration rises sharply between
> pH 4 and pH 5 and then plateaus. These results suggest that the
> dissociation constant is lower than previously believed, and they may
> explain the discrepancies in the earlier literature. Further work is
> required to establish whether the effect persists at higher
> temperatures.*

Every sentence does one job, in order: what is known, what is not, what we
did, what we found, what it means, what is next. The tense moves past to
present and back exactly where the meaning moves. Every claim is hedged
except the one about what was done.

<DeepDive>
The paragraph above is worth reading twice for a reason that is not
obvious: **it is nearly impossible to write one of these badly, because the
structure is so constrained.**

Six slots, each with a job, each with a conventional tense and a
conventional set of hedges. A learner who memorises the six slots can
produce a competent scientific paragraph on any subject, and the slots are
the same in physics, biology, economics and computer science.

This is the most transferable thing in the book for anyone who needs to
write academically in English. The vocabulary of a field can be looked up.
The structure of a paragraph cannot be inferred from the subject, and it is
identical across every subject that uses this register.

The one thing to be careful about is the temptation to pad. Scientific
English rewards compression, and the pressure to sound scientific produces
sentences like *the results were found to be in agreement with the
hypothesis that had been previously put forward by the authors*, which is
thirteen words long and says *the results agreed with our prediction*.
Compression is a feature of the register, not a formality, and the plain
version is normally better English.
</DeepDive>

## Four things learners get wrong about scientific English

**Present tense for a method.** *We measure the samples* → ✓ *We **measured**
the samples*. Only the facts stay in the present.

**Overclaiming.** *Prove*, *demonstrate conclusively*, *establish beyond
doubt*. Science hedges; the hedge is not weakness, it is accuracy.

**Latinate padding.** *Owing to the fact that* → ✓ *because*.

**A unit with a plural.** *3 metreses* → ✓ *3 metres*.
