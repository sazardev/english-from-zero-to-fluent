2. ✓ correct

4. ✓ correct — a *historic* fiction would be an important one
5. ✓ correct — *guess* means work it out, and here he did

7. ✓ correct
8. ✓ correct

## 7. Formal and Latinate — answered

| | more precise, or just longer? | plain |
|---|---|---|
| 1. prior to | just longer | before |
| 2. sufficient | **more precise** | enough — *sufficient* means "as much as is required" |
| 3. regarding | just longer | about |
| 4. ascertain | **more precise** | find out |
| 5. utilise | just longer, and rare | use |
| 6. pursuant to | just longer | under, according to |
| 7. approximately | **more precise** | about — it is the standard scientific form |
| 8. terminate | **more precise**, in law and contracts | end |
| 9. in order to | just longer | to |
| 10. owing to the fact that | just longer | because |

Five of each. The rule from part 6.4: if the formal word is more precise,
keep it in formal writing; if it is only longer, do not use it at all.

## 8. Paragraphs — answered

1. **Two.** The second sentence changes the subject from the sample to the
   figures, and the third starts a new topic. The link is there but the
   topic has moved.
2. **One.** All four sentences are about the quality of her writing, and
   the third is the counter-argument inside the same idea.
3. **One.** This is a list, and it would be better as a list. As prose it
   works because the three factors are one idea, but bullets are cleaner.
4. **Two.** Sentences 1 to 3 are a second thought and need a topic
   sentence. *I had assumed otherwise for years* is a topic sentence
   already, and it comes last.

## 9. Cohesion — answered

1. ✓ correct — *they* can only be the samples
2. ✗ **The model** had assumed otherwise.
3. ✗ This is ambiguous. → *The instability of the pH caused it* — or name
   what *this* is.
4. ✗ **The authors of the earlier paper** say the findings are
   unreliable.
5. ✓ correct
6. ✗ **The analysis** gave an unexpected answer.
7. ✗ The temperature was too high, and this **caused** the failure — or
   *...and the failure was caused by this*
8. ✗ **which** had no possible referent. → *..., which I had already
   suspected.* — here *which* refers to the whole clause, which is the
   one use of *which* with no obvious noun

Items 5 and 8 are the interesting pair: the same sentence is correct in
one form and broken in the other, and the difference is whether the
repeated noun is available as the referent.

## 10. Signposting — answered

1. *Furthermore*, → **delete**. *The results cannot be generalised.*
2. *It is worth noting that* → **delete**. *The method is sound.*
3. ✓ **keep** — *however* is carrying the contrast, and there is nothing
   before it in this exercise but in a real text it would be doing real
   work
4. → *Three factors matter.*
5. ✓ **keep and rewrite**: *That said, three factors matter, and the third
   is the supply chain.* — here the connective names a genuine contrast
   and the sentence adds information

## 11. Hedging — answered

1. **definitely better** → *The measurements are consistent with a
   genuine improvement.*
2. **some evidence** → *The results suggest a rise.*
3. **somebody said it** → *It has been claimed that the paper is wrong.*
4. **no uncertainty given** → *The data, from a single run, suggest a
   tentative improvement.*
5. **no comparison group** → *Of the 40 patients treated, 40 improved,
   which is not by itself evidence of an effect.*
6. **a real comparison** → *The data show a substantially higher
   improvement rate than the control group.*

The order of the exercise is a ladder from a fact to a supported claim, and
items 4, 5 and 6 are the three that a paper has to be able to do.

## 12. Hedges that do not work — answered

1. *We **suggest** that the data show a trend.*
2. *It **may** be true.* — or *It might be true*, but not both
3. *The sample was contaminated.*
4. *The sample was **slightly** contaminated.* — or *was somewhat*, but
   not both
5. *We **expect** a rise.*

Item 3 is the one to notice: *it is important to note that* before a fact
is padding, because the fact needs no announcement.

## 13. Registers in practice — answered

**The fact.** *It's done. Second sample was bad so I left it out. Raw data
available.*

**Professional email.** *The analysis is now complete. I have excluded
the second sample because of contamination, and I have attached the raw
data in case you would like to work with it directly.*

**Formal letter.** *I am writing to confirm that the analysis has now been
completed. The second sample has been excluded on the grounds of
contamination, and the underlying data are enclosed for your reference.*

**To a friend.** *All done! Second sample was dodgy so I've left it out.
Raw data there if you want to poke at it.*

The differences that are doing work: the professional version says
*because of contamination* and the formal says *on the grounds of
contamination*, and the friend gets *dodgy*. The professional version
offers the data as a convenience; the formal one encloses it; the friend
just puts it there.

## 14. The fourteen errors — answered

| | error | correction |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1. missing *-s* | She **writes** every day. |
| 2 | 2. perfect with a finished time | I **saw** him yesterday. |
| 3 | 3. past perfect with no reference point | The company **was** founded in 1994. |
| 4 | 4. *to* after an *-ing* verb | I enjoy **reading**. |
| 5 | 5. preposition added to a transitive verb | We discussed **the** plan. |
| 6 | 6. preposition from the noun | It depends **on** the answer. |
| 7 | 7 and 8. apostrophe | The dog wagged **its** tail. |
| 8 | 9. doubled comparative | She is **much** taller. |
| 9 | 11. dangling participle | **Walking** to school, **I** dropped my bag in the river. |
| 10 | 12. nominalisation | **Staff resisted** the policy. |
| 11 | 13. *despite* plus a phrase | **In spite of** the rain, we went out. |
| 12 | 14. two coordinators | **Although** it was long, I enjoyed it. |
| 13 | 1 and existential *there* | There **are** many reasons. |
| 14 | participle where a past is needed | The report **was written** yesterday. |

Item 7 is on the list twice, which is deliberate: the two apostrophe
errors are one idea.

## 15. Proofreading order — answered

| order | step | errors it catches |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | the main argument | — none; it is not an error hunt |
| 2 | the paragraph structure | 11, 12 |
| 3 | the tenses | 2, 3, 14 |
| 4 | the articles | 7, 8 |
| 5 | the third person *-s* | 1, 13 |
| 6 | the prepositions | 4, 5, 6 |

The tenses come before the articles because a sentence with the wrong
tense will also have the wrong article, and fixing the article first means
doing the work twice.

## 16. Write a paragraph — answered

There is no model answer, because the exercise is the writing. What to
check afterwards.

1. Can you name your paragraph in three words? If not, it is two
   paragraphs.
2. Is your topic sentence the first sentence? If it is the last, it is a
   summary, and that is a choice rather than a default.
3. Does every other sentence still be about the same thing?
4. How many sentences have a pronoun with more than one possible
   referent? Aim for zero.
5. Are three consecutive sentences the same length? If so, merge two.
