# The passive

The passive changes who the sentence is about, not who did the action. A
learner who avoids it writes *Someone stole my bike*, and a learner who
misuses it writes *My bike was stolen by someone*, which says the same
thing at twice the length.

## The three reasons to use it

**The doer is unknown or irrelevant.**

> The window *was broken*. *(by whom, do not care)*
> The bridge *is being built*.

**The doer is obvious.**

> He *was arrested*. By the police. Everyone knows who.
> The report *has been written*. By me.

**The doer comes after the verb, so it can be dropped.**

> The results *must be announced* by Friday. *(by me, understood)*
> She *was given* the prize. *(by the judges, understood)*

In the third case the sentence is *shorter*, and that is usually the real
reason.

<Warning>
The passive is not a more polite or more formal version of the active. It
is a way of putting the receiver in the subject slot, and using it when
the doer is the point is a real mistake of emphasis.

> The police *arrested* him. — the police did it, and that is the news
> ✗ He *was arrested* by the police. — the police is a trailing detail

In academic and technical writing the passive is common and usually
correct, because the argument, not the agent, is the point. In news and
speech the active is usually stronger. Know which register you are in
before you reach for the passive.
</Warning>

## How it is formed

**Auxiliary + past participle.** The auxiliary carries the tense.

| tense | active | passive |
|---|---|---|
| present simple | She *writes* it | It *is written* |
| present continuous | She *is writing* it | It *is being written* |
| past simple | She *wrote* it | It *was written* |
| past continuous | She *was writing* it | It *was being written* |
| present perfect | She *has written* it | It *has been written* |
| future | She *will write* it | It *will be written* |
| modal | She *can write* it | It *can be written* |

The rule that makes the whole table work: **the auxiliary is exactly the
one the active verb would have had**, and the main verb becomes the past
participle.

> *is* + *written* ← *writes*
> *was* + *written* ← *wrote*
> *has been* + *written* ← *has written*
> *can be* + *written* ← *can write*

## The one-letter trap

*Be* in the passive takes *-s* exactly as any other third person verb
does. This is the most frequent error in the passive.

> ✓ It *is written* in the contract.
> ✗ It *are written* in the contract.
> ✓ They *are* being inspected now.
> ✗ They *is* being inspected now.
> ✓ Nothing *was* lost.
> ✗ Nothing *were* lost.

And in the negative and the question the *-s* stays on the auxiliary and
goes nowhere else.

> ✓ It *is not* written down.
> ✓ *Is* it written down?
> ✗ It *is not* written **-s** down.

## Passive with modals

The modal comes first, then *be*, then the participle. The pattern is
identical for every modal, and it is worth learning once.

> It *can be* done. / It *must be* done. / It *should be* done.
> It *might be* done. / It *would be* done. / It *may be* done.

Questions and negatives:

> *Can* it *be done*?
> It *cannot be* done. / It *can't be* done.

## Passive with two verbs

When one verb is passive and the next is active, only the first takes the
*be* + participle.

> The letter *was written* **and** it *was sent*.
> The book *has been translated** **into** twelve languages.
> The problem *must be solved* **before** Friday.

The asymmetry is the part to remember: *was written and sent*, never *was
written and was sent* — unless both really are passive, in which case
repeat the *be*.

> The room *was cleaned and was painted*. *(both actions complete)*

## Stative verbs do not go passive

Some verbs have no passive because they describe a state, not an action.

> ✓ He *lacks* experience.
> ✗ Experience *is lacked* by him.
> ✓ The problem *concerns* everyone.
> ✗ Everyone *is concerned* by the problem. → ✓ Everyone *is concerned*
> about the problem. *(concerned is an adjective here)*

The list: *belong, consist, contain, include, lack, matter, own, resemble,
seem, suit, fit, exist, deserve, happen.*

If the passive sounds absurd, the verb is one of these.

## Passive with *by*

The *by* phrase names the doer, and it goes at the end.

> The book *was written by* a retired teacher.
> The window *was broken by* the ball.
> The decision *has been made by* the committee.

**Delete it when it is known, obvious or unimportant.** Most of the time
it should go.

> The results *must be submitted by Friday*. ✓
> ✗ The results must be submitted by Friday by the department. *(by Friday
> is not a doer; the sentence is broken)*

> The meeting *was cancelled*. ✓ *(everyone knows why)*
> ✗ The meeting was cancelled by the manager in charge of the fourth-floor
> booking, who had left for the airport.

<DeepDive>
A *by* phrase is not a prepositional phrase you can put anywhere. It
attaches to the passive verb, and the verb is finite, so the phrase
normally follows the whole clause rather than sitting mid-sentence.

> The report *was checked by* a reviewer **and approved by** the board.
> ✓ Both phrases sit after the verb, in step.

> ✗ By a reviewer the report was checked.

If you find yourself wanting to start with a *by* phrase, you usually want
a different structure. *According to a reviewer, the report was checked* is
the way to do it, and it is also a way to signal that the source is
doubtful.
</DeepDive>

## Passive with two objects

When a verb takes two objects, either can become the subject, and the
choice is about what the sentence is about.

> She *gave* the prize **to** the winner. / She *gave* the winner the prize.
> The prize *was given* to the winner. / The winner *was given* the prize.

> They *sent* him the letter. / They *sent* the letter to him.
> He *was sent* the letter. / The letter *was sent* to him.

**Some verbs only permit one order to go passive.** With these, one noun
becomes the subject and the other follows a preposition, and the choice is
forced by the verb, not by emphasis.

> She *told* me the news. → *I was told the news.* ✓
> ✗ *The news was told me.*

> She *gave* the prize to the winner. → *The winner was given the prize.* ✓
> → *The prize was given to the winner.* ✓

*Tell* does not passivise with the thing told as the subject. *Give* does.
The difference is conventional and has to be learned verb by verb, and the
verbs where it matters most are *tell, show, ask, want, buy, sell, teach,
lend, send, offer, provide, deny, refuse*.

## *Get* passive

Informal English has a second passive, with *get* instead of *be*, and it
adds a sense of accident or difficulty that *be* does not.

> The window *got broken*. *(it was broken, and that was annoying)*
> The car *got stolen*. *(you did not expect it)*

Use it when something happened to you that you did not intend. It is
common in speech and informal writing, and it is not for exams.

> He *got sacked*. → He was sacked. *(neutral)*

## Reporting the news

Newspapers reach for the passive constantly, because the actor is often
the subject of legal action and naming it first would be a claim.

> Allegations *were made* that funds had been misused. *(no one named)*
> The minister *was questioned* about the delay.
> Steps *have been taken* to improve safety.
> It *is thought* that the company will close. *(rumour, and the writer
> will not commit)*

Learn to read these. *It is believed, it is reported, it is alleged, it is
thought* are all ways of saying "somebody says this and I am not
responsible for it". They are not passive forms of a hidden fact; they
are attribution, and the difference matters when you are evaluating a
claim.
