# Cohesion

Cohesion is the set of devices that hold a text together: reference,
substitution, ellipsis, conjunction and lexical chains. Part 5 covered the
structures. This chapter is about which device to reach for, and the answer
is that a text works when the choice between them is invisible.

## The five devices

| device | what it does | example |
|---|---|---|
| reference | a pronoun or phrase points back | *The method failed. **It** was not robust.* |
| substitution | a general word replaces a phrase | *...a better result. **This** is unexpected.* |
| ellipsis | a word is left out | *She wrote to him, and he wrote **back**.* |
| conjunction | a relationship is named | *The sample **was** contaminated **and** the pH was wrong.* |
| lexical chain | the same root repeats | *result, results, results, result* |

A paragraph uses all five without noticing. A reader notices when one is
missing or wrong.

<Warning>
The cohesion error that makes writing look translated is **the vague
pronoun**: an *it*, a *this* or a *they* with nothing clearly to point
back to. It is the most frequent problem in learner writing and it is
almost always about a **long distance**.

> *We measured the samples at four temperatures. **They** were incubated
> for 48 hours.* — *They* = the samples or the temperatures
> → *The samples were incubated for 48 hours.*

> ***This** is because the pH was unstable.* — what does *this* refer to?
> The measurement? The instability? The reason?

> ***They** say the results are unreliable.* — who? The reviewers? The
> authors of the other paper? The people next door?

The fix is not to avoid pronouns. It is to make the referent the most
salient thing in the sentence, so *they* has one candidate.

> *The samples were incubated for 48 hours, **and they** were then
> measured.*

That is longer, and it is correct. The version without *and* was correct
too, and the reader supplied the link, and a reader supplying a link is a
reader doing your work.
</Warning>

## Keeping the referent salient

Four ways to make a pronoun safe.

**Put the referent in the subject, as late as possible and as early as
necessary.**

> ✗ *After the samples were incubated, **it** was measured.*
> ✓ *After the samples were incubated, **each sample** was measured.*

**Repeat the noun instead of using a pronoun, when the distance is more
than one sentence.**

> ✗ *The results contradicted the model. **It** had assumed...* — *it* =
> the model or the results
> ✓ *The results contradicted the model. **The model** had assumed...*

**Use a demonstrative for a whole clause.**

> *The pH was outside the specified range. **This** means the result
> cannot be trusted.*

**Name the category rather than using *they*.**

> ✗ *They rejected the paper.* — who?
> ✓ *The reviewers rejected the paper.*

## Lexical chains

A text is held together partly by the recurrence of its key words. This is
invisible when it works and glaring when it does not.

> *The result was unexpected. The result contradicted the model. **Result**
> after **result**, each time a different idea, and the reader follows
> without effort.

The opposite is worse than repetition, and it is the more common problem.

> *The findings showed... The data demonstrated... The analysis revealed...
> The results indicated...*
> — four different words for one thing, in four sentences, and the reader
> has to learn a new noun each time to follow one argument

Both failures are real. Repeating *result* forty times is dull; varying
among *finding, data, analysis, result* for one thing is confusing. The
distinction is that a lexical chain is about **one thing**, and variety is
about **different things**.

> *The **results** showed a trend. The **analysis** of the second sample
> was more reliable.* — the *results* and the *analysis* are different
> things, and the shift is informative

## Signposting

Signposting tells the reader the structure explicitly. It is used more in
speaking and academic writing than in prose, and the rule is that a
signpost should carry information.

| informative | not informative |
|---|---|
| *Three factors account for the delay.* | *There are three points to discuss.* |
| *The first problem is the sample.* | *Firstly, it is worth noting that...* |
| *As a result, the data are unreliable.* | *Furthermore, there is another issue.* |
| *This is in contrast to the small samples.* | *On the other hand, however...* |

<DeepDive>
The reason signposting sounds hollow in learner writing is usually that it
has been learned as a list of connectives rather than as a claim about
structure.

*Furthermore*, *moreover*, *in addition*, *what is more* and *added to
this* are all ways of saying **and**. If a paragraph needs one, the reader
has learned nothing about the argument and has spent four words.

The versions that carry information name the connection:

> *Additionally*, the sample was contaminated. — still just *and*
> *This is a further problem, and it is more serious*, because the
> contamination affects the concentration measurement. — now the reader
> knows why the paragraph follows

The test is whether the connective could be deleted without the paragraph
becoming wrong. If it could, it was a filler. *Furthermore* can almost
always be deleted, which is why it is almost always filler.

There is one place where signposting is not filler: in a long piece where
the reader needs to know where they are. A two thousand word document with
*Part one*, *Part two* at the top of each is easier to navigate than one
that flows continuously, and a learner writing an essay is usually
under-signposting rather than over-signposting. The error is using *moreover*
four times in a paragraph, not using it at all.
</DeepDive>

## Varying sentence length

Related to cohesion and worth stating separately, because uniformity is
the most audible flaw in learner prose.

| pattern | effect |
|---|---|
| every sentence the same length | monotonous, and noticeable |
| one very long then several very short | choppy, and it reads as hesitation |
| long, short, medium, short | a rhythm, and it reads as confident |

The mechanism is that a short sentence after a long one lands harder. This
is why a long, complicated sentence followed by *That is all.* is a
technique and not an error.

> *Having established that the contamination could not be explained by any
> of the three candidate mechanisms, and having ruled out the possibility
> that the discrepancy arose from an error in the calibration of the
> instrument, we conclude that the effect is real.*
> *It is real.*

The second sentence is one clause and it lands, because the first one is
overwhelmed the reader by the time it arrives.

<Warning>
Uniform sentence length is the flaw that makes written English sound
machine-made, and it comes from a specific cause: writing one clause at a
time and putting a full stop wherever a clause ends.

> ✗ *The samples were collected. The samples were incubated. The samples
> were measured.*
> ✓ *The samples were collected, incubated and measured.*

Three subjects, three verbs, one subject, three verbs. The second is one
clause longer and carries three actions, which is what a reader expects a
sentence to do.

The test: find three consecutive sentences in your own writing that begin
with the same word or the same word class. Half of them can be merged or
varied, and the paragraph is better for it.
</Warning>

## Four things learners get wrong about cohesion

**A vague pronoun across a long distance.** *This*, *it* and *they* with
two possible referents.

**Varying the key noun for no reason.** Four words for one thing in four
sentences.

**Signposting that carries no information.** *Furthermore* → delete it.

**Uniform sentence length.** One clause per sentence, full stop, next
clause.
