# The perfect

The perfect is not one tense. It is three, built on three different
auxiliaries, and all of them do the same job: connect a past event to the
present.

The job is always the same. The **present** part says the event is over.
The **past** part says which event. The **connection** says whether it
still matters now.

## The three perfects

| form | name | example |
|---|---|---|
| *have/has* + past | present perfect | I *have read* it. |
| *had* + past | past perfect | I *had read* it. |
| *will/have* + past | future perfect | I *will have read* it by June. |

All three need a past participle, and the only irregularity worth
memorising is where the participle is not the *-ed* form.

> *read* → *read* (same spelling, different sound)
> *write* → *written*
> *go* → *gone*
> *see* → *seen*
> *do* → *done*
> *speak* → *spoken*

Part 10 lists all 415, with meanings, because the reference half of this
book exists so that the teaching half never has to guess.

## Present perfect again, in its own right

Already covered in the present chapter, because it belongs there. The
single rule that governs it: **no finished time**.

> ✓ I have *finished* the report.
> ✗ I have *finished* the report *last week*.
> ✓ I *finished* the report last week.

Everything else follows from that one constraint.

### Adverbs of time that are allowed

A vague time expression is allowed, because it does not close the period.

> I have *seen* him *recently*.
> I have *known* her *for years*.
> I have *already* eaten.
> I have *never* been to Japan.
> I *haven't* finished *yet*.

Everything in that list is compatible with the present continuing now.

### State verbs and the perfect

A state has no result, so it takes the perfect with a duration.

> I have *lived* here for six years.
> She has *had* that car since 2015.
> We have *known* each other forever.

And the moment the state ends, the perfect is replaced by the past simple.

> I *lived* here for six years. → I moved. ✓
> ✗ I have lived here for six years. → and then I moved. ✓

<Warning>
The error that never goes away is the present perfect with a specific past
time. It is so common in learner English that native speakers recognise it
before they read the end of the sentence.

> ✗ I have *gone* to Paris in 2018.
> ✓ I *went* to Paris in 2018.

> ✗ I have *finished* it yesterday.
> ✓ I *finished* it yesterday.

> ✗ I have *lost* my keys *last night*.
> ✓ I *lost* my keys last night.

Three more that look right and are not:

> ✗ I have *seen* him *when I was in Rome*.
> ✓ I *saw* him when I was in Rome.

> ✗ I have *bought* this book *two years ago*.
> ✓ I *bought* this book two years ago.

> ✗ I have *worked* here *since* 2010. → and I still work here, so this one
> is correct, but only because *since* without an end point leaves the
> situation open.
</Warning>

## Past perfect again, and its second job

**Earlier than another past event.** Covered in the past chapter: it
appears only when the order is genuinely ambiguous.

> When I arrived, the film had started.
> She realised she had left her keys at home.

**A state that held before a change.**

> Before the war, the country had been a monarchy for two hundred years.
> When I got there, she had already left.

**A first meeting in a narrative that backtracks.**

> He recognised her. They had not met for twenty years.
> We found the letter. It had been written in 1911.

**An unreal past.** This is the one learners do not expect, and it is
where the past perfect is genuinely useful.

> If I had known, I would have come.
> She would have helped, if she had been able.
> I would have told you, had I known.

<DeepDive>
The past perfect also expresses *unreality* rather than *earliness* in the
second conditional, and this is the exception that catches people.

> If I *had* been taller, I *would have been* a model. — neither is true
> If I *was* taller when I was a boy, I *would* be a model now. — mixed, and
> the past simple is right because one half is a real past fact

So the past perfect after *if* means *not true*. It is not a way of
saying "before". A learner who has learned the past perfect as "the
earlier past" will get this wrong for months, and the fix is to learn the
second conditional as a single pattern rather than as two tenses.

| second conditional | structure |
|---|---|
| if clause | *if* + past simple / *were* |
| result | *would* + base |
| past perfect variant | *if* + past perfect, *would have* + past participle |

The *were* form matters: *If I were you* is the only idiomatic option.
*If I was you* is not wrong in speech but it is wrong in writing.
</DeepDive>

## Future perfect

**Finished by a point in the future.** The reference point is in the
future, and the perfect looks back from it.

> By June I *will have finished* the book.
> In ten years she *will have been* teaching for thirty.
> By the time you arrive, I *will have left*.

**A guess about the present from a future viewpoint.** Less common, and
easier to use *probably* with.

> I think you *will have heard* about it by now.
> He *will have finished* by now — he left at eight and it is nine.

**With *by*, *before*, *after*, *once*, *when*.** These are the natural
partners, because they mark the point the perfect looks back from.

> *By* Friday I *will have moved*.
> *Once* I *have found* a job, I *will* buy a car.
> *Before* the deadline, I *will have submitted* everything.

<Warning>
*Will have* is not a prediction about your own behaviour. It is an
assertion that something will be complete at a future point, and the
assertion has to be supportable, or it sounds like a threat.

> ✓ By Friday, I *will have finished* the draft.
> ✗ By Friday, I *will have finished* the draft. *(said on Monday, with no
> intention to)*

> ✓ By Monday, I *will have failed* all my exams. *(a joke, and a real
> prediction)*

The tense carries confidence. If you are not confident, use a modal:
*might have, could have, may have*.
</Warning>

## Perfect continuous

Not the same tense. The perfect says **an event is over**. The perfect
continuous says **a situation has lasted, and lasted visibly**.

| perfect | perfect continuous |
|---|---|
| I have *read* the book. | I have been *reading* the book. |
| finished, result | still going, or was going |
| the book is read | the reading is the point |

The continuous version is required with a few verbs and impossible with
others.

**Required**: *live, work, study, learn, wait, read, write, rain, snow,
play, run, walk, sit, stand, drive, talk.*

> I have been *working* here for ten years. ✓
> ✗ I have worked here for ten years. *(understandable but odd — it makes
> it sound like a series of jobs)*

**Impossible**: *know, like, love, hate, want, need, believe, own, belong,
seem, consist, contain, include, exist.*

> I have *known* him for years. ✓
> ✗ I have been knowing him for years.

**This is the test that separates the two for a state verb**: if the
continuous sounds wrong, the verb is a state, and the continuous form does
not exist.

<DeepDive>
The present perfect continuous has a second job that is easy to miss: it
criticises.

> *You have been working* all night. — it is four in the morning
> *You have worked* all night. — neutral, possibly admiring

The continuous puts the emphasis on the activity itself, over a duration,
which is why it can sound like an accusation. Native speakers use it to
signal irritation, or sometimes simple approval: *You have been doing
well.*

> *He has been asking* for the same thing for weeks. — this is a complaint
> *He has asked* for the same thing for weeks. — neutral

If you are writing and want to be neutral, use the perfect, not the
perfect continuous.
</DeepDive>

## The perfect, side by side

> I have *gone* to Berlin. *(I am not here; that is the current fact)*
> I have been *to* Berlin. *(I was there at some point, back now)*

> I have *finished* reading it. *(the reading is over)*
> I have been *reading* it. *(the reading has been going on)*

> I *had left* before you called. *(my leaving was earlier)*
> I *would have left* before you called. *(I did not leave)*

> By August I *will have finished*. *(complete by then)*
> By August I *will be finishing*. *(the finishing lands around then)*

> I have *worked* here for six years. *(possibly several jobs, or one)*
> I have been *working* here for six years. *(one continuous job)*

## Four things learners get wrong about the perfect

**Present perfect with a finished time.** ✗ *I have seen him yesterday.*
→ ✓ *I saw him yesterday.*

**Present perfect continuous with a state verb.** ✗ *I have been knowing
him.* → ✓ *I have known him.*

**Past perfect with a word that already fixes the order.** ✗ *After I had
left, she went out.* → ✓ *After I left, she went out.*

**Present perfect for a habit in a closed past period.** ✗ *I have visited
Paris three times when I lived there.* → ✓ *I visited Paris three times
while I lived there.*

<Warning>
If you take one thing from this chapter, take this: **the present perfect
is not "the distant past".** It is "the past that is still relevant now".

That single inversion fixes more errors than any other rule in this book,
because most of the errors come from treating the perfect as vaguer and
older than the simple, when in fact it is newer and closer.

> I have *read* it. — the book exists, I know it, it is relevant
> I *read* it. — that happened, and the matter is closed
</Warning>
