# How to listen

Reading and listening are separate systems. Twenty minutes a day of
reading will improve your reading and leave your listening exactly where
it was, because your ear has not been trained at all.

This is the most common gap in self-study, and it is the one that most
people give up on, because listening feels impossible. It is not
impossible. It is a technique problem, not a talent problem.

## The three passes

<Warning>
One listening attempt is always a failure. You will understand about
sixty per cent and it will feel like forty. This is normal and it is why
the material has to be used three times.
</Warning>

| pass | subtitles | what your brain does | what you get |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | none | you decode from sound alone | the gist, maybe 50% |
| 2 | English, on time | you confirm what you heard | 80 to 90% |
| 3 | English, **delayed 0.4s** | you produce the word yourself | real listening |

Most people stop after pass one, conclude they have a bad ear, and never
find out that pass three is where it happens.

## The delayed subtitle, and why it works

This is the single most useful listening technique and almost nobody
knows about it.

When subtitles are on time, you are reading with an audio track attached.
The eye does the work and the ear confirms.

When subtitles are four tenths of a second behind, there is a gap. The
word arrives, the subtitle has not. In that gap your brain has to produce
the word itself, and that production is the skill you are trying to build.

<DeepDive>
The reason it works is that recognition and production are different
operations, and subtitles on time only train recognition. Reading a
subtitle confirms the sound without ever generating it, so you can sit
through an hour of English and produce nothing at all.
<br><br>
The delay forces generation. It is uncomfortable, and the discomfort is
the signal that you are working at the right difficulty.
</DeepDive>

## Playing it

```bash
# 1. no subtitles
mpv --no-sub video.mkv

# 2. subtitles on time
mpv --sub-file=video.srt video.mkv

# 3. subtitles late, the real work
mpv --sub-file=video.srt --sub-delay=-0.4 video.mkv
```

Negative delay puts the subtitle in the future relative to the audio, so
it appears after the word is spoken. Four tenths of a second is the
usual starting point; if that feels easy, go to `-0.6`, and if it feels
impossible, drop to `-0.2`.

## Choosing material

| level | what to use | why |
|---|---|---|
| A2 | very short, slow, clear, familiar topic | you need the words to be nearly all known |
| B1 | a talk with a clear single speaker | 10 minutes is enough |
| B2 | a talk, a documentary, a drama series | variety of voice starts to matter |
| C1 | an audiobook, a lecture, a news programme | dense information, native speed |
| C2 | literature read aloud, a debate | speed and inference |

<Warning>
Do not use a film or series you have already seen in your own language.
It feels easier because you understand the plot, and the plot is not
English. You are being carried by a language you already have, and you
are not training the one you want.
</Warning>

## The graded ladder

Once a text is comfortable at 0.4 seconds, make it harder. Change one
thing at a time.

1. increase the delay: 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 seconds
2. remove the subtitles entirely
3. add background noise: a café, a bus
4. raise the speed to 1.1x, then 1.25x
5. choose something harder

Doing all five at once is the reason people plateau. The signal that you
are ready to advance is simple: **the delay stops feeling hard.**

## Listening to the unlistenable

At B2 and above you will hit material you cannot follow, and there are
three things worth knowing.

### The two-layer approach

One layer is comprehensible, one is not. Speak at a normal speed and you
will follow maybe seventy per cent, and that seventy per cent is enough
to keep going.

> The committee will presumably recommend that we **defer** the decision
> until the **revised** figures are available, which is frankly
> **disgraceful** after this much delay.

You need *defer*, *revised* and *disgraceful*. You can follow the
argument without them: something is being put off, the numbers changed,
and someone is angry about it. That is the content. The three words are
the content's clothing.

### The second-listen rule

<DeepDive>
Never look up a word while the audio is running. Stop it, write the word
down, and continue without it.
<br><br>
Looking up mid-stream breaks the flow, and the flow is what carries the
listening. The word will still be there in ten minutes. What you lose by
stopping is the twenty seconds of context that would have made the word
guessable anyway.
</DeepDive>

### Numbers and names

The first two minutes of almost any news item are numbers, dates, names
and places, and they are unlistenable. Let them go.

> *...on the fourteenth of March, in Kigali, Dr. Amara Okonkwo told
> reporters that...*

You lost forty seconds. You did not lose the story, and you will not
miss the story. Write `[names/numbers]` in your notes and move on.

## Shadowing

<DeepDive>
Shadowing is repeating aloud, in real time, a few seconds behind the
speaker. It is the most intense thing in this book and it should be the
last thing you add.
<br><br>
It works because it forces three things at once: you must hear the word,
you must produce it, and you must produce it fast enough to stay in sync.
The sync is the point. You are not reciting, you are keeping up with a
native speaker, and the gap between your fluency and that is exactly what
you feel.
<br><br>
Fifteen minutes, three times a week, on a passage you have already
listened to twice. If you cannot stay in sync, slow the audio to 0.9x
and build up.
</DeepDive>

## A twenty-minute listening session

| minutes | what |
|---|---|
| 0 to 8 | pass 1, no subtitles, gist only |
| 8 to 14 | pass 2, English subtitles on time, note the gaps |
| 14 to 20 | pass 3, subtitles at `-0.4`, repeat the gaps aloud |

Twenty minutes, three passes, one text. That is the whole method, and
doing it on five texts is worth more than doing pass one on fifty.

## What you should be able to do by C1

| skill | test |
|---|---|
| follow an argument in a talk | can you say what the speaker concluded? |
| follow a news item | can you say the headline without reading? |
| follow fiction | can you say what just happened? |
| follow a lecture | can you write three bullet points from it? |
| infer unknown words | can you say what a new word probably means? |

That last row is the one that marks real progress. When you start
guessing meaning from sound the way you already guess it from context, the
listening barrier has been crossed.
