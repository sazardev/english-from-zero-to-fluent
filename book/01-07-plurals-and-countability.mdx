# Plurals and countability

English has one plural ending and a long list of exceptions, but the
mistake learners actually make is not the plural. It is treating a noun as
countable when it is not, or the reverse. That single distinction causes
more errors than every irregular plural in the language.

## The regular plural

Add *-s* to a singular noun.

> *one* book, *two* **books**
> *one* table, *three* **tables**
> *one* idea, *many* **ideas**

Three spelling changes when you add *-s*.

| after | add | examples |
|---|---|---|
| most consonants | *-s* | *books, tables, dogs* |
| *-s, -x, -z, -ch, -sh* | *-es* | *boxes, buses, watches, dishes* |
| consonant + *-y* | *-ies* | *cities, countries, studies* |
| *-f, --fe* | usually *-ves* | *leaves, knives, wives* |

## The irregular plurals

This list is short and worth learning as a list. Most of the nouns on it
are countable and few of them occur often, so a learner who skips it will
be understood anyway and will eventually be caught.

| singular | plural | note |
|---|---|---|
| *man* | *men* | |
| *woman* | *women* | |
| *child* | *children* | |
| *person* | *people* | the plural is irregular, *persons* means individuals |
| *foot* | *feet* | |
| *tooth* | *teeth* | |
| *goose* | *geese* | |
| *mouse* | *mice* | the animal; *mouses* is not it |
| *louse* | *lice* | |
| *die* | *dice* | a single dice is a die; *dice* is also the unchanged plural |
| *penny* | *pence* | *pennies* is also correct, for the coins |
| *half* | *halves* | |
| *calf* | *calves* | |
| *leaf* | *leaves* | |
| *life* | *lives* | |
| *knife* | *knives* | |
| *wife*, *wolf* | *wives, wolves* | |
| *self* | *selves* | *ourselves, yourselves, themselves* |
| *loaf* | *loaves* | |
| *thief* | *thieves* | |

<Warning>
Four plurals are the same in the singular and the plural, and the only
signal is the verb.

> *three* **sheep** *are* grazing.  ✓
> *three* **sheep** *is* grazing.  ✗
> *two* **deer** *were* shot.  ✓

> *two* **fish** *were* caught.
> *a* **fish** *was* caught.

> *two* **species** *exist*. / *a* **species** *exists*.
> *two* **aircraft** *are* on the tarmac.
> *one* **aircraft** *is* on the tarmac.

> *Ten* **degrees Celsius** *is* how cold it is. — a temperature, read as
> one measurement
> *Ten* **degrees** *are* in the room. — ten separate degrees, no
> measurement

The verb is the only way a reader can tell, and this is the most reliable
way to fix it in your own speech: when in doubt about a plural, look at the
verb in the next sentence.
</Warning>

## Countable nouns and uncountable nouns

**Countable** nouns have a singular and a plural, and you can count them.
*a book, two books, three books.*

**Uncountable** nouns have no plural, no *-s*, and no *a*.

> ✗ *two **informations*** → ✓ *two pieces of information*
> ✗ *an **advice*** → ✓ *a piece of advice*
> ✗ *three **equipages*** → ✓ *three pieces of equipment*
> ✗ *a **research*** → ✓ *a piece of research*
> ✗ *four **softwares*** → ✓ *four pieces of software*
> ✗ *an **equipment*** → ✓ *some equipment*

<Warning>
The uncountable error is a real hole in the sentence, and it is always
visible: the missing article is the clue. If you write *I need information*,
the reader is waiting for a word that is not coming.

What is uncountable falls into groups, and the groups are worth learning
because they are predictable.

| group | uncountable |
|---|---|
| material and substance | *water, milk, air, wood, cotton, glass, paper, plastic, wood* |
| food | *bread, cheese, butter, sugar, rice, coffee, milk, jam, meat* |
| liquid and gas | *air, oxygen, water, oil, steam, smoke, juice* |
| abstractions | *advice, information, knowledge, evidence, progress, research, freedom, courage, honesty, work* |
| qualities and activities | *fun, laughter, happiness, help, homework, work, damage, rubbish* |
| sciences and fields | *mathematics, physics, economics, politics, statistics, linguistics* |
| time and measure | *time, space, energy, power, money, room* |

Three fixes exist, and all three are needed.

**A container word.** *a piece of, a glass of, a cup of, a bag of, an item
of, a slice of*

> *a piece of* advice
> *a piece of* information
> *two items of* furniture
> *three slices of* bread
> *a glass of* water

**Pluralisation, where a plural exists.**

> *furnitures* → *furniture items*, or *pieces of furniture*
> *informations* → *information items* → *pieces of information*
> *softwares* → *software packages*
> *researches* → *research studies*, or *studies*

**Verbalisation.** A verb, which is usually the cleanest.

> *a research* → *to conduct research*, *to do research*
> *an advice* → *to give advice*
> *an equipment* → *to equip*
> *a damage* → *to damage*
> *a homework* → *to do homework*

<DeepDive>
English has been losing countability for centuries, and the direction is
consistent: abstract nouns have been turning into countable ones, one
century at a time, and the process is still running. This explains several
things that otherwise look like arbitrary rules.

> *research* → *a research study*, *researches*
> *information* → *an information item*
> *advice* → *advices* — this has never been accepted, and a learner who
> tries it will be marked wrong
> *evidence* → *evidences* — rare, and mostly wrong outside law
> *damage* → *damages* — in law only, meaning the cost of repairing

The pattern is that a noun becomes countable when it needs to be referred
to as a unit: a single instance of a thing, or an item in a list.

*We need information* — the mass, collectively.
*We need an information sheet* — one unit, one document.

The same shift happened with *advice* in the eighteenth century, and
*advices* is still rejected, which is a live example of a change in
progress being visible in the language. It is also why dictionaries
disagree: the countability of a word is not a fact about the word, it is a
fact about how the word is being used, and English is still deciding.
</DeepDive>

## Nouns that are both

Some nouns are countable in one sense and uncountable in another, and the
sense decides the treatment.

| noun | uncountable use | countable use |
|---|---|---|
| *work* | *a lot of **work*** | *a **work** of art*, *four **works** by Monet* |
| *paper* | *some **paper*** | *a **paper***, *two **papers** — academic articles |
| *coffee* | *some **coffee*** | *a **coffee***, *two **coffees*** — cups |
| *experience* | *lots of **experience*** | *a **experience***, *several **experiences*** — events |
| *time* | *a lot of **time*** | *a **time*** — an occasion |
| *hair* | *long **hair*** | *a **hair*** — a single strand |
| *light* | *a lot of **light*** | *a **light*** — a lamp |
| *knowledge* | *a lot of **knowledge*** | ✗ no countable form |
| *information* | *a lot of **information*** | *an **information*** — rare, and marked |

> ✓ *She has **experience** in teaching.*
> ✓ *She had two frightening **experiences** as a child.*

The same word, the same sentence structure, and the treatment depends on
the meaning.

<Warning>
A handful of uncountable nouns have a countable form that means something
different, and learners who add the *-s* have produced a different claim
rather than a grammar error.

> *a **beauty*** — a woman who is beautiful, or an exceptional thing
> ✗ *a **beauties***

> *a **power*** — an electricity supply, a political influence
> *the **powers** that be* — a phrase from a film, and a different thing
> entirely

> *a **spirit*** — a mood, a soul, a ghost
> *the **spirits*** — alcohol, and the mood in the plural

> *a **premise*** — a statement in an argument
> *the **premises*** — a building and the land around it

The last one is the classic, because *premise* and *premises* are
unrelated in meaning and both are common, and a learner who assumes a
pattern will get it wrong in a business email.
</Warning>

## How to count uncountable nouns

Four methods, and all four are in normal use.

**By weight or volume.**

> *two kilos of* flour
> *a litre of* milk
> *three metres of* cloth

**By container.**

> *a packet of* tea
> *a bottle of* wine
> *a bar of* soap
> *a loaf of* bread
> *a slice of* cheese
> *a can of* beer

**By unit named explicitly.**

> *twenty kilos of* potatoes
> *a hundred head of* cattle
> *fifty head of* sheep
> *ten thousand head of* visitors

**By making it plural with a different word.**

> *two pieces of* furniture
> *three items of* luggage
> *four kinds of* cheese
> *five sorts of* insurance

## Four things learners get wrong about plurals and countability

**Pluralising an uncountable noun.** ✗ *informations, advices, softwares,
equipages*. → *pieces of information, a piece of advice, software,
equipment*.

**Missing the article before an uncountable noun.** ✗ *I need information*
→ ✓ *I need **some** information*. The missing word is the clue.

**Treating *people* as the plural of *person* everywhere.** *Several people
were there* is correct; *three persons of the board* means three
individuals, and in most contexts *people* is what you want.

**A singular noun with a plural verb.** ✗ *two sheep is* → ✓ *two sheep
are*. The verb is the only clue, so check it.
</Warning>
