# Reading poetry

Poetry is in this book for a reason that has nothing to do with
appreciating it. Poetry is compressed English in which **every word is
load-bearing**, and a learner who reads even a few poems becomes
alert to the choices that ordinary prose hides.

## Why it belongs in a course

Ordinary prose is invisible. You read *I went to the shop* and see
meaning, and you do not register that *shop* could have been *store*, or
that *went* could have been *walked*.

A poem makes the choice visible by refusing the ordinary word. Once you
have noticed a poet reaching for a strange word in a line, you start
noticing a writer in a novel reaching for a plain one, and that noticing
is comprehension.

> *I have eaten the plums* / *that were in the icebox* — the plums are
> ordinary, the icebox is not, and the oddness is what makes the
> sentence feel like a held breath

## Six things to look for

**Repetition and variation.** Poets repeat a word and change it by one
degree, and the change carries the meaning.

> *I have eaten the plums* / *that were in the icebox* / *and those
> that were frozen with the ice on the stream*
> — the same two lines repeated, and the tense and the repetition are the
> poem

**Line breaks.** A line break is a decision, not a formatting convenience.
A reader can stop anywhere in a line, and the poet decides where you must.

> *Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?* / *Thou art more lovely and
> more temperate:*
> — the break before *more* makes the second clause arrive like a
> correction

**Enjambment.** A sentence running past the end of a line.

> *The woods are lovely, dark and deep,* / *But I have promises to keep,*
> / *And miles to go before I sleep.*
> — each line ends mid-thought, and the reader is carried over the breaks

Every line here is a complete clause except the last, and that is the
point: the poem is a movement, and the line endings are the beats.

**Repetition of structure.** Parallelism, and it is how a poem builds
force.

> *Because I could not stop for Death —* / *He kindly stopped for me —* / *The carriage held but just ourselves —* / *And Immortality.*
> — four lines, four structures, each shorter than the last, and the last
> one line long

**Sound.** Vowel sounds carry the long vowels, and a poem that rhymes in
English is rhyming on the vowel, not the final consonant.

> *moon*, *soon*, *June* — rhymed on /uː/
> *night*, *light*, *bright* — rhymed on /aɪt/

**Diction.** The register of the words, and a deliberate jump in level is
one of the oldest moves there is.

> *He czar of the sky* — *czar* is a transliteration, chosen for its
> hard consonants rather than for its meaning, and the effect is to make
> the word sound foreign inside an English sentence
> *The prince of darkness was a gentleman* — *prince* and *gentleman* are
> elevated, and the collision with the devil is the joke

<Warning>
The word order of poetry is not English word order, and a learner who
corrects it destroys the poem.

> *In a station of the Metro* — not *In a metro station*
> *Because I could not stop for Death —* — the *for* is not idiomatic, and
> idiomatic would be *for me*
> *I have eaten the plums* / *that were in the icebox* — a relative clause
> after the object, which is exactly what part 1 says English does not do

Emily Dickinson's most famous poem reverses the order of half its
prepositions in a few lines, and the reversals are not mistakes. They are
the poem.

The practical rule: read it as written, and ask what the order is doing
rather than correcting it. If a line sounds wrong, that is a signal about
emphasis, not about error.
</Warning>

## Fixed forms worth meeting

| form | what it does | example |
|---|---|---|
| sonnet | 14 lines, a turn at 9 | Shakespeare, sonnets 18 and 130 |
| ballad | quatrains, ABCB rhyme, narrative | *The Ballad of Reading Gauss* |
| limerick | 5 lines, AABBA, comic | most limericks you have heard |
| blank verse | unrhymed iambic pentameter | *The Merchant of Venice*, Milton |
| haiku | 3 lines of 5, 7, 5 syllables, a cut | *The Fog comes* |
| free verse | no metre, no rhyme | most twentieth-century poetry |
| prose poem | a poem in sentences | *The Great Figure* |
| villanelle | 19 lines, two rhymes, two refrains | Bishop, *One Art* |

The forms are learnable and it pays to know two or three, because the form
is what tells you what to expect next. A sonnet's turn at line 9 is a
reversal of argument, and if you know that, you can read an unfamiliar
sonnet and predict its shape.

## A poem, read

Here is Emily Dickinson's *Because I could not stop for Death*, in full,
because a complete poem teaches more than a list of devices.

> Because I could not stop for Death —
> He kindly stopped for me —
> The carriage held but just ourselves —
> And Immortality.
>
> We paused before a House that seemed
> A Swelling of the Ground —
> The Roof was scarcely visible —
> The Footstep overpaid —
>
> But then a Bell, within, I heard
> A single toll, to tell
> The Hour of the Departing —
> The Hour of the Burial —
>
> And then we passed before a House
> That seemed a stately House —
> And through it, somewhere, a Drum —
> And then, within, a Cake —
>
> The Carriage, in these Boots, did pass
> And I could see a Face —
> And then a Casement, in the Dark —
> And then — I could not see —

Four stanzas, four rhyme schemes that change, one image that darkens from
a house to a grave to a room to a face to nothing. The noun in the first
line of each stanza is the subject of that stanza, and the sequence is
house, house, carriage, and then nothing.

There is no rhyme in the last stanza, and the poem's last line is a
dash, and those are the only two things you need to know to understand why
this poem works.

## Writing poetry as an exercise

Three exercises, and each one trains something a learner needs for
ordinary English.

**Compression.** Take a paragraph and cut it by half. Every cut has to go.
The point is to learn which words are load-bearing, and you cannot feel that
in a paragraph you wrote yourself.

**Repetition with variation.** Write the same sentence five times, changing
one word each time. Read the five together. This teaches you what a word
does, which is the single most useful thing about knowing a word.

**Read aloud.** Poetry is written for the ear, and a line that is beautiful
on the page can be unreadable aloud. Every one of Dickinson's inversions is
tested by saying it out loud, and the ones that fail are the ones that were
only ever about the page.
