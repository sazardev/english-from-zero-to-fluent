# Connectors

A connector joins two ideas and tells the reader how they relate. Choosing
the wrong one is worse than using none, because a wrong connector asserts
a relationship that is not there.

## The four kinds

| kind | joins | example |
|---|---|---|
| additive | adds | *and, also, besides, moreover* |
| additive, contrastive | adds against expectation | *but, although, while, whereas, yet* |
| consequential | causes | *so, therefore, because, since, as a result* |
| sequential | orders in time | *first, then, next, finally, meanwhile* |

There is a fifth that matters more than the other four: **concessive**,
which adds against the strongest expectation.

> *He prepared carefully. **Nevertheless** he failed.*
> *He prepared carefully. **Still** he failed.*
> *He prepared carefully. **Even so** he failed.*

<Warning>
English has a very large stock of these, and a learner who learns them as
a list produces sentences where the connector contradicts the meaning of
the clauses around it.

> *I did not go **because** I was busy.* — reason
> *I went **because** I was busy.* — reason
> *I went, **because** I was busy.* — with the comma, this is a different
> claim: "I went, and the reason was that I was busy"

The comma after a consequential *because* or *since* reverses the
emphasis. It puts the main clause first and the reason second, which
changes what the sentence is about.

> *I went because I was busy.* — the reason is the point
> *I went, because I was busy.* — the going is the point

The same applies to *but* versus *yet*, and to *so* versus *therefore*.
</Warning>

## Additive

| connector | strength | use |
|---|---|---|
| *and* | neutral | the default |
| *and also* | slight emphasis | |
| *plus* | informal | |
| *as well as* | neutral, formal | |
| *besides* | adds to what is already there | *Besides, it was raining.* |
| *moreover* | formal, adds a further point | *Moreover, the cost rose.* |
| *what is more* | adds weight | *It was late; what is more, it was cold.* |

*Besides* and *what is more* are not the same as *and*, and the difference
is worth noticing: they insist that the second point is additional rather
than merely subsequent.

> *I like the film and the music.* — two things
> *I like the film; **besides**, I like the music.* — the music is an extra,
> an argument that could have stopped earlier

## Contrastive

This is the largest and most misused group, and the misuse is almost always
about a distinction that does not exist in English.

| connector | what it contrasts |
|---|---|
| *but* | two facts that are simply different |
| *yet* | same, slightly formal, stronger surprise |
| *however* | same, formal, and it can be a sentence on its own |
| *although, though* | concession, with a subordinate clause |
| *while, whereas* | two parallel facts, usually with a comma |
| *despite, in spite of* | concession, with a noun phrase |
| *whereas* | two facts being compared as parallel |
| *instead* | one thing in place of another |
| *rather* | a mild correction |
| *on the other hand* | a second point, in a list |
| *in fact* | a correction, and the second point is the true one |

<Warning>
*Despite* and *although* are the pair that produces the most confident
errors, because they take different things.

**Although / though** takes a clause with a subject and a verb.

> ✓ **Although** it *was raining*, we went out.
> ✗ Although the rain, we went out.

**Despite / in spite of** takes a noun phrase or a gerund, never a clause.

> ✓ **Despite** the rain, we went out. ✓
> ✓ **Despite** the weather, we went out. ✓
> ✗ **Despite** it *was raining*, we went out.
> ✗ **Despite of** the rain, we went out. ✗

The two errors that follow from this are both extremely common.

> ✗ *In spite of it was late*, we left.
> ✓ *In spite of the lateness*, we left. / *Even though it was late*, we left.

> ✗ *Despite of the problem*, we continued.
> ✓ *Despite the problem*, we continued.

*Despite of* is not English. It appears because the phrase feels like
*in spite of* and the preposition carries over, and the two are fixed
compounds: *in spite of* and *despite*, never *in spite of* the noun alone
being interchangeable with *despite* the noun plus something else.
</Warning>

### *While* against *although*

| connector | shape | example |
|---|---|---|
| *while* | two clauses, comma between | *She reads **while** he cooks.* |
| *while* | concession, with a clause | ***While** I agree, I see a problem.* |
| *although* | concession, with a clause | ***Although** I agree, I see a problem.* |
| *whereas* | two parallel facts, comma | *She reads a lot, **whereas** he hardly reads.* |

*While* is genuinely ambiguous, and the two meanings are unrelated.

> *I read **while** she writes.* — simultaneously
> ***While** I understand the rule, I disagree with it.* — although

The first has two subjects and describes two actions. The second has one
subject and two verbs, and means *although*. If the sentence has one
subject and *while* comes after a comma, it is concession.

## Consequential

| connector | shape | example |
|---|---|---|
| *so* | general | *It was late, **so** we left.* |
| *therefore* | formal, often the subject | ***Therefore** the decision stands.* |
| *because* | general | ***Because** it was late, we left.* |
| *since* | formal, or temporal | ***Since** it was late, we left.* |
| *as a result* | emphasizes the outcome | *He was late; **as a result**, we left.* |
| *so that* | purpose, and a real clause follows | *He spoke slowly **so that** I could understand.* |
| *due to* | after a noun | ***Due to** the delay, we left.* |

*So* and *therefore* can begin a sentence, and when they do, they take a
comma after them.

> *It was late. **So**, we left.
> ***Therefore**, the decision stands.*

*Because* cannot begin a sentence in formal English. It is a conjunction,
and a sentence cannot start with a conjunction. Informal English does it
constantly.

> ✗ *Because it was late, we left.* *(formal)*
> ✓ *It was late, **so** we left.*
> ✓ *We left **because** it was late.*

<DeepDive>
*Since* is worth a paragraph of its own, because it means two unrelated
things and using the wrong one is a real meaning error.

**Since, meaning because.** Formal, and interchangeable with *because*.

> ***Since** the road was closed, we took a detour.*
> ***Because** the road was closed, we took a detour.*

**Since, meaning from a point in time.** And then it takes the present
perfect, because the situation is still true.

> ***Since** I moved here, I have not seen her.* *(from the move until now)*
> ✗ *Since I moved here, I did not see her.*

> *I have been learning English **since** 2019.*
> *I have been learning English **for** six years.*

If the sentence also has a past tense verb and no perfect, *since* is
almost certainly being used as *because*.

The test: if you can replace it with *from that point onwards*, it is the
temporal *since*. If you can replace it with *because*, it is the causal
one.
</DeepDive>

## Sequential

| marker | position |
|---|---|
| *first, second, third, finally* | the usual list |
| *then, next, afterwards* | simple sequence |
| *meanwhile, in the meantime* | two things happening at once |
| *eventually* | after a long delay, often with effort |
| *finally* | the last one, and the sense of arrival |
| *subsequently, previously* | formal |
| *in the end* | the outcome, often not the plan |
| *to begin with* | the first point, in an argument |
| *on the one hand ... on the other hand* | two balanced points |

## The connectors to be careful with

**So** and *because* do not go together. ✗ *So because it was late* is
two reasons for one result.

**And** cannot join two sentences about different times without
punctuation, or the reader will treat it as a list.

> ✗ *I was tired and I went to bed.* — actually fine, but ambiguous
> ✓ *I was tired, and I went to bed.*

**But** and *however* differ in punctuation. *However* takes a comma; *but*
does not.

> ✓ *However*, we continued. / ✓ *We continued, however*.
> ✗ *However we continued.*
> ✓ *But we continued.* / ✗ *We continued but.*

**Whereas** needs two parallel facts, and it is wrong when one side is
longer or less parallel than the other.

> ✓ *She likes tea **whereas** he likes coffee.*
> ✗ *She likes tea in the morning whereas he usually prefers a large
> black coffee, although not always.*

## The order of connectors

In English, the connector that states the writer's attitude comes last.

> *He is young, **but** he is responsible.* — attitude after the facts
> *Although he is young**,** he is responsible.* — attitude first

This is why *although* usually appears first and *but* usually appears in
the middle: *although* is a conjunction and must open its clause, and *but*
is a coordinator and can sit anywhere.

> *Although* he is young, he is responsible.
> He is young **but** he is responsible.
> He is young, **however**, he is responsible. ✗ — two coordinators

You cannot use two coordinators in a row. That is why *but* and *however*
are alternatives, never partners.

## Four things learners get wrong about connectors

**Two coordinators.** ✗ *I was tired but however I went.* → *I was tired,
however, I went.*

*Despite of.* → *Despite*, or *even though* with a clause.

**A clause after *despite*.** → *even though* + clause, or *despite* + noun.

**Starting a formal sentence with *because*.** → *Since* + clause, or
restructure with *so*.
