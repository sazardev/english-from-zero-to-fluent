# How to write

Writing is where grammar stops being knowledge and becomes ability. You
can believe you know the present perfect for years and find out in one
afternoon that your hand reaches for the past simple every time.

Write. That is the whole chapter. The rest is how to get the feedback
that makes writing teach.

## The daily session

Fifteen minutes, one topic, one hundred and fifty to two hundred words.
Not more. The number matters, because a number you can always reach is
what makes a habit survive a bad day.

| words | what happens |
|---|---|
| 50 | not enough to hit a real structure |
| 150 to 200 | enough to need three tenses, and that is the point |
| 400 | you start writing around the grammar instead of through it |
| 1000 | you will not do it tomorrow either |

<Warning>
The most common way to abandon writing is to set a target you meet on
good days. Set one you meet on bad days. A hundred and fifty words
written badly is worth more than a thousand written carefully once a
fortnight.
</Warning>

## Choose topics that force tenses

A topic that can be answered in the present teaches you the present.

| topic | forces |
|---|---|
| what I did last week | past simple, past continuous |
| what I have learned this year | present perfect, present perfect continuous |
| what I would do differently | second conditional |
| a plan I have made | going to, present continuous for arrangements |
| an opinion I disagree with | modals of certainty, hedging |
| a story from my childhood | the whole narrative system |

<DeepDive>
Pick the topic *because* of the grammar, not despite it. "My holiday" can
be written entirely in the present simple if you describe the photo album
rather than the holiday, and you will practise nothing.
<br><br>
"Three things that changed my mind last year" cannot be written without
the past perfect, because the mind had to change before the change. The
title chooses the tense for you.
</DeepDive>

## The three passes over your own text

Writing once and checking once is not a method. Writing is three passes
and the passes have different jobs.

| pass | job | when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | meaning: is it clear what you meant? | after writing |
| 2 | grammar: check the tenses and the articles | after a break |
| 3 | flow: read it aloud | next day |

### Pass 1, meaning

Read it and ask one question: could a reader who has never met me say
back what I meant? Not is it correct, is it clear.

If the answer is no, the problem is not grammar. It is that you assumed
the reader knows what you are thinking, and nobody does.

### Pass 2, grammar

<DeepDive>
Do this after a break, not immediately. Twenty minutes later you see
things that were invisible while you were writing, and you catch your own
mistakes because they stop being what you meant and start being what you
typed.
<br><br>
Check in this order, and the order is not arbitrary:
<br><br>
<b>1. Tenses.</b> Is every verb in the right tense for its time reference?
This is where most errors live.
<br><br>
<b>2. Articles.</b> Every countable singular noun needs one. English
demands it and Spanish does not, so this is the highest-yield check
there is.
<br><br>
<b>3. Word order.</b> In a question, the auxiliary comes first: *Did you
go*, not *You did go?*
<br><br>
<b>4. Endings.</b> Plurals, third person *-s*, participles.
</DeepDive>

### Pass 3, flow

Read it aloud. Your ear finds things your eye skips, and it finds them
faster than any checklist.

| what you hear | what it means |
|---|---|
| you run out of breath | the sentence is too long, or the words are too long |
| you stumble | the word order is wrong, or the word is not the one you meant |
| you would not say it | the register is wrong |
| there is a silence | you have written something you do not believe |

<Warning>
If you would not say it out loud, do not write it. Formal written English
full of words you would never speak is not better English. It is a
different and much rarer register, and it is part 8.
</Warning>

## Getting feedback without a teacher

You do not need a person. You need a discrepancy.

| method | what it catches | cost |
|---|---|---|
| reread after a break | tenses, agreement | free |
| read aloud | flow, register, word order | free |
| search your writing for `\bthe\b` and check each one | articles | free |
| a grammar checker offline | articles, verb forms, agreement | free |
| a local language model | everything, and it explains | free, needs a model |

The last one is worth setting up properly, because it is the only method
that gives you a second opinion on meaning rather than form.

<DeepDive>
Give it a strict prompt, or it will be encouraging rather than useful:
<br><br>
<i>You are a strict English examiner. I am a B2 Spanish speaker. Below is
my English. List every error as: what I wrote, the correction, and a
one-line reason in English. Group them by grammar, articles, prepositions,
verb form, word choice, punctuation. Do not rewrite the text for me.</i>
<br><br>
The "do not rewrite" instruction is what makes it useful. A model that
rewrites gives you a sentence you can copy and learn nothing.
</DeepDive>

## The four things that get checked most

If you only ever check four things, check these. They are between sixty
and eighty per cent of the errors in learner writing.

### Articles

> ✗ I went to *school* to learn English.
> ✓ I went to *the* school to meet the teacher.

Two errors, one check. Look at every noun and ask whether the reader
already knows which one you mean.

| you mean | you need |
|---|---|
| a specific one, already mentioned | *the* |
| any one, not yet specified | *a* or *an* |
| a general category | plural, or uncountable, no article |
| a unique thing | *the* — the sun, the internet |
| a place as an institution | zero — *go to school, work at hospital* |
| a country or city | zero — *in Spain, to Madrid* |
| a meal | zero — *have breakfast* |
| a language or subject | zero — *speak English, study history* |

### Tenses

> ✗ When I arrived, she already left.
> ✓ When I arrived, she had already left.

Two past actions, the earlier one takes the past perfect. This is not a
guess, it is a rule with no exceptions.

### Prepositions after verbs

> ✗ I look forward to see you.
> ✓ I look forward to seeing you.

Some verbs take a preposition, and after the preposition comes a gerund,
not an infinitive. These are learned as units: *look forward to + ing*,
*insist on + ing*, *be used to + ing*, *object to + ing*.

### Word choice

> ✗ I want that you come.
> ✓ I want you to come.

Not an error of form. An error of structure, and it comes from
translating. *Want* and a few dozen other verbs take *object + to +
infinitive*, never *that*.

## Write about what you know

The topic is not the point, the output is. But a topic you know something
about produces sentences with real content, and real content is what
forces you into real structure.

| writes easily | writes badly |
|---|---|
| a description of a place you have been | a description of a place you have not seen |
| an opinion you hold | an opinion you have to invent |
| a story that happened | a story that might have |

<DeepDive>
The right-hand column produces simple, safe sentences, because you have
no opinion to express and no detail to report. You write *people live in
big cities* because you have nothing specific to say, and *nobody* is
learning anything from it.
<br><br>
You do not need to be interesting. You need to be specific. A dull
specific sentence teaches more than an exciting vague one.
</DeepDive>

## Keeping the errors

One file. Every mistake, the correction, and the reason.

```
Look forward to see you.      ->  look forward to + gerund
I have been lived here.       ->  no passive of "live"
If I was you, I would go.     ->  were for every person in a 2nd conditional
```

Three lines a week is normal after a few months. Most people find that
between three and five of them account for most of what they get wrong,
and those five are worth more than this whole chapter.
