# Paragraphs

A paragraph is one idea, developed, with a first sentence that says which
idea and a last sentence that says what it came to. That is the whole
craft, and almost every bad paragraph fails on one of those two sentences
rather than on the sentences in between.

## The shape

| part | job | usually |
|---|---|---|
| topic sentence | what this paragraph is about | first |
| development | evidence, example, explanation | the middle |
| closing | what it means, or the link to the next | last |
| topic sentence | what the next paragraph is about | first of the next |

A paragraph of three sentences and a paragraph of nine have the same
shape. The difference is how much development fits, and a paragraph that
will not hold its development is two paragraphs.

<Warning>
The most common failure is a paragraph with two topic sentences, which is
really two paragraphs written as one. The symptom is a paragraph that
keeps changing its subject, and a reader who has followed the first half
finds the second half unrelated.

The test: read only the topic sentences of your paragraphs in order. If
that reads as a coherent argument, the paragraphs work. If it reads as a
list, you have written a list, and a list should be a list, in bullets.
</Warning>

## Topic sentences that work

**A claim, not a topic.**

> ✗ *There are several reasons for this.* — a topic, and it says nothing
> ✓ *Three factors account for the delay.*

**Specific enough to argue with.**

> ✗ *The results were interesting.*
> ✓ *The delay increased with temperature, which the model does not
> predict.*

**Not the last word of the previous paragraph repeated.**

> ✗ *That was one problem with the method. **The method** had other
> problems too.*
> ✓ *The method also assumes the sample is homogeneous, which it is not.*

<DeepDive>
There is a reliable test for a paragraph that is worth using on everything
you write, and it comes from asking what the paragraph would be called.

A paragraph has one job, so it has one natural name, and the name is either
the topic sentence with the verb removed, or a single word.

> *Three factors account for the delay.* — **the three causes of the delay**
> *The delay increased with temperature.* — **temperature and delay**
> *Learners who write errors down make fewer of them.* — **the error log**

If a paragraph has two names, it is two paragraphs. If it has no name, it
is not a paragraph.

The same test applied to a whole essay or a whole answer is a version of
the outline, and it is worth doing before writing rather than after. A one
sentence answer to "what is this essay about?" that requires two sentences
is a sign the essay is doing two things, and the reader will feel that
without being able to say why.
</DeepDive>

## Length

| element | sentences | note |
|---|---|---|
| topic sentence | 1 | |
| development | 2 to 6 | one idea per sentence |
| closing | 1 | sometimes the same as the topic |
| a whole paragraph | 4 to 8 | longer is not better |

A paragraph of one sentence is a paragraph, and it is used in speech, in
lists and in email. A paragraph of fifteen sentences has lost its shape
and needs a break.

## The link between paragraphs

A paragraph that ends without a link makes the reader work. Four ways to
link, and they are not interchangeable.

**Continuity.** The next paragraph is the same subject.

> *...and the delay increased with temperature.* → *The model does not
> predict this.*

**Consequence.** The next paragraph is a result.

> *The sample was contaminated.* → *As a result, the measured
> concentrations are unreliable.*

**Contrast.** The next paragraph is the opposite case.

> *Small samples were unreliable.* → *Large samples behaved differently.*

**Reference.** The next paragraph returns to something named.

> *There were three problems with the method.* → *The first was the
> sample.*

<Warning>
The phrase that links a paragraph to the next should name the connection,
not announce it. *Furthermore*, *moreover*, *in addition* and *it is also
worth noting that* are all ways of saying *and*, and using them adds four
words and no information.

> *The sample was contaminated. **Furthermore**, the pH was outside the
> specified range.*
> → *The sample was contaminated, and the pH was outside the specified
> range.*

The connective that earns its place is the one that tells the reader *why*
the next paragraph follows: *as a result, which suggests, in contrast,
the first of these*. The other connectives are punctuation pretending to
be logic.
</Warning>

## Four mistakes in paragraphs

**Two ideas in one paragraph.** Split it.

**A topic sentence at the end.** It can work, as a summary, and it must be
recognised as one. A paragraph whose only topic sentence is the last one
has delayed its point for no reason.

**A paragraph of one long sentence.** A sentence over about forty words is
hard to hold in the head, and a paragraph of three of them is unreadable.
Split the sentence; the paragraph gets shorter and the meaning does not
change.

**Chop.** Two short sentences that belong together, separated by a full
stop, read as a stutter. *The results were clear. The method was not.*
→ *The results were clear, but the method was not.*
