# Pronouns

Pronouns replace nouns. English has more of them than Spanish, and the
ones Spanish lacks are the interesting ones.

## The system

| subject | object | possessive adj | possessive pronoun | reflexive | reciprocal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | me | my | mine | myself | — |
| you | you | your | yours | yourself | each other |
| he | him | his | his | himself | each other |
| she | her | her | hers | herself | each other |
| it | it | its | — | itself | — |
| we | us | our | ours | ourselves | each other |
| they | them | their | theirs | themselves | each other |

<Warning>
*Its* is possessive, *it's* is the contraction of *it is*. A possessive
never takes an apostrophe, and an apostrophe always marks something
missing.
</Warning>

## The three "it" structures

English has three unrelated *its*, and choosing the wrong one is a very
visible error.

### it as a placeholder

> **It** is raining. **It** was cold yesterday. **It** seems fine.

For the weather, the time, or a situation already mentioned.

### it as an expletive, to open a sentence

> **It** is important to be honest.
> **It** was a mistake to leave.
> **It** seems that nobody knew.

<DeepDive>
English needs something in the subject slot before the verb, and when
there is no real subject, *it* fills it. A language that marks the verb
for person can simply leave the subject out and let the ending carry it,
so the equivalent English has to be built out of filler words:
<i>It is important to be honest.</i>
<br><br>
This is why so many English sentences start with *it* even when nothing
in the sentence is a thing. It is a grammatical scaffold, not a
reference. Once the real subject appears, *it* usually becomes *he*,
*she* or *they*.
<br><br>
<i>It is important for <b>students</b> to know that ...</i> is often better
than <i>It is important that students know that ...</i>, because it moves
the weight to the end and avoids stacking two clauses.
</DeepDive>

### it to refer to a whole previous idea

> She passed the exam. **It** was not easy.

## Personal pronouns: subject or object

The rule is position.

> **She** sings.  *(subject: before the verb)*
> I saw **her**.  *(object: after the verb)*

With *be*, the pronoun is always the subject.

> It is **I**. ✓
> It is **me**. ✗ — as a genuine subject this is old-fashioned. As a
> contrast it is emphatic: *Me? I don't know her.*

<Warning>
<em>Between you and me</em>, not <em>between you and I</em>. The phrase
treats *between* as a preposition, and a preposition takes the object
form. The same is true of *for*, *to* and *with* in these phrases.
</Warning>

## Singular they

One word, two jobs. It is the correct modern English for a person whose
gender is unknown or irrelevant.

> **They** emailed me to say they could not come.

<DeepDive>
The *they* was invented because English has no neuter singular pronoun.
Options all have costs:
<br><br>
<em>He</em> assumes a gender. <em>He or she</em> is clumsy and, before
*they*, often written with a slash. <em>It</em> is dehumanising for a
person.
<br><br>
<em>They</em> solves it and is now standard. It is used for one person and
is accepted in formal writing by every major style guide. It has a second
function too: <em>they</em> meaning "people in general", as in
<em>they say</em>.
</DeepDive>

## Possessives, the two jobs

| form | used before a noun | used alone |
|---|---|---|
| my, your, his, her, its, our, their | **the** book that is mine | yes |
| mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs | ✗ | yes |

<Warning>
*Hers* is the only one that differs visibly. <em>The book is her</em> is
wrong; it must be <em>the book is hers</em>.
</Warning>

## Reflexive pronouns

Used when the subject and the object are the same person, or for emphasis.

> She taught **herself**.
> He cut **himself**.
> The dog wagged **its tail** — not *itself*, because the tail is not the
> dog.

<DeepDive>
Two uses are worth separating.
<br><br>
<b>Reflexive as object:</b> <i>I hurt myself</i>. The action comes back
to the subject. Spanish does this with *se*, and often leaves the
reflexive out: <i>Me corté</i> versus <i>I cut myself</i>.
<br><br>
<b>Reflexive for emphasis:</b> <i>He was kind enough to give the money
himself</i>. Here it adds nothing to the meaning except emphasis. You
cannot remove it.
<br><br>
A common learner error is a reflexive that does not refer back to the
subject. <i>She sent it to himself</i> is wrong unless he sent it to
himself; if he sent it to her, it is <i>to herself</i>.
</DeepDive>

## Reciprocal

> They helped **each other**.
> The two of us greeted **each other**.
> They looked at **each other**, not *at us*.

*Each other* takes a plural or a pair. *One another* is the same thing
in formal English, with no grammatical difference.

## Relative pronouns

English splits one relationship into six pronouns, and the choice is made
by what stands in front of the pronoun and after it, not by meaning.

| | people | things | after a preposition | possessive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| subject | who | which / that | — | whose |
| object | whom | which / that | whom / which | whose |
| place | — | where | where | — |
| time | — | when | when | — |
| reason | — | why | why | — |

<Warning>
For everyday English, <em>who</em> replaces <em>whom</em> as the object
after a verb: <i>the man <b>who</b> I met</i> is normal, <i>whom</i> is
formal. Reserve <em>whom</em> for the awkward after-a-preposition slot,
where <em>who</em> is not possible at all: <i>to whom</i>, never *to
who*.
</Warning>

## Missing relative pronouns

English omits the relative pronoun whenever it is the object.

> the book (that) I bought
> the woman (whom) I met
> the film (which) we watched

<DeepDive>
You can only omit it for the object. If the relative pronoun is the
subject, it must stay, and the sentence becomes incomplete without it.
<br><br>
<i>the book ___ I bought</i> — fine, understood.
<br><br>
<i>the book ___ fell off the table</i> — not fine. <i>That</i> is the
subject of *fell*, so removing it leaves nothing.
<br><br>
In speech, <i>which</i> and <i>that</i> are also omitted as subjects, and
nobody notices. In writing, keep them.
</DeepDive>

## Demonstratives and quantifiers

| | singular | plural |
|---|---|---|
| close | this, these | that, those |
| far | that, those | those |
| pointing | this one, that one | these, those |

<Warning>
<i>this</i> points at what is near the speaker, <i>that</i> at what is
far. In writing, without either, the noun is general: <i>people</i>,
<i>life</i>, <i>the internet</i>.
</Warning>
