# Reading a paper

There is a specific, learnable way to read an academic paper, and almost
nobody does it, because the natural approach is to start at the beginning
and read to the end. The efficient approach reads a paper in about fifteen
minutes and tells you whether it is worth an hour.

## The fifteen-minute read

Read in this order, and stop at each point if the answer is no.

| step | where | minutes | what you are looking for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | title and abstract | 2 | is this about your question |
| 2 | introduction, last paragraph | 3 | what gap are they filling |
| 3 | section headings | 1 | the shape of the argument |
| 4 | the figures | 4 | what the evidence actually shows |
| 5 | conclusion | 2 | what they claim |
| 6 | methods | 3 | whether the claim is supportable |

<Warning>
The reason this works is that a paper is written in a conventional order
and the conventional order is not the informative order. The introduction
is written last and read first; the methods is written in the middle and
is the part that decides whether anything in the paper is true.

Reading in publication order means reading the least informative
3000 words before the most informative 200. Reading in the order above
means you have the claim, the evidence and the caveat within fifteen
minutes, and you can stop.
</Warning>

## The abstract

An abstract is a compressed paper with a fixed structure, and knowing the
structure tells you how to read it.

> **Background.** *Previous work has shown that the compound is stable in
> acidic conditions...*
> **Problem.** *...but the reported measurements rely on indirect methods.*
> **Method.** *This study measures the concentration directly, by
> titration...*
> **Result.** *We find that the concentration rises sharply...*
> **Conclusion.** *These results suggest that...*

The first two sentences are the most important, because they tell you the
gap. If the gap is not a gap you care about, stop.

## Section headings as an outline

Reading only the headings gives you the argument in order, in about thirty
words. This is the single most efficient thing a learner can do with a
paper, and almost nobody does it.

A paper with these headings is telling you its own structure:

> 1. Introduction
> 2. Related work
> 3. Method
> 4. Results
> 5. Discussion
> 6. Conclusion

And a paper with these headings is doing something unusual, which means
it may be interesting:

> 3. Why our baseline fails
> 4. A reframing of the problem
> 5. What this costs

Headings that argue rather than label are the sign of a paper with a
position, and those are the ones worth your hour.

## The figures

Most learners skip the figures, which is the mistake. A figure is the
evidence, and the text above it is interpretation.

What to check, in order.

**Is the axis labelled and does it start at zero?** A truncated axis
exaggerates differences and is the most common way a figure misleads.

> y-axis from 0.95 to 1.00 instead of 0 to 1.00

**Are the error bars present, and do they overlap?**

> No error bars means a single run, or a figure the authors believe is
> noise-free. In most fields, no error bars means the result is not yet
> solid.

**Does the caption say how many samples?**

> *Results from a single representative run* means n = 1, which in most
> fields means a demonstration, not a finding.

**Do the numbers in the text match the numbers in the figure?** A common
surprise: they do not, and the discrepancy is usually in the direction of
the larger number.

<DeepDive>
The vocabulary of a methods section is small, fixed, and worth learning as
a list, because it is the part that tells you whether a result is real, and
a learner who cannot read it has to take the results on trust.

**Design words.** *randomised, controlled, blinded, double-blind,
placebo-controlled, prospective, retrospective, cross-sectional,
longitudinal, randomised*

*Blinded* and *double-blind* are the ones a learner meets most. Blinded
means the researcher does not know which group each participant is in;
double-blind means neither the researcher nor the participant does. These
are the words that tell you whether a comparison is honest, and a study
without them in a medical or psychological paper is a study where the
expectations of the people running it are uncontrolled.

**Size words.** *power, significance, confidence interval, effect size,
sample size, n = , statistically significant*

*p* < 0.05 and *95% confidence interval* are the two a learner meets most.
The honest reading of *statistically significant* is **"unlikely to be
chance, and probably small"**, and a paper reporting only significance
without an effect size is hiding the second half of that.

**Honesty words.** *limitation, confounding factor, bias, sample bias,
selection bias, external validity, generalisability, confound*

*Confound* and *confounding factor* are the ones to watch. A confound is
something that varies with the thing you are measuring and produces the
effect you have attributed to it. A drug that appears to work may be
working because the people who take it are also eating less, and reading
the limitations section is how you find out.

**Withdrawal words.** *reject, revise, resubmit, referee, reviewer,
major revision, minor revision, desk reject, preprint*

A *major revision* means the work is sound and the argument is not yet
made. A *reject* from a journal usually means the fit is wrong rather than
the work is, because a rejected paper is usually submitted elsewhere and
published.

None of this requires understanding the field. It requires recognising
about forty words that repeat in every paper ever written, and knowing what
each one tells you about the reliability of what surrounds it.
</DeepDive>

## Vocabulary in a paper

Four kinds of word, and only one of them is the science.

| kind | example | treatment |
|---|---|---|
| the science | *titration, dissociation, convolution* | look up, and expect to look up |
| the method | *randomised, blinded, significance* | learn as a set, from part 7.3 |
| the claim | *suggest, indicate, demonstrate* | learn as a set, it is how you calibrate a claim |
| the filler | *utilise, ascertain, in order to, it is worth noting that* | delete it and see if anything is lost |

That last row is the useful one. *It is worth noting that*, *it is important
to observe that*, *in order to*, *due to the fact that*, *has the ability
to* and *in the event that* are all common in papers and all deletable.

> *It is worth noting that the results **show** a trend.*
> → *The results show a trend.*

Half the length, and nothing lost.

## Reading for structure, not for meaning

There is a reading strategy for a paper whose subject you do not know, and
it is the same one for a novel in a language you have not studied.

**Read the last sentence of each paragraph.** Paragraphs in academic prose
follow a topic-sentence-then-detail structure, and the last sentence is
usually the claim. Four paragraphs, four claims, and you have the argument.

**Read the first and last sentence of the paper.** These are usually the
whole thing, and the abstract is usually a worse version of the pair.

**Skim the middle for the numbers.** Find *n*, find the error bars, find
the confidence interval, find the effect size. If they are absent, the
paper is making a claim without evidence and you have learned that in two
minutes.

<Warning>
The one thing to avoid is reading a paper you have no background for as
though it is a novel, and giving up at the first unfamiliar noun. This is
the most common reason learners decide they cannot read academic English,
and the diagnosis is nearly always the same: they were trying to understand
everything at once, and they were reading linearly.

The alternative is to read for structure first and content second, and to
accept that the content will take a vocabulary list rather than a reading
comprehension. A paper is not a text to be understood in one pass. It is a
document to be taken apart, and the parts come in a known order, and knowing
the order is worth more than knowing the subject.
</Warning>

## Four things learners get wrong about papers

**Reading linearly.** Introduction to conclusion, three thousand words
before any evidence.

**Skipping the methods.** The methods decide whether the results are
possible.

**Missing that *statistically significant* does not mean large.** It means
unlikely to be chance.

**Accepting the first noun of every sentence as the subject.** Academic
sentences put methods and results in the subject slot, so *the analysis*
is not doing anything and *the samples* are.
