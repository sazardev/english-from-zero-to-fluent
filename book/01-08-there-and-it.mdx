# There, it and possessives

Three small subjects that carry more weight than their length suggests. A
learner who uses *there* correctly and *its* correctly writes noticeably
better English than a learner who does not, because these are the errors
that most clearly mark writing as foreign.

## *There* as a subject

*There* is not a real subject. It is a placeholder, and the real subject
comes after the verb.

> **There** **is** a *problem*.
> real subject: *a problem*

Because it is a placeholder, English puts the verb after it in agreement
with the real subject that follows, not with *there*.

> ***There is** one book on the table.*
> ***There are** three books on the table.*

This is the opposite of the ordinary rule. In a normal sentence the verb
agrees with the subject that comes **before** it.

> *The books **are** there.* — subject *books* comes first, so *are*

> *There **are** three books.* — subject *books* comes **after*, and the
> verb still agrees with it

<Warning>
The error is almost universal and it is understandable, because *there*
looks like the subject and the rule the learner learned says the verb
agrees with the subject.

> ✗ *There **is** many people at the door.*
> ✓ ***There are** many people at the door.*

> ✗ *There **is** two problems.*
> ✓ ***There are** two problems.*

> ✗ *There **is** a book and two pens.*
> ✓ ***There are** a book and two pens.* — the verb agrees with the
> nearest noun, which is *pens*

A reliable shortcut: find the noun after the verb, and make the verb agree
with it. The noun is always there, and it is never *there*.
</Warning>

### The contractions

*There is* and *there has* have standard contractions, and using them is
what makes writing sound like English rather than translated.

> ***There's** a problem.*
> ***There's** been a change.*
> ***There's** been a mistake.* *(past perfect)*

For *there are* and *there have*, the contraction exists but is avoided,
because *they're* and *they've* would be ambiguous with any plural noun
in the previous clause.

> *There **are** many reasons.* — normally uncontracted
> *There **have** been many changes.* — normally uncontracted

### *There* in other positions

*There* can be an adverb of place, and then it means something different.

> ***There** is the book you wanted.* — it exists, unspecified place
> ***There** sits a hill behind the village.* — formal, literary, and
> emphatic

The existential *there* and the place *there* are the same word doing two
jobs, and the sentence decides which:

> *The book **is there**.* — at that place
> ***There is** a book here.* — a book exists somewhere

## *It* as a subject

*It* fills the subject slot when there is no real subject, and there are
four distinct cases.

**The weather, the time and the world.**

> *It is raining. It is Tuesday. It is getting cold.*

**A situation in general.**

> ***It is** important to be honest.*
> ***It is** no use waiting.*
> ***It is** a waste of time to argue.*

**A reference back to something already mentioned.**

> *I have a car. **It** is fifteen years old.*

**Anticipating, with the real subject following.**

> ***It is** obvious that he was lying.*
> ***It is** no longer possible to ignore this.*

<Warning>
In the second and fourth cases, *it* is followed by an adjective, and the
real subject comes later. This produces a construction learners avoid
because the subject is so far from the verb, and the avoidance is worse
than the construction.

> ✗ *Being honest is important.* — this is not wrong, but it is stiff and
> it buries the point
> ✓ ***It is important** to be honest.*
> ✓ *I think **it is important** to be honest.*

> ✗ *To ignore the problem is impossible.*
> ✓ ***It is impossible** to ignore the problem.*

The version with *it* is shorter, and it is the version a speaker would
say. The other one is a sentence that has been assembled rather than
spoken.
</Warning>

### *It* and *there* together

Two placeholders in one sentence, and the order is fixed.

> *It is clear that there is a problem.*

*It* for the situation, *there* for existence. And *it* always comes
first, because *it* is the subject of the outer clause and *there* belongs
to the inner one.

> *It is obvious that **there** is no alternative.*

## Possessives

Two mechanisms, and the difference between them is worth knowing.

### The apostrophe, for possession and omission

*'s* attaches to a noun.

> *the **dog's** tail* — the dog owns the tail
> *my **brother's** house*
> *the **students'** union* — plural, and the apostrophe goes on the *s*

| | singular | plural ending in *-s* | plural not ending in *-s* |
|---|---|---|---|
| possessive | ***the dog's** tail* | ***the students'** union* | ***the children's** room* |
| contraction | ***the dog's** sick* | ***the students'** here* | — |

**To avoid repeating a noun**, the apostrophe means *the one that is*.

> *the **dog's** bowl* — the dog's bowl, not mine
> *the **dog's** bowl and mine*
> *This book is better than John**'s**.*
> *This book is better than John**'s is**.*

<Warning>
The apostrophe has one job in English and it is not decoration. It marks
**something missing**: a letter, or a noun.

| apostrophe present | apostrophe absent | what is missing |
|---|---|---|
| *the dog**'s** tail* | *the **dog tail*** | the *-s* |
| *John**'s** book* | *the dog tail* | nothing, different words |
| ***don't*** | *do not* | the second *o* |
| ***it's*** | *its* | the second *t* |
| ***they're*** | *their* | the *h* |
| ***who's*** | *whose* | the *e* |

The two errors that follow are the most visible in learner writing.

**A plural apostrophe on a singular noun.** ✗ *the dog**'s*** and ✗
*the dogs tail*. The possessive is *the dog's tail*; the plural of
*dog* is *dogs*, and *the dogs' tails* is a different thing.

**A possessive where a contraction is meant.** ✗ ***Its** raining.* →
✓ ***It's** raining.* ✗ ***Its** a nice day.* → ✓ ***It's** a nice day.*

The second one is the single most frequent error in learner English
writing, and it is always the same three letters. If in doubt, say the
sentence out loud. "Its raining" is ungrammatical as a phrase, and your ear
will catch it even if your eye does not.
</Warning>

### The *of* form, for long and abstract possessors

An apostrophe on a long phrase is unreadable, so English switches to *of*.

> *the **death of** his father*
> *the **beginning of** the war*
> *the **roof of** the building*
> *the **result of** the experiment*

The rule is about length, not grammar. Both forms are correct, and the
*of* form is preferred for a possessor of more than two words or an
abstract noun.

> *the student's book* ✓ — short
> *the student's mother's book* ✓ — still fine
> *the book of the student of my mother* ✗ — past the point of readability

A note on a common learner instinct: English does **not** need a preposition
for most possessors. The accusative case is gone, and what remains is
*'s* or *of*.

> ✗ *the book **of** my mother* — understandable, and wrong
> ✓ *my mother's book*
> ✓ *the book **of** my mother* — occasionally used for emphasis, when
> the *'s* form would be clumsy

## Four things learners get wrong about there and it

**A verb after existential *there* agreeing with nothing.** ✗ *There is
many people* → ✓ *There **are** many people*.

***Its* for *it's*.** The three-letter error. Read the sentence aloud.

**A plural possessive.** ✗ *the dog**'s*** → ✓ *the dog's*; ✗ *the
student**'s*** → ✓ *the students'* when several own it.

***It's* for *its*.** ✓ *the dog wagged **its** tail.* ✗ *the dog wagged
**it's** tail.*
