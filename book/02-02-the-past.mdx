# The past

The past is the easiest tense in English and the one used wrong the most,
because a language that marks the past on the verb can tell a lot in one
word and English has to say it in several.

## Past simple

One form, made by adding *-ed* to the base, with twelve exceptions that
the whole reference section of this book exists to cover.

> *I worked.* / *She worked.* / *They worked.*
> *I went.* / *She went.* / *They went.*

Use it for four things.

**A finished past event.** The most common use, and the safest.

> I *wrote* the letter on Monday.
> She *left* at six.

**A sequence in the past.** Events in order, each finished.

> I *woke* up, *showered*, and *left*.

**A state that held in the past.**

> The shop *was* closed all week.
> He *lived* in Seville for ten years.

**A habit in the past**, usually with a stated or understood limit.

> I *smoked* for fifteen years.
> We *walked* to school every day.

<Warning>
The fourth use is where the two past tenses separate. The difference is
whether the period is finished.

> I *lived* in Seville for ten years. → I live in Berlin now. **The period
> is over.**
> I *have lived* in Seville for ten years. → I still live there. **The
> situation continues.**

Both are true English. Only one is true at the time you say it. The past
simple closes the door; the present perfect leaves it open.
</Warning>

### Regular past *-ed*

Adding *-ed* sounds like three endings, and it is three.

| after | ending | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-e* | *-d* | *liked*, *lived*, *opened* |
| a consonant | *-ed* | *worked*, *walked*, *stopped* |
| *-y* after a consonant | *-ied* | *carried*, *studied*, *tried* |

The *-y* rule is about sound, not spelling. If *-y* is pronounced /aɪ/,
keep it; if /i/, change it to *-ie-*.

> *cry* /kraɪ/ → *cried* /kraɪd/
> *play* /pleɪ/ → *played*
> *study* /ˈstʌdi/ → *studied* /ˈstʌdid/
> *carry* /ˈkæri/ → *carried* /ˈkærid/

Spelling rules cannot tell you which case you are in, because *play* and
*study* are both *-y*. Only pronunciation decides. When in doubt, say the
word out loud.

## Past continuous

**An action in progress at a past moment.** The action was unfinished
then, even though it is finished now.

> At nine o'clock I *was reading*.
> What *were you doing* when I called?

**A long action interrupted by a short one.** This is the classic use, and
it is the reason the past continuous exists.

> I *was reading* when the phone *rang*.
> She *was cooking* while I *was cleaning*.

**Two actions in progress together.**

> They *were talking* while the film *was playing*.

**The background of a story.** Sets the scene, so the short event lands.

> It *was raining*. A man *walked* in.

<Warning>
The rule that governs the interrupted pair is short and absolute.

**The long, unfinished action takes the continuous. The short, completed
action takes the simple.**

> I *was reading* when it *rang*.
> ✗ I *read* when it was ringing.
> ✓ It *rang* while I *was reading*.

This holds even when the continuous verb is stative, and even when the
simple verb is not short. *I was working all night* is continuous because
it was unfinished. *I worked all night* is simple because the night is
over.

The reverse error is common too: using the continuous for the completed
event.

> ✗ I *was going* home when the accident *was happened*.
> ✓ I *was going* home when the accident *happened*.
</Warning>

### Past continuous with *while* and *when*

The two words do almost all the work, and the difference is which action
is in the foreground.

| word | takes | example |
|---|---|---|
| *while* | the continuous, the background | *While I *was cooking*, she arrived.* |
| *when* | the simple, the foreground event | *I was cooking when she arrived.* |

Both orders work with both words, as long as the tense is right:

> *While* I *was cooking*, she arrived. / She arrived *while* I *was cooking*.
> I *was cooking when* she arrived. / She arrived *when* I *was cooking*.

The tense carries the meaning. The conjunction only carries emphasis.

## Past perfect, the earlier of two past events

**The earlier of two past events.** The past simple is too late to order
two things that both happened before now.

> When I *arrived*, the film *had already started*.
> After she *left*, nobody *spoke* to me. → ✗ After she *had left*, nobody
> *spoke* to me.

Past simple, no past perfect: the order is already clear from *after*.

> After she left, nobody spoke to me. ✓

Past perfect, past simple: the order is *not* clear, because the past
simple alone does not say which came first.

> After she had left, nobody spoke to me. ✓

This is the whole rule. Past perfect appears exactly when the sequence is
genuinely ambiguous, and never otherwise.

<Warning>
The past perfect is not "more finished" than the past simple. It is only
"earlier". A frequent error is using it to mean "long ago", which is a
meaning that does not exist in English.

> I *ate* it yesterday. — neutral, a finished event
> ✗ I *had eaten* it yesterday. — sounds like a mistake to a native ear
> ✓ I *had eaten* it by the time you arrived. — earlier, and relevant

Past perfect always needs a reference point in the past to be earlier
*than*. Without one it has nothing to be earlier than.
</Warning>

### Past perfect or past simple

| situation | tense | example |
|---|---|---|
| order is clear from a word | past simple | *After I left, she shut the door.* |
| order is not marked, and matters | past perfect | *After I had left, she shut the door.* |
| two events in a story, in order | both past simple | *He opened it. He looked inside. He screamed.* |
| later event described first | past perfect | *He screamed, having seen what was inside.* |
| an action that habitually preceded another | past simple | *Whenever I arrived, he was waiting.* |

The last row is important and rarely taught. A repeated situation from the
past takes the past simple, even though one event precedes another in each
case.

> *Whenever I arrived, he was waiting for me.* ✓
> ✗ *Whenever I had arrived, he had been waiting.*

## Narrative order

English usually tells a story forwards. When the author chooses to
backtrack, the past perfect marks the backtrack.

> The letter arrived on Monday. It *had been* written three days earlier.
> She read it twice. She *had not expected* such news.

The past perfect here is not decoration. It is the only signal the reader
has that the written date is not the arrival date. Take it out and the
sentence says the letter was written on Monday, which is a different
story.

<DeepDive>
A test that works almost every time: if you can put a *before* in front of
it, it wants the past perfect.

> *Before* the concert began, we had eaten.
> *Before* we left, I had paid the bill.

And a test for the opposite: if the sentence is a statement of fact about
the past with nothing to be earlier than, the past perfect is wrong.

> ✓ The company was founded in 1994.
> ✗ The company had been founded in 1994.

Founded in 1994 is a fact with a date. There is no earlier past event in
the sentence, so the past perfect has nothing to point back to. The same
sentence becomes correct the moment you add a second event.

> ✓ *By the time we bought the company,* it had been founded in 1994.
</DeepDive>

## The past, side by side

> I **wrote** it last week. *(finished, closed)*
> I **was writing** it last week. *(unfinished then)*

> When I **arrived**, the film **had started**. *(the film was earlier)*
> When I **arrived**, the film **started**. *(my arrival was earlier)*

> I **lived** there for ten years. *(the period is over)*
> I **have lived** there for ten years. *(it continues)*

> She **was living** in Berlin. *(temporarily, at that time)*
> She **lived** in Berlin. *(that was her home)*

## Four things learners get wrong about the past

**The *-y* spelling, decided by sound.** *study* → *studied*, *play* →
*played*. Spelling cannot tell you; pronunciation can.

**The continuous for a short event.** ✗ *I was going home when the
accident happened.* The accident is finished, so it takes the simple.

**The past perfect without a reference point.** ✗ *The company had been
founded in 1994.* There is no earlier past event to be earlier than.

**The past perfect for a habit.** ✗ *Whenever I had arrived, he had been
waiting.* Repeated past situations take the past simple.

<Warning>
The order of the two past tenses inside a sentence is fixed and never
changes: **continuous first, simple second**, when both describe the past
of the same moment.

> *was working* + *rang* ✓
> ✗ *rang* + *was working*

The continuous is the background, so it comes first, and the simple is
the event that interrupted it, so it comes second. Break this and a
listener stops following the timeline, which is why it is worth
learning as a reflex rather than a rule you look up.
</Warning>
