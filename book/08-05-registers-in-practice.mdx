# Registers in practice

Part 6 set out the theory. This chapter is four versions of the same
message, in four registers, with the reasoning shown, because the useful
skill is not knowing that a register exists but being able to move between
them deliberately.

## The same message, four ways

**The fact.** A colleague asks whether the analysis is finished.

> *It's done.*

**Neutral professional.** An email to someone you do not know well.

> *The analysis is now complete. I have attached the results and a summary
> of the main findings. Please let me know if you would like the underlying
> data as well.*

**Formal.** A letter to an external organisation.

> *I am writing to confirm that the analysis has now been completed. Please
> find attached the results together with a summary of the principal
> findings. I should be glad to provide the underlying data should you
> require it.*

**Warm and informal.** A message to a friend who asked.

> *Done! Results attached. Main thing is the second sample was dodgy, so
> I've left it out — shout if you want the raw data.*

<Warning>
The differences are not decorations. Each version makes a different set of
choices about **how much effort the reader has to make**, and choosing the
wrong one is a real problem in both directions.

The warm version says *the second sample was dodgy* and gives a reason. The
formal version does not, because in a formal letter a summary of findings
belongs in the attachment and not in the message, and adding it would be
odd rather than friendly.

The professional version offers the data. The formal version offers it more
politely. The informal one does not offer it at all, because offering
something to a friend reads as professional anxiety.

The most common mistake is sending the wrong one: a warm message to a
supplier, or a formal letter to a friend. Both are read as strange, and
neither is about the content.
</Warning>

## Four registers, four vocabularies

| | email to a stranger | work email | message to a friend | academic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| greeting | *Dear Sir or Madam* | *Hi Maria* / *Dear Maria* | *Hey* / *You* | not used |
| opening | *I am writing to...* | *Hope you're well* | *You'll never guess* | *This paper examines...* |
| request | *I would be grateful if you could...* | *Could you...?* | *Can you...?* | *We ask whether...* |
| agreement | *Thank you for your kind attention* | *Thanks, that works* | *Perfect* | *This confirms* |
| disagreement | *I am afraid I cannot agree* | *I don't think that quite holds* | *Nah* | *This claim is not supported by...* |
| sign-off | *Yours faithfully* | *Best* | *Cheers* | *References* |

**The one that catches learners: disagreement in a work email.** The
strongest-sounding form is not neutral, and a learner who writes *This is
wrong* to a colleague is making a social error that has nothing to do with
the argument.

| level | form |
|---|---|
| blunt | *This is wrong.* |
| direct but fair | *I don't think this holds.* |
| hedged | *I may be missing something, but this doesn't seem to hold.* |
| indirect | *There may be a problem with this.* → *Could you look at X
  again?* |
| deferential | *Would you mind checking this? I might have missed
  something.* |

None of these is dishonest, and the last is often the right one, because a
colleague's face is worth more than the appearance of directness.

## Formal and Latinate, and when to use it

Some words are formal because they are precise, not because they are
impolite. This is the useful distinction and it decides which you want.

| plain | formal | both used? |
|---|---|---|
| *use* | *utilise* | ✗ *utilise* is not more precise, it is just rare |
| *find out* | *ascertain* | ✓ *ascertain* is slightly more precise |
| *about* | *regarding* | ✗ *regarding* adds nothing |
| *so* | *therefore* | ✓ *therefore* is the formal connective |
| *end* | *terminate* | ✓ in law and contracts |
| *buy* | *purchase* | ✓ in contracts and formal writing |
| *before* | *prior to* | ✓ in contracts |
| *help* | *assistance* | ✓ in business English |
| *enough* | *sufficient* | ✓ in technical prose |

**The rule.** If the formal word is more precise, use it in formal writing.
If it is merely longer, do not.

*Prior to* is not more precise than *before*. *Sufficient* is more precise
than *enough*, because *enough* is a vague quantity and *sufficient* means
"as much as is required". *Regarding* is never more precise than *about*.

<DeepDive>
There is one register error that is worth naming separately, because it is
the one that makes a learner's writing consistently hard to read: the
learner has learned the formal register and uses it everywhere, including
where it is wrong.

The tell is a text with no short words. Not a single *get, put, find out,
a lot, so, but, thing* in a page. Every noun nominalised, every verb
Latinate, every connector a formal connective.

> *The implementation of the recommendation necessitated the utilisation of
> additional resources, which were subsequently allocated by the
> aforementioned committee.*

Twenty-four words, and the reader has to work for every one of them. The
same information:

> *The committee had to use more resources, and it got them.*

The reason this matters more than a grammar error is that it is not
recoverable by the reader. A misplaced *-s* is a glance. A sentence where
every word is chosen for its length is a wall, and a reader who hits a
wall stops reading, and the argument is lost rather than merely imperfect.

The test: **if a word would be the first choice of a friend speaking, use
it.** Reserve the Latinate ones for the Latinate contexts: contracts,
methodology, formal correspondence, and the places where a technical term
genuinely is the technical term.
</DeepDive>

## Slang, and when it is not slang

Some words look like slang and are perfectly standard.

| word | status |
|---|---|
| *okay* | standard, and *OK* is a little more formal |
| *email* | standard, and a verb |
| *phone* | standard as a verb, from telephone |
| *TV* | standard |
| *kids* | informal but universally understood |
| *a bit* | informal but normal in speech |
| *get* | standard, and preferred to *obtain* in almost every context |
| *big* | standard, and not a childish word |
| *thing* | standard, and overused in learner speech |

These are not slang, and a learner who avoids them in normal conversation
sounds stiff.

Actual slang, and the register that goes with it:

| word | register |
|---|---|
| *cool* | informal, and a bit dated as an adjective |
| *gonna, wanna, gotta* | speech only |
| *kinda, sorta* | speech, and informal writing |
| *stuff, things* | informal |
| *guys* | informal, and to men or mixed groups |
| *awesome* | informal, and enthusiasm, not assessment |
| *no biggie* | very informal |
| *my bad* | informal, and an apology of unequal weight |

<Warning>
A learner writing a formal email with *Thanks* and *Cheers* and *Hey* is
mixing registers, and the mixing is more jarring than being consistently
informal. Pick one and stay in it.

*Thanks* is fine in a work email. *Cheers* is fine with a colleague you
know. *Hey* is fine with someone you know well and slightly too familiar
with a stranger.

*Hey Maria, hope you're well* in a message to a supplier is the error.
*Dear Maria, I hope this finds you well* to a close colleague is also the
error, in the other direction. Both are register mistakes, and both are
about five words.
</Warning>

## Choosing, in one procedure

1. **Who is this for?** A stranger gets formal. A colleague gets
   professional. A friend gets informal.
2. **What does the reader have to do afterwards?** If they have to act, be
   precise. If they are reading for pleasure, be warm.
3. **What is the cost of a misunderstanding?** High cost gets formal and
   hedged. Low cost gets short.
4. **When in doubt, one level more formal than seems right.** Being
   slightly too formal in a message is repaired by the reader's good
   humour. Being too informal in a formal context is not repaired at all.

## Four things learners get wrong about registers in practice

**Formal everywhere.** The most damaging, and the hardest to hear in your
own writing.

**Mixing registers in one message.** *Hey* and *Dear* in the same email.

***It is important to note that* in academic writing.** Padding, and it
usually hides a claim that should be direct.

**Confusing formal with more precise.** *Prior to* is not more precise
than *before*; *utilise* is not more precise than *use*.
