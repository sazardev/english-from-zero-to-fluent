# What English is

Before learning to use English, it helps to know what kind of language it
is, because a great deal of its difficulty comes from three properties
that Spanish does not share.

## Three properties that cause the difficulty

### Fixed word order

English usually puts the subject first, the verb second and the object
third, and it keeps that order.

> **She** *writes* **letters**.

A language that allows the object first and the verb last can drop the
subject without a trace, because the ending of the verb already says who
acted. English has almost no such marking, so the subject has to be
spoken.

> *Letters she writes them.* — object first, pronoun object, no real subject

<Warning>
Reordering a sentence on the model of another language produces something
that is understandable and is not English. <i>Letters she writes them.</i>
has a subject buried in the middle, two objects and no verb agreement. The
order is the price of admission.
</Warning>

### Little inflection

A language that marks every noun for gender and number and every verb for
person, tense and mood can compress a great deal into a single word.
English marks almost nothing, and spells the difference out in separate
words instead.

| what is marked | marked on the word | marked in English |
|---|---|---|
| one or many | *one book*, *books* | *a book*, *two books* |
| who acted | *she writes* | *she* + *writes* |
| who or which | *the man that I met* | *the man* + *that* + *I met* |
| male or female | *the table* | *the table* — no marking at all |
| possessive | *my book* | *my* + *book*, as separate words |

<DeepDive>
English lost its case endings roughly a thousand years ago. What survived
is the plural *-s*, the third person *-s*, and the past *-ed*. Everything
else that Latin had, English threw away.
<br><br>
The consequence for you is that information another language carries in
its endings, English carries in word order and in the choice of
preposition instead. So the work moves from learning endings to learning
order, and from gender to articles and prepositions.
</DeepDive>

### The verb carries the time

A language can be verb-heavy: a whole clause collapses into one verb form.
English is noun-heavy and has almost no synthetic verb forms, so the same
information has to be spelled out with auxiliary verbs and extra words.

| meaning | compressed | English, expanded |
|---|---|---|
| a future arrangement | I am going to go. | I am + going + to + go |
| a completed action | I have eaten. | I + have + eaten |
| a state before a past event | I had been living there for a year. | I + had + been + living |
| a passive action | The room was cleaned. | The room + was + cleaned |

<DeepDive>
English can put an entire sentence into a single verb form. English needs
four words to do the same work, which is why English feels repetitive:
<i>will</i>, <i>going to</i>, <i>has</i>, <i>have</i> and <i>had</i> are
all over the place and none of them can be avoided.
<br><br>
The workaround is worth learning early, because it buys you back
everything the lost endings took away: contraction, relative clauses,
and omitting words you have already said.
</DeepDive>

## The structure of an English sentence

### The two basic patterns

Most English sentences are one of these.

| pattern | form | example |
|---|---|---|
| subject + verb | intransitive | Birds fly. |
| subject + verb + object | transitive | She writes letters. |
| subject + verb + indirect + direct | ditransitive | She gave me the letter. |
| subject + verb + complement | copular | He is a teacher. |

<Warning>
Verbs that take no object cannot be given one. <i>She arrived home</i> is
wrong because <i>arrive</i> is intransitive; <i>she arrived to home</i> is
worse. And <i>live</i> takes a preposition, not an object:
<i>she lives in Madrid</i>, not <i>she lives Madrid</i>.
</Warning>

### The parts of a sentence

Every English sentence, however long, is made of these.

| part | question it answers | example |
|---|---|---|
| subject | who or what does it | *The book* fell. |
| verb | what happened | *fell* |
| object | what or whom | *She dropped **it***. |
| indirect object | to whom | *She gave **me** the letter.* |
| direct object | what | *She gave me **the letter**.* |
| complement | what the subject is | *He is **a teacher**.* |
| adjunct | when, where, how, why | *It fell **yesterday**.* |

The subject is the part that can be replaced by *it* or *she* without
changing the meaning, and finding it is the first step in every grammar
question.

## Word classes

Every English word is one of eight things, and knowing which is most of
the work of reading a dictionary entry.

| class | what it does | examples |
|---|---|---|
| noun | names a thing | book, London, kindness |
| pronoun | replaces a noun | I, you, he, it, they, this, who |
| verb | names an action or a state | go, think, be, become |
| adjective | describes a noun | tall, red, interesting |
| adverb | describes a verb, an adjective or a whole clause | quickly, very, then |
| determiner | introduces a noun | the, a, my, some, this |
| preposition | relates a noun to something else | in, on, at, to, of |
| conjunction | joins | and, but, because, although |

<DeepDive>
The hard pair is adjective and adverb, and it is hard because English
allows many adverbs to modify adjectives: <i>very tall</i>,
<i>quite interesting</i>, <i>a little tired</i>, <i>deeply worried</i>.
Most other languages do not allow this, and learners get <i>very
interestingly</i> instead of <i>very interesting</i>.
<br><br>
And English is full of words that end in <i>-ly</i> and are not adverbs
(<i>family</i>, <i>only</i>, <i>early</i>, <i>lovely</i>), and adverbs that
do not (<i>fast</i>, <i>hard</i>, <i>late</i>, <i>straight</i>). Never guess
from the ending.
</DeepDive>

## The two verbs that build everything

Almost every English sentence contains a form of *be* or a form of *have*.
They are the scaffolding.

| | *be* | *have* |
|---|---|---|
| identity, state, description | He **is** tired. | He **is** hungry. |
| possession | — | He **has** a car. |
| passive | He **is** beaten. | — |
| perfect | He **has** beaten me. | — |
| continuous | He **is** beating me. | — |
| condition | If he **is** late, ... | If he **has** time, ... |

## How a sentence is negative and how it is a question

English builds negatives and questions with the same two auxiliaries, and
everything else stays in place.

| | statement | negative | question |
|---|---|---|---|
| present | She works. | She **does not** work. | **Does** she work? |
| past | She worked. | She **did not** work. | **Did** she work? |
| future | She will work. | She **will not** work. | **Will** she work? |
| modal | She can work. | She **cannot** work. | **Can** she work? |

<Warning>
The key is that the main verb does not change after <i>do</i> or
<i>does</i>. <i>She doesn't works</i> and <i>Does she works</i> are both
wrong. The auxiliary carries the tense and the main verb goes back to its
base form.
</Warning>

## What this means for you

Three habits follow from the three properties, and they are the habits
that separate someone who improves from someone who plateaus.

| habit | why |
|---|---|
| learn word order, not endings | English has almost no endings to learn |
| expect articles everywhere | English marks what Spanish marks with gender and case |
| expect the verb to be explicit | English has no synthetic past or future to hide behind |

<DeepDive>
Everything in this book follows from those three facts. The verb chapters
are long because English has almost no conjugation, so all the work is in
choosing which auxiliary and which form. The article chapters exist
because English compensates for lost case with articles. The clause
chapters exist because word order carries the relationships that Spanish
marks in endings.
<br><br>
Once these three are internalised, the rest of English is vocabulary, and
vocabulary is the easy half.
</DeepDive>
