# Your tools

Reading a book about English will not make you speak English. This chapter
is about the four things that do, and how to set each one up in the first
half hour so that the rest of the book can be used the moment you finish it.

## The four tools

| tool | what it does | why it is not optional |
|---|---|---|
| a dictionary | you look words up | nothing else replaces it |
| a spaced repetition deck | you review at the right moment | forgetting is the default |
| a voice recorder | you hear yourself | you cannot hear your own accent |
| a correction log | you record your own errors | this is the one nobody does |

## The dictionary

Any good one. What matters is learning to use it well, because most
learners use a dictionary as a translation machine and get the worst
possible return from it.

**Look up the definition last.** Read the example sentences first. The
example is English being used, and the definition is English being
described, and the first teaches you more.

> **looked up** *to hold something so that you can examine it*
> 1. I *looked up* the definition in a dictionary. 2. She *looked up* at
> the sky. 3. This word *looks up* nicely in a phrase.

Sentence 2 has nothing to do with dictionaries. That is the point. A word
has a range, and a single definition hides the range. The examples show
you where in that range you are.

**Read the whole entry, not the first line.** Dictionaries list the most
common meaning first, which is usually right and occasionally not.

> **run** *to move quickly on foot*
> ... also: to manage a company; to operate a machine; to flow; a
> continuous series.

*The water runs through the pipes* and *she runs a company* are the same
verb. Knowing that is worth more than three new words.

**Note the labels.** *countable*, *uncountable*, *transitive*,
*intransitive*, *formal*, *informal*, *slang*. They tell you the rules
that go with the word, and the labels are the dictionary being honest with
you about how the word behaves.

<Warning>
The habit that costs the most and returns the least is looking a word up
the instant you meet it. It feels productive and it teaches you almost
nothing, because a word looked up in isolation is a word with no
connections.

Read past it, guess from context, and look it up after you have finished
the page. The guessing is where the learning happens, because guessing
trains the skill you actually need, which is inferring meaning from
context. And the second time you meet the word, it is in a sentence you
have already read, so it arrives attached to something.

A useful rule: **no more than five lookups per page.** Five is a lot. A
page of a graded reader has maybe three words you do not know, and those
three are the page's real lesson.
</Warning>

## The deck

The flashcards in part 9 are generated from an Anki collection of 5,471
notes, and the point of them is not the vocabulary. It is the schedule.

**The schedule is the whole method.** A learner who reviews a word once a
week forgets it. A learner who reviews it on the day it is about to be
forgotten keeps it, and the effort per word is a fraction. That is the
finding behind every spaced repetition system ever built, and it is not
close.

**Start small.** Twenty cards a day, every day, beats two hundred on a
Sunday. The reason is that the thing you are building is a habit, and a
habit is built by frequency, not by volume.

**Do not add cards you did not fail at.** A card you add from a
dictionary while reading is a card you will not review tomorrow, because
you have not failed at it yet. Add cards from your correction log, and
only from there. That is why the log comes before the deck in this list.

## The recorder

Your ear cannot hear your own voice. This is not a figure of speech: the
vocal tract that produces your speech is connected to your ears by bone
and tissue, and it changes what they hear. Every speaker of every language
sounds different to themselves.

The consequence is that you cannot learn to pronounce by listening to
yourself, and you cannot learn to speak English by reading about it.

**The drill.** Read a page aloud. Record it. Play it back. Do it again,
and this time fix one thing.

Not nine things. One. The one you just heard. Ten recordings later you
will have fixed ten things, and the tenth recording will be unrecognisable
from the first.

**Three recordings per week, five minutes each.** That is the entire
requirement, and it is the single highest-value fifteen minutes in this
book.

<Warning>
The most common way to fail at pronunciation is to record yourself once,
hear the difference, conclude that you cannot do it, and stop. One
recording shows you a gap. Ten recordings show you a trajectory, and the
trajectory is what tells you whether the practice works.

Another way to fail is to record yourself reading something you have read a
hundred times, which is why fluent reading sounds fine and is no evidence
at all. Record something you have never read before. The unfamiliarity is
what makes the recording diagnostic.
</Warning>

## The correction log

One file. Every error you make, written down.

> I have been lived here.
> → no passive of "live" → errors/01.txt

**Write the error exactly as you wrote it.** Not the corrected version. The
value is in seeing your own sentence, and a corrected version in the log
teaches you the correction but not the mistake.

**Write what the rule is, in one line.**

> I have been lived here.
> → no passive of "live": live has no passive form
> → correction/02-05.mdx, the passive chapter

**Review it weekly.** Most learners have between three and five errors that
account for the majority of their mistakes. Those five are worth more than
the entire grammar section of this book, and you cannot find them without a
log.

<DeepDive>
There is a reason the log is the tool nobody uses, and it is worth naming,
because the reason is a real cognitive fact rather than laziness.

A mistake you have noticed is a mistake you will make again. Writing it
down feels redundant, because you are already aware of it. The
redundancy is the point: the act of writing forces you to state the rule,
and stating a rule is a different memory from recognising a violation.

Recognition and production are different skills. You can recognise a
broken sentence easily, and produce a correct one is much harder, and the
gap between them is where most learners' errors live. The log works
because it forces production.

The second reason is quieter. A mistake you have noticed feels small,
because noticing it is a kind of achievement. Writing it in a file where it
sits in a list with four other mistakes you made this week is not an
achievement at all, and it is the only way the number becomes visible.
Once you can see that you have made the same article error forty times,
the motivation to fix it no longer comes from willpower.
</DeepDive>

## Setting it up now

Thirty minutes, once, and then the tools are there for the rest of the
book.

| minutes | what to do |
|---|---|
| 5 | install a dictionary you like and learn where the examples are |
| 5 | create the deck, and import or build the first twenty cards |
| 5 | create the correction log as an empty file named after today |
| 5 | install a recorder, and make your first recording |
| 10 | read page one of part 1 aloud, record it, and play it back |

The last row is the one that matters. The other four are setup. The fifth
is the practice, and it is the same practice for the next thousand pages.
