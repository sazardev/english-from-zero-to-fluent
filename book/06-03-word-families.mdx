# Word families

A word family is one root and the words built from it. Learning the family
rather than the word is the difference between a vocabulary you can use and
a vocabulary you can recognise.

## The families that matter most

| root | noun | verb | adjective | adverb |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *educate* | *education, educator* | *educate* | *educational* | *educationally* |
| *create* | *creation, creator* | *create* | *creative, creative* | *creatively* |
| *produce* | *production, producer* | *produce* | *productive* | *productively* |
| *describe* | *description* | *describe* | *descriptive* | *descriptively* |
| *analyse* | *analysis, analyst* | *analyse* | *analytical* | *analytically* |
| *explain* | *explanation* | *explain* | *explanatory* | — |
| *predict* | *prediction* | *predict* | *predictable* | — |
| *compete* | *competition, competitor* | *compete* | *competitive* | *competitively* |
| *decide* | *decision* | *decide* | *decisive* | *decisively* |
| *succeed* | *success* | *succeed* | *successful* | *successfully* |
| *strengthen* | *strength* | *strengthen* | *strong* | *strongly* |
| *differ* | *difference* | *differ* | *different* | *differently* |
| *depend* | *dependence* | *depend* | *dependent* | — |
| *concentrate* | *concentration* | *concentrate* | *concentrated* | — |

| *qualify* | *qualification* | *qualify* | *qualified* | — |
| *identify* | *identification* | *identify* | *identifiable* | — |
| *justify* | *justification* | *justify* | *justifiable* | — |
| *signify* | *significance* | *signify* | *significant* | — |

<Warning>
The pair that catches learners out most often is **noun and adjective of
the same root**, because they often have different meanings and sometimes
opposite ones.

> *a **strong** argument* — powerful
> *a **strength** — power, a muscle
> *a **strong** smell* — intense
> *a **strength** of smell* — a degree, and this is a different word from
> the first *strength*

> *an **economical** car* — cheap to run
> *an **economy*** — the system, or thrift
> ✗ *an economical car* means cheap, not cheap to buy, and the two are
> different cars at different prices

> *a **historic** building* — old and important
> *a **historical** novel* — set in the past
> a **classic** building — of recognised quality
> ✗ *a **classic** novel* — established, and not necessarily set in the
> past

> *a **classic** error* — typical
> *a **classical** error* — ✗, and this pair is inverted often enough to
> be worth memorising
> a **classic** record — an album everyone knows
> a **classical** record — music of that tradition

The *historic* and *historical* pair is a real trap: in ordinary use
*historic* is the one that means "important in history", and *historical*
means "connected with history, possibly imaginary". The film is
*historic*; the drama about it is *historical*.
</Warning>

## The four suffixes that hide the hardest distinctions

**-*ment*.** The noun. Almost always a process, an action, or a result.

> *move* → *movement*
> *replace* → *replacement*
> *require* → *requirement*
> *agree* → *agreement*
> *develop* → *development*
> *invest* → *investment*
> *measure* → *measurement*

**-*-tion*, -*sion*.** Also a process, usually a formal one.

> *apply* → *application*
> *explain* → *explanation*
> *decide* → *decision*
> *divide* → *division*
> *conclude* → *conclusion*
> *admit* → *admission*
> *permit* → *permission*

**-*-ance*, -*ence*, *-ancy*, -*ency*.** A state, and the two spellings are
not interchangeable: one family takes *-ance*, the other *-ence*, and which
is which is fixed per word.

> *appear* → *appearance*
> *rely* → *reliance*
> *accept* → *acceptance*
> *differ* → *difference*
> *refer* → *reference*
> *prefer* → *preference*
> *occur* → *occurrence*

**-*-ity*, -*ty*.** A quality, and these are the ones learners over-use,
because *-ity* makes a noun out of almost any adjective.

> *able* → *ability*
> *possible* → *possibility*
> *responsible* → *responsibility*
> *able* → *capability*
> *visible* → *visibility*
> *honest* → *honesty* — no *-ity*
> *pure* → *purity*
> *dense* → *density*
> *brief* → *brevity*
> *novel* → *novelty*
> *valid* → *validity*
> *obvious* → *obviousness*

<DeepDive>
A useful fact about word families, and the reason they are worth learning
as families: **the four forms are not interchangeable, and the noun is
usually the most useful and the least used in speech.**

| form | register | example |
|---|---|---|
| verb | everyday | *The results **show** a clear pattern.* |
| adjective | everyday | *a **clear** pattern* |
| noun, *-ion* | formal, academic | *the **clarity** of the pattern* |
| nominalisation | very formal | *The **clarification** of the pattern.* |

The sequence from verb to nominalisation is a sequence of increasing
formality and decreasing clarity, and it is the most reliable description
of the difference between plain and academic English that exists.

The advice that follows is not "avoid *-ion*" but "use the verb when the
verb is available". *The results show a clear pattern* is a sentence anyone
can follow. *The results demonstrate the clarity of the pattern* is a
sentence everyone can parse and nobody enjoys. The information is identical
and the second one costs the reader more.

A quick test for nominalisation: if the sentence's main verb is *is*, *was*
or *were*, look for a verb hiding in a noun and put it back.
</DeepDive>

## Opposites and the pairs that are not symmetrical

Some families contain pairs whose members are not logical opposites, and
the asymmetry is the interesting part.

| word | apparently opposite | actually |
|---|---|---|
| *borrow* | *lend* | not opposites: you borrow, someone else lends |
| *lend* | *borrow* | same, in reverse |
| *alike* | *unlike* | *alike* has no *un-* form in normal use |
| *unaware* | *aware* | ✓ symmetrical |
| *unaffected* | *affected* | ✓ |
| *unemployed* | *employed* | ✓, and a job category |
| *unethical* | *ethical* | ✓ |
| *unequal* | *equal* | ✓ |
| *disagree* | *agree* | ✓ |
| *disappear* | *appear* | ✓ |
| *unveil* | *veil* | the *un-* verb reverses the action, as part 3 explains |
| *untie* | *tie* | ✓ and it means to open |
| *unpack* | *pack* | ✓ and it means to open |
| *invaluable* | *valuable* | ✗ **not opposites at all** |
| *regardless* | *regard* | ✗ unrelated |
| *before* | *after* | ✓ |

<Warning>
Three false friends in English that a learner will meet in the first
hundred pages, all of them formed from a real word:

**Invaluable** means *extremely valuable*, not *worthless*. The *in-* here
is emphatic, like *inexhaustible* and *incredible*.

> *Her advice was **invaluable**.* — she was extremely helpful
> ✗ *Her advice was invaluable* — understood as "worth nothing"

**Regardless** means *whatever happens*, not *disregarding*. *Of* is
wrong.

> *Sales rose **regardless of** the weather.*
> ✗ *Regardless the weather, sales rose.* — needs *of*
> ✗ *Regardless of the weather* — ✓ this is right; the error is dropping
> *of*

**Indisputable** means *cannot be disputed*, i.e. it strengthens rather than
negates, and the same goes for *inevitable, interminable, immaculate,
impeccable*.

The pattern: *in-* before *-evitable, -disputable, -exhaustible,
-credible, -defensible, -excusable* all intensify. Everywhere else *in-* is
a negation. Once you notice the list, you will not confuse it again.
</Warning>

## Four things learners get wrong about word families

**Using the noun where the verb belongs.** *The clarification of the
pattern* → *The results clarify the pattern.*

**Assuming a word family is symmetrical.** *Invaluable* is not the opposite
of *valuable*. *Borrow* is not the opposite of *lend*.

***-ance* and *-ence* mixed up.** *appearance* not *appearence*;
*difference* not *differance*.

**Adding *-ity* to every adjective.** *honesty*, not *honestity*.
