# The errors that cost the most

Fourteen errors account for the majority of marks lost in learner writing.
This chapter lists them by frequency, with the correction, and it is the
one chapter in part 8 to keep open while you write.

The ordering is by how often they cost marks, not by how important they
are.

## 1. The missing third person *-s*

The commonest error in learner English at every level above beginner.

> ✗ *She **write** every day.*
> ✓ *She **writes** every day.*
> ✗ *He **don't** work on Sundays.*
> ✓ *He **doesn't work** on Sundays.*
> ✗ *It **rain** in November.*
> ✓ ***It rains** in November.*

Three forms, one rule: **the *-s* goes on the first auxiliary and nowhere
else.**

## 2. The present perfect with a finished time

> ✗ *I have **seen** him **yesterday**.*
> ✓ ***I saw him** yesterday.*
> ✗ *I have **finished** it **last week**.*
> ✓ ***I finished** it last week.*

Part 2 covers this. The rule is one sentence: **name a finished time and
the past simple is the only option.**

## 3. The past perfect without a reference point

> ✗ *The company **had been** founded in 1994.*
> ✓ ***The company was** founded in 1994.*
> ✗ ***After I had left**, she shut the door.*
> ✓ ***After I left**, she shut the door.*

Two different errors. The first is a past perfect with nothing to be
earlier than. The second is a past perfect where a word already fixes the
order.

## 4. *-ing* after a verb that wants *to*

> ✗ *I enjoy **to read**.*
> ✓ *I enjoy **reading**.*
> ✗ *I suggest **to go** instead.*
> ✓ *I suggest **going** instead.*
> ✗ *He denied **to steal** it.*
> ✓ *He denied **stealing** it.*

Part 3 has the two lists. They do not overlap, and the *-ing* verbs are the
ones that surprise people.

## 5. A preposition added to a transitive verb

> ✗ *We discussed **about** the plan.*
> ✓ *We discussed **the plan**.*
> ✗ *I contacted **with** her.*
> ✓ ***I contacted** her.*
> ✗ *Could you explain **me** how it works?*
> ✓ *Could you explain **how it works**?* / *...explain **it** to me?*

## 6. A preposition invented from the noun

> ✗ *It depends **of** the answer.*
> ✓ *It depends **on** the answer.*
> ✗ *It consists **in** three parts.*
> ✓ *It consists **of** three parts.*
> ✗ *I insisted **in** paying.*
> ✓ *I insisted **on** paying.*

Part 3 has the verbs. There is no way to derive these, which is the whole
point of learning them as whole verbs.

## 7. A plural apostrophe on a singular noun

> ✗ ***the dog's*** (alone)
> ✓ ***the dog's** tail*
> ✗ ***the dog's** and the cats*
> ✓ ***the dog** and the cat*

## 8. *Its* for *it's*

> ✗ *Its raining.*
> ✓ ***It's** raining.*
> ✗ ***Its** a nice day.*
> ✓ ***It's** a nice day.*
> ✓ ***the dog wagged its** tail.* — and this one is correct

Read the sentence aloud. Two of these three are wrong, and the third is
right, and the difference is one apostrophe.

## 9. A comparative with *than* for a comparative with *as*

> ✗ ***more** quieter*
> ✗ *She is **more** taller than her sister.*
> ✓ *She is **much** taller than her sister.*
> ✗ *My results are **better than** last year.* → ✓ *...**better than** they
> were last year.* — you need two things to compare
> ✓ *My results are **better than** last year's* — and *last year's* is
> acceptable

## 10. The comparative *-er* with a long word

> ✗ ***more** beautifuler*
> ✓ ***more** beautiful*
> ✗ *more difficulter* → ✓ *more difficult*

Short words take *-er*, long words take *more*, and no word takes both.

## 11. A dangling participle

> ✗ ***Walking** to the station, **her keys** dropped.*
> ✓ ***Walking** to the station, **she** dropped her keys.*
> ✗ *Sitting on the grass, **the picnic** was enjoyable.*
> ✓ *Sitting on the grass, **we** enjoyed the picnic.*

The subject of the participle must be the subject of the main clause.

## 12. Nominalisation hiding the actor

> ✗ ***The implementation of** the new policy **was met with** resistance.*
> ✓ ***Staff resisted** the new policy.*

## 13. *Although* and a clause, *despite* and a noun

> ✗ ***Despite** it was late, we left.*
> ✓ ***Even though** it was late, we left.*
> ✓ ***Despite** the lateness, we left.*
> ✗ ***Despite of** the rain, we left.*
> ✓ ***Despite** the rain, we left.*

## 14. Two coordinators

> ✗ *I was tired **but however** I went to bed.*
> ✓ *I was tired, **however** I went to bed.*
> ✗ ***Although** the film was long, **but** I enjoyed it.*
> ✓ ***Although** the film was long, I enjoyed it.* / *The film was long,
> **but** I enjoyed it.*

<Warning>
Five of the fourteen are the same underlying mistake in different clothes,
and finding that out is the most useful thing in this chapter.

**Errors 1, 5 and 6 are all about the same thing:** the learner is placing
or omitting a grammatical element according to a rule about the wrong
thing. The *-s* is placed according to "verbs take *-s* in the third
person" rather than "the first auxiliary takes *-s*". The preposition is
chosen by the noun's meaning rather than by the verb.

**Errors 2 and 3 are the same thing:** the perfect tense used for a
finished past event. Both come from treating the perfect as vaguer and
older than the simple, when it is newer and closer.

**Errors 7 and 8 are the same thing:** the apostrophe placed by what feels
right rather than by what is missing.

**Errors 9 and 10 are the same thing:** comparative forms mixed without
checking which pattern applies.

So the fourteen reduce to about six ideas, and the six ideas are in parts 2,
3, 4 and 5. If you fix the ideas, the fourteen disappear. If you memorise
the fourteen, you will get them back wrong in new sentences.
</Warning>

## The proofreading order

Proofreading in the wrong order means fixing the easy errors and missing the
hard ones. This order works.

1. **Content.** Does it say anything? Read only the topic sentences, from
   part 8.2.
2. **Structure.** One idea per paragraph, and each paragraph has a topic
   sentence.
3. **Tenses.** Go through every verb and ask what the time relation is.
   This is where errors 2 and 3 live, and it needs a deliberate pass.
4. **Articles.** Every noun that needs one. Errors 7 and 8 live here.
5. **Verbs.** The *-s*, and the *-ing* against *to* choice.
6. **Prepositions.** The last pass, because it needs the most thought and
   it is the easiest to get wrong when tired.
7. **Punctuation and spelling.**

The order matters because a sentence with the wrong tense can also have the
wrong article, and fixing the article first means doing the work twice.

## Building your own list

The fourteen in this chapter are the ones every learner makes. The ones
**you** make are in your correction log, and they are worth more than this
list, because these are general and those are specific to you.

> I have been lived here.
> → no passive of "live": live has no passive form
> → book/02-05-the-passive.mdx

Five entries, and they are the whole of what you need to review. The
general errors in this chapter will be caught by the same habit.

<DeepDive>
The reason proofreading in the right order works, and the reason the
opposite order does not, is that proofreading is a search, and a search
finds what is easy to find.

Reading for *-s* and spelling finds errors 1, 7 and 8 quickly, because
they are visible on the surface. Reading for tense has to reconstruct the
time relation of the sentence, which is a slower and more deliberate
operation, and if you have already spent your attention on the surface the
deliberate pass never happens.

This produces a familiar and sad pattern. A learner proofreads carefully,
fixes nine spelling errors, submits, and gets the same three grammar errors
marked that they have been getting for six weeks. Nothing about the
grammar has changed and nothing about the proofreading has changed either,
so the same nine get fixed and the same three get missed.

The fix is boring and it works: go through the document more than once,
each time looking for one thing. Four passes of five minutes beat one pass
of twenty, because one pass of twenty has a single search strategy and four
passes have four.

The second thing worth saying is that the errors in this chapter are
**priority order by how often they cost marks**, and this is not the same as
severity. A dangling participle is a worse sentence than a missing *-s*,
and it is *less* frequent, and it is number 11. A missing *-s* is a
trivial fix and it is number 1. If you have limited time, fix in this
order and not by how bad the error feels.
</DeepDive>

## Four things learners get wrong about errors

**Fixing the easy ones first.** Spelling before tense, every time.

**Memorising a list of errors rather than the ideas behind them.** Fourteen
errors, six ideas.

**Believing a repeated error is a memory problem.** If you have written *I
have seen him yesterday* twice, it is not forgetting; the rule is wrong in
your head.

**Not writing them down.** A mistake noticed is a mistake repeated. Part 0
covers why the log is the tool that works.
