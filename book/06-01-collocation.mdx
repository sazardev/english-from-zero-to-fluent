# Collocations

A collocation is a combination of words that goes together in English and
not in other languages. It is not a phrase you translate; it is a phrase
that exists, like an atom or a chord, and knowing the atoms gets you
closer to sounding right than knowing a thousand isolated words.

## Why it matters more than vocabulary

A learner who knows a word and uses it in the wrong company is understood,
and sounds foreign. A learner who knows a collocation sounds native in
that sentence, even if their vocabulary is small.

> *make* a decision ✓ / *do* a decision ✗
> *take* a decision ✓ / *make* a decision ✓ — both, but *take* is formal
> ✗ *do* a decision

> *pay* attention ✓
> ✗ *give* attention ✗ *do* attention

> *do* research ✓
> ✗ *make* research ✗ *do* a research

> *keep* a promise
> *break* a promise
> *keep* a secret
> *break* a secret
> *break* the news
> *break* a habit
> *break* a record
> *break* a leg

> *break* the rules, *break* a window, *break* an arm, *break* down,
> *break* in, *break* out, *break* through

And break is a rare case. **About half of the most common English verbs
only make sense with particular nouns**, and a learner who assembles
sentences word by word gets them wrong constantly without knowing why.

<Warning>
The error is invisible to the learner and obvious to a speaker, and it is
the one that makes writing look translated even when every word is
correct.

> ✗ *I did a mistake on the exam.*
> ✓ ***I made** a mistake on the exam.*

> ✗ *She gave an interesting suggestion.*
> ✓ ***She made** an interesting suggestion.*

> ✗ *He did a big effort.*
> ✓ ***He made** a big effort.*

> ✗ *I will do a decision after the meeting.*
> ✓ ***I will make** a decision after the meeting.*

*Do* is the workhorse verb of English and it collocates with a small set of
nouns: *work, business, homework, a favour, a mistake, research, the
laundry, the dishes*. Everything else takes a different verb, and the verb
is not guessable from the noun. There is no reason *make* goes with
*mistake* and not *error*. It is a fact about the language and it has to
be learned.
</Warning>

## Verb and noun

The most productive pattern, and the one to learn first.

| verb | nouns it takes |
|---|---|
| *make* | a decision, a mistake, an effort, progress, a difference, a suggestion, a point, a profit, a request, a speech, a reservation |
| *do* | work, business, homework, research, a favour, the laundry, the dishes, a good turn, one's best |
| *take* | a decision, a risk, a chance, a place, a break, a nap, a shower, medicine, responsibility, action, notes |
| *give* | advice, information, permission, a chance, a speech, a presentation, thanks, a hand, a message |
| *have* | a look, a bath, a break, a meeting, a party, a problem, a reason, a chance, an effect, a headache |
| *pay* | attention, a visit, a bill, a price, a fine, a compliment, a debt |
| *hold* | a meeting, a conversation, a party, a record, a grudge, an opinion, a conversation |
| *keep* | a promise, a secret, a habit, a diary, a record, calm, control, track |
| *put* | an end to, a stop to, pressure on, a question to, off, up, down, on |
| *get* | a job, a degree, a result, a grip, the hang of, a run at, away with, rid of |

The way to use this table is as a *check*, not a vocabulary. When you have
written a sentence with one of these verbs, ask whether the noun is the one
it takes. If not, the verb is wrong and the sentence is wrong even though
every word is a real word.

## Adjective and noun

| adjective | nouns |
|---|---|
| *strong* | argument, case, evidence, reaction, impact, accent, smell, taste |
| *weak* | argument, case, evidence, moment, point, link, spot |
| *deep* | sleep, breath, thought, concern, damage, impression, roots, shade |
| *heavy* | rain, traffic, smoker, drinker, losses, damage, weight, fog |
| *close* | friend, relative, contact, connection, match, call, race, chapter |
| *key* | figure, role, factor, point, element, word, issue |
| *common* | ground, sense, practice, knowledge, thread, denominator |
| *general* | public, practice, rule, principle, outline, terms |

*Deep sleep* and *strong argument* are not rules; they are facts. But they
are learnable, and a learner who collects them produces writing that reads
like English rather than like a dictionary.

## Adverb and adjective

Some adverbs are conventional partners of particular adjectives, and the
wrong one is noticeable.

> *deeply**sorry***, *deeply**moved***, *deeply**regret***
> ✗ *very sorry* — acceptable, and *deeply* is more emphatic
> *hardly**aware***, *barely**aware***, *hardly**recognisable***
> *highly**skilled***, *highly**unusual***
> *strongly**opposed***, *widely**known***, *wrongly**accused***
> *poorly**defined***, *poorly**made***
> *newly**built***, *newly**elected***
> *highly**aware***, *well**aware***
> *openly**critical***, *overtly**critical***, *clearly**visible***

<DeepDive>
Two things explain why collocations are hard to learn and why the effort is
still worth making.

**The partner is not a rule, it is a fact.** There is no way to reason from
*decision* to *make*. A language that marked this with a prefix, or that
used a different word entirely, would be learnable by rule, and English
does not, because *make* and *do* are both old Germanic general-purpose
verbs that absorbed different sets of jobs and kept them.

**The pairs are semantically arbitrary but often gradient.** *Make a
decision* and *take a decision* both work, and the difference is register:
*make* is neutral, *take* is slightly more formal and more deliberate.
*Make a mistake* and *do a mistake* — only *make* works. *Make progress*
and *do progress* — only *make* works. So some pairs are a choice and some
are a fact, and you cannot tell which from the noun alone.

The practical method is not to learn lists but to notice them in reading,
and to add the whole chunk rather than the single word. *Make a decision* is
one item for memory. *Decide* is another item. Learning *decision* on its
own leaves you with no way to use it, because in English the noun almost
never travels without its verb.
</DeepDive>

## Verb and preposition

Part 3 covered this. It belongs here too, because the most frequent
collocation errors are verb-plus-preposition phrases, and the preposition
is chosen by the verb.

> *depend on*, *rely on*, *concentrate on*, *insist on*
> *consist of*, *result in*, *lead to*, *contribute to*
> *apologise for*, *account for*, *blame for*
> *believe in*, *belong to*, *refer to*, *object to*

## Adjective and preposition

The same phenomenon with adjectives, and the four most reversed pairs are
in part 3.

> *good **at***, *good **with***, *good **for***, *good **to***
> *interested **in***, *responsible **for***, *famous **for***,
> *similar **to***, *different **from***

## Register pairs

Some collocations are formal and some are not, and using a formal one in
casual writing is as noticeable as the wrong noun.

| formal | neutral | informal |
|---|---|---|
| *reside in* | *live in* | *live at* |
| *purchase* | *buy* | *get* |
| *request* | *ask for* | *want* |
| *assistance* | *help* | *a hand* |
| *terminate* | *end* | *stop* |
| *commence* | *start* | *begin, kick off* |
| *sufficient* | *enough* | *plenty* |
| *approximately* | *about* | *around* |
| *endeavour* | *try* | *have a go* |
| *individuals* | *people* | *folks* |
| *obtain* | *get* | *pick up* |

## Four things learners get wrong about collocation

**The wrong verb for the noun.** ✗ *do a mistake* → ✓ *make a mistake*.
*Make* and *do* are the worst offenders.

**Translating a noun phrase whole.** The chunk is a unit, so learn the
chunk. *Make a decision*, not *decision*.

**A formal collocation in casual writing.** *Reside in* in a message to a
friend reads as stiff, and it is as wrong as a grammar error in the other
direction.

**A preposition chosen from the noun.** → *depend on*, not *depend of*.
The verb chooses.
