# Adjectives and adverbs

An adjective describes a noun or a pronoun. An adverb describes a verb, an
adjective or another adverb. The division is not hard, and the places it
goes wrong are worth learning because they are all in the same four
categories.

## What each one modifies

| word | modifies | examples |
|---|---|---|
| adjective | a noun, a pronoun | *a **red** car*, *she seems **tired*** |
| adverb | a verb | *he **quickly** left* |
| adverb | an adjective | *a **very** good idea* |
| adverb | another adverb | *he ran **very** quickly* |
| adverb | a whole sentence | ***Unfortunately**, we were late.* |
| adverb | a prepositional phrase | *she spoke **quietly** in the library.* |

## Adjectives: order

When several adjectives modify one noun, English has a fixed order.

| position | category | examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | opinion | *lovely, terrible, wonderful* |
| 2 | size | *big, small, huge* |
| 3 | age | *old, new, ancient* |
| 4 | shape | *round, square, triangular* |
| 5 | colour | *red, blue, pale* |
| 6 | origin | *French, Japanese, Arctic* |
| 7 | material | *wooden, silk, iron* |
| 8 | purpose | *walking, running, sleeping* |

> *a **lovely big old square red French wooden walking stick***

Read it and the order holds. Reverse any pair and it is wrong.

> ✗ *a red lovely stick* → ✓ *a lovely red stick*
> ✗ *a wooden French stick* → ✓ *a French wooden stick*

<Warning>
The order is not optional in English, and it is checked by more speakers
than you would expect. Native speakers feel a wrong order immediately,
the way they feel a wrong word, even when they cannot explain why.

The four orders learners get wrong most:

> ✗ *a car **red big*** → ✓ *a big red car*
> ✗ *a **wooden French** chair* → ✓ *a **French wooden** chair*
> ✗ *a **new Italian** restaurant* → ✓ *an **Italian new** restaurant* → ✓
> *a new Italian restaurant*, because *new* is opinion-adjacent
> ✗ *a **blue dark** room* → ✓ *a dark blue room*

When in doubt, use one adjective. A noun modified by two adjectives is
already marked, and the order is a small price for not being marked.
</Warning>

## Adjectives that come before the noun

Most adjectives can go either way. A small group cannot, and using them
after the noun is a real error.

> *the **only** book that matters* / ✗ *the book that matters **only***
> *the **main** problem* / ✗ *the problem **main***
> *a **unique** opportunity* / ✗ *an opportunity **unique***

| always before | only after |
|---|---|
| *a main reason* | *the reason is **main*** ✗ |
| *the only way* | *a way **only*** ✗ |
| *a small child* | *a child **small*** — acceptable, literary |
| *a lovely day* | *a day **lovely*** — rare, literary |

The *-s* adjectives are the other group that behaves oddly.

> *a **physics** exam* / *physics* is the subject
> *a **politics** degree*
> *a **maths** mistake* — British
> *a **math** mistake* — American

There is no article: *a maths exam*, never *an maths exam*, because
*maths* begins with a /m/ sound.

## Adverbs: the three positions

**After the verb.** The default, and the neutral position.

> *She speaks **English** well.* / *He answered **quickly**.*

**Before the main verb.** For emphasis on the manner, or with a
*be*-verb.

> *She **often** speaks English.* / *He **rarely** answers.*
> *He **is** often late.*

**At the end.** For a whole clause, or to avoid a second auxiliary.

> *I will call you **tomorrow**.*
> *I **have** never seen anything **like it**.*

The rule that decides: an adverb of manner goes after the verb, an adverb
of time goes at the end, and an adverb of frequency goes before the main
verb but after the auxiliary.

> *I **have** never **seen** it.* — *have* then adverb then main verb
> *I **never saw** it.* — adverb then verb, because *saw* is not auxiliary
> *I saw it **yesterday**.* — time at the end

<Warning>
*Hardly*, *barely* and *scarcely* go **between the auxiliary and the main
verb**, and nowhere else. This is a rule with no exceptions and it is
wrong in learner English more often than any other adverb placement.

> ✓ *I **have hardly** seen anything **like** it.*
> ✓ *She **had barely** arrived **when** it started.*
> ✓ *We **can scarcely** afford it.*
> ✗ *I hardly have seen it.*
> ✗ *I have seen hardly anything.*

The same three words are also the ones that most often take inversion
after them, which is a separate rule from part 5.

> ✓ *Hardly **had I** arrived when it started.*
> ✗ *Hardly **I had** arrived when it started.*
</Warning>

## Adverbs of degree

These modify adjectives and other adverbs, and they are graded.

| word | modifies | example |
|---|---|---|
| *absolutely, completely, entirely, totally* | adjectives, adverbs | ***absolutely** certain* |
| *very, quite, rather, fairly* | adjectives, adverbs | ***very** tall* |
| *too* | adjectives, adverbs | ***too** difficult* |
| *enough* | after a word | *large **enough*** |
| *slightly, hardly, barely* | adjectives, adverbs | ***slightly** wrong* |
| *deeply, highly, widely* | with particular adjectives | ***deeply** regretted* |

*The* cannot be modified by a degree adverb.

> ✗ *The very good* → ✓ *The very best* → ✓ *a very good idea*

And *very* cannot stand before a past participle in that use.

> ✓ *a very well-known fact* — the hyphen matters
> ✗ *a very known fact*
> ✓ *He is very interested.* — *interested* is an adjective
> ✗ *He is very interested in it* → ✓ *He is very interested in it.* ✓

<DeepDive>
A small group of words is both adjective and adverb, with the same form
and two different jobs. These are the ones that cause real confusion,
because a learner picks one reading and applies it everywhere.

| word | as adjective | as adverb |
|---|---|---|
| *fast* | a **fast** car | **runs fast** |
| *hard* | a **hard** question | **works hard** |
| *late* | a **late** train | **arrives late** |
| *early* | an **early** bus | **leaves early** |
| *straight* | the **straight** road | **walk straight** |
| *right* | the **right** answer | **turn right** |
| *well* | the **well** children | **sings well** |

*Well* is the interesting one, because it is also the adverb form of the
adjective *good*.

> *She sings **well**.* — adverb
> *She is **well**.* — adjective, meaning healthy
> *She is **well** prepared.* — adverb, before a participle
> *She sings **good**.* — ✗, in careful English. In speech it is common.

So *well* does three jobs and the form does not change for any of them,
which is why it is worth a line of its own: if you are not sure whether
you want an adjective or an adverb, *well* is already both.
</DeepDive>

## Dangling adverbs

An adverb must attach to something, and if the thing it is supposed to
modify is not there, it dangles and often reverses the meaning.

> ✓ *She ran quickly **to catch the bus**.*
> ✗ *Running quickly, the bus was caught.*

> ✓ ***After losing** his wallet, he went home.*
> ✗ *After losing his wallet, **the police** went home.*

> ✗ *Sitting on the grass, **the picnic** was enjoyable.*
> ✓ *Sitting on the grass, **we** enjoyed the picnic.*

<Warning>
This one is a favourite of published prose and it reverses the sense of the
sentence while remaining perfectly grammatical. A picnic cannot sit on the
grass, so a reader has to stop and work out what was meant, and a reader
who has to stop has left the writer's argument.

The pattern to watch for is any sentence that begins with a participle and
puts a thing, rather than a person, in the subject slot. If the subject
cannot perform the action described, the participle is in the wrong place.

Fix it by naming the actor: *When I sat on the grass, the picnic became
enjoyable.*
</Warning>

## Four things learners get wrong about modifiers

**Adjective order.** Opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material,
purpose. Fixed, and felt if broken.

***Hardly* in the wrong place.** It goes between the auxiliary and the main
verb, always.

**A dangling participle or adverb.** Check that the subject can do what
the phrase says it does.

**An adverb of frequency after the verb.** ✗ *I go always there* → ✓ *I
**always** go there.*
