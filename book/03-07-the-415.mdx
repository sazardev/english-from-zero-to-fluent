# The 415 irregular verbs

This chapter explains what the reference table in part 10 is and how to use
it. The table itself is generated, so it is not in this chapter; it is in
part 10, where it runs to seven thousand words and lists every verb with
its three forms and its meaning.

## What makes a verb irregular

An English regular verb takes *-ed* in the past and *-ing* in the
continuous. A regular past participle is the same as the past simple.

> *walk* → *walked* → *walked*
> *call* → *called* → *called*

An irregular verb is one where at least one of those three forms breaks
the pattern.

> *write* → *wrote* → *written*
> *see* → *saw* → *seen*
> *go* → *went* → *gone*

Some are irregular in one form only, which surprises people who assume
that irregular means irregular throughout.

> *cut* → *cut* → *cut* — nothing changes
> *put* → *put* → *put* — nothing changes
> *set* → *set* → *set* — nothing changes
> *cost* → *cost* → *cost* — nothing changes

<Warning>
The verbs whose three forms are identical are the ones learners are most
likely to over-regularise, because the pattern they have been taught says
*cut* becomes *cutted*. In formal English *cutted* is wrong, and a
native speaker notices it immediately.

The list is short and worth learning as a set: *cut, put, set, cost, cast,
hit, hurt, let, shut, spread, burst, quit, split, thrust, upset.*

> ✓ We *cut* the rope. / ✗ We *cutted* the rope.
> ✓ He *quit* last week. / ✗ He *quitted* last week.

Compare *quit* with *quit* in the third person, where the *-s* is
unavoidable: *He quits.* That is not a contradiction; the verb is
irregular in the past only, and regular in the present.
</Warning>

## The three groups

Of the 415, the irregular ones fall into groups that share a pattern, and
the pattern tells you the form if you know it.

**The *-d* to *-t* group.** The past ends in *-t* where the base ends in
*d*.

> *feed* → *fed* → *fed*
> *bleed* → *bled* → *bled*
> *flee* → *fled* → *fled*
> *speed* → *sped* → *sped*
> *breed* → *bred* → *bred*
> *succeed* → *succeeded* — regular, because *-ed* is stressed
> *need* → *needed* — regular

**The doubling group.** A short vowel is doubled before *-ed* and *-ing*.

> *stop* → *stopped* → *stopping*
> *plan* → *planned* → *planning*
> *admit* → *admitted* → *admitting*
> *occur* → *occurred* → *occurring*
> *prefer* → *preferred* → *preferring*

The rule has three conditions and all three must hold: one syllable, a
single vowel at the end, and a single consonant at the end. Break any one
and the doubling stops.

> *need* → *needed* (two syllables)
> *travel* → *travelled* (British) or *traveled* (American)
> *open* → *opened* (no consonant at the end)
> *visit* → *visited* (two syllables)

**The *-ow* to *-ew* group.**

> *know* → *knew* → *known*
> *throw* → *threw* → *thrown*
> *grow* → *grew* → *grown*
> *show* → *showed* → *shown*
> *blow* → *blew* → *blown*
> *flow* → *flowed* → *flown*

Note that *show* and *flow* keep the regular past and only change the
participle, which is the usual pattern: the participle is where the
irregularity concentrates.

**The vowel-change group.** The vowel changes and nothing else.

> *sing* → *sang* → *sung*
> *drink* → *drank* → *drunk*
> *ring* → *rang* → *rung*
> *swim* → *swam* → *swum*

*Swum* is the exception that proves the rule: it is the only one of these
with an invented participle, and it is the correct form.

<DeepDive>
The vowel-change group is the largest and the most arbitrary, and it has
one feature worth knowing: **the participle is almost always a vowel plus
*n***.

> *sing* → *sung*, *drink* → *drunk*, *ring* → *rung*, *begin* → *begun*,
> *swim* → *swum*, *hang* → *hung*, *sink* → *sunk*, *spin* → *spun*

There are about twenty of these, and they are worth learning as one group,
because seeing *sung*, *rung*, *drunk* and *swum* together is
memorable, while learning them one at a time from a table of 415 is not.

Two in the group break the *-n* pattern and both are common: *see* →
*seen* and *do* → *done*. And one is irregular in the other direction,
adding a syllable rather than a letter: *go* → *gone*.
</DeepDive>

## The same verb, twice

Some verbs change their meaning with their form, and using the wrong one
changes the sentence rather than breaking it.

> *I **had** a car.* — possession
> ✗ *I **have had** a car.* — not possession, see below

> *I **have** a car.* — possession

> *I **did** read it.* — I did read it
> *I **didn't** read it.* — I did not read it

> *I **have** had this problem before.* — experience, repeated
> *I **had** this problem.* — one specific past event

> *She **went** to the door.* — movement
> *She **went** for the door.* — nonsense; the verb for that is *went
  for*, and it means she fetched it

> *He **did** nothing.* — he was idle
> *He **did not** do anything.* — same meaning, different emphasis

The double form *have had* is worth a moment, because it looks impossible
until you hear it.

> I *have had* this car for ten years. — the car exists, I own it, the
> situation continues
> *I have had* many cars. — the situation is general, and *had* is the
> participle of a second *have*
> *I had had* enough by then. — past perfect of a perfect, and it means I
> was already fed up before something else happened

The form *had had* is correct English and appears in formal writing. What
is wrong is *had have*, never.

## Using the table

The table in part 10 is sorted alphabetically and gives, for each verb,
the base form, the past, the participle, the meaning, the situations it
appears in, and the traps it causes. Read it three ways.

**As a lookup.** You meet a verb in a text and need the form. Look it up.
This is the reason the table exists and it justifies itself immediately.

**As a list to memorise.** The first hundred cover almost everything you
meet daily. *be, have, do, go, make, take, come, get, give, see, know,
think, say, tell, become, find, leave, feel, put, bring, begin, keep, hold,
write, stand, hear, let, mean, set, meet, run, pay, sit, speak, lie, lead,
read, grow, lose, fall, send, build, understand, draw, break, spend, cut,
rise, drive, buy, wear, choose, seek, throw, catch, deal, win, forget,
teach, eat, fly, beat, sell, buy, fight, sing.*

**As a diagnostic.** Look at the traps column and you will find your own
errors listed. The most common are the same for every learner, and they
are recorded in the table for exactly this purpose.

<Warning>
Two errors account for more lost marks in irregular verbs than all the
rest together, and both come from treating a *-ed* form as correct when
the verb is irregular.

**Writing the past as a real word.** ✗ *He goed* → ✓ *He went*. ✗ *She
buyed* → ✓ *She bought*. ✗ *I writed* → ✓ *I wrote*.

**Confusing past and participle.** They are the same for most verbs and
different for about eighty, and the difference matters in the perfect and
the passive.

> ✗ *I have **went** there.* → ✓ *I have **gone** there.*
> ✗ *The report has **wrote**.* → ✓ *The report has **written**.*

A useful test: if the form is used after *have*, it must be a participle.
Part 10's table marks which verbs differ, and those eighty are the ones to
drill.
</Warning>

## What is not in the table

**Phrasal verbs.** The table lists *give up*, not just *give*, because the
particles are part of the verb. Part 3 covered them separately; part 6
covers the phrasal verbs that change meaning.

**Auxiliary verbs.** *be, have, do* and the modals are irregular but they
are in part 3, because their grammar is not the same as the rest. *be* has
three forms and ten functions, and treating it as an entry in a list of 415
loses the thing that makes it useful.

**Compound verbs.** *make up, hand over, take off* are listed as their base
verb, because the irregularity is in the base.

## How to actually learn them

Not by reading the table. Four methods, in order of how well they work.

**Learn the shapes, not the list.** The *-n* participles, the *-ow* to
*-ew* group, the identical triples. That covers about a hundred verbs
from about six patterns.

**Learn the high-frequency hundred first.** The rest will be picked up
from reading, because they are low-frequency and you will meet them once a
fortnight.

**Use them in sentences you would say.** *I **had** a long day* teaches
*had* better than a row in a table, because the sentence is yours and you
will need it again.

**Write your own errors.** Every irregular verb you get wrong, write in
the errors file with the right form. Part 0 explains why this works
better than any other method: a mistake written down is a mistake you do
not make a third time.
