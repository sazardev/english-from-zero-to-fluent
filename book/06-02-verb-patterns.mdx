# Verb patterns

A verb pattern is the shape of what follows a verb: nothing, a
preposition, a noun, a *-ing* clause, a *to* clause, or a whole sentence
with its own subject. English has about a dozen patterns, they combine
predictably, and a learner who knows the patterns can use a verb they have
never studied.

## The patterns

| pattern | marker | example |
|---|---|---|
| intransitive | — | *She left.* |
| transitive, no marker | bare noun | *She wrote a letter.* |
| transitive, preposition | *on, in, to, for* | *She replied **to** it.* |
| *-ing* clause | *doing* | *She enjoys **reading**.* |
| *to* clause | *to do* | *She wants **to read**.* |
| *that* clause | *that + clause* | *She knows **that it** is late.* |
| bare *that* clause | clause, no *that* | *She said **it** was late.* |
| question clause | *whether, if, wh-* | *She asked **whether it** was late.* |
| noun clause | a noun as object | *She expected **an answer**.* |
| *-ing* after a preposition | preposition + doing | *She is good **at reading**.* |

## Why the pattern matters

Because it tells you what can follow, and it is almost always the same
pattern for every verb in a family.

> *decide, determine, expect, intend, plan, promise, refuse, agree, offer,
> manage, fail, pretend, seem, appear, tend*
> all + *to* + base

> *admit, avoid, consider, deny, enjoy, finish, imagine, mind, miss,
> postpone, practise, risk, suggest, quit*
> all + *-ing*

> *believe, consider, expect, feel, find, hope, imagine, suppose, think,
> wonder*
> all + *that* + clause, or bare *that*

> *announce, describe, explain, mention, propose, reply, say, suggest*
> all + a bare clause, with no *that*

<Warning>
The *-ing* family and the *to* family are the two that matter, and they do
not overlap. A learner who has met one verb in each family and worked out
the pattern can predict the rest, and a learner who has not will guess.

The prediction is reliable enough to be worth making. If *enjoy* takes
*-ing*, then *avoid* almost certainly does, and *suggest* almost certainly
does, and *resent* and *mind* and *visualise* do. If *decide* takes *to*,
then *refuse* and *promise* and *fail* do.

Where the prediction fails, it fails in both directions and the failures
are the interesting ones. *Smell* takes both: *I smell smoke* (transitive,
no marker) and *I smell of smoke* (unusual) and *I smell smoke coming*
(*-ing* clause). *Taste* and *feel* are the same: *I feel pain*, *I feel
like crying*.
</Warning>

## Patterns that surprise

**Verbs that take both a bare noun and a clause, with the meaning
shifting.**

> *He **found** the book. — discovered
> *He **found** the book **to be** interesting. — discovered a property
> *He **found** it **difficult**. — experienced
> *He **found** that it was difficult. — same, in a clause
> *He **found** me a taxi. — provided, transitive
> *He **found** out. — discovered after a period

*Find* is the extreme case: it takes a bare noun, an *-ing* clause, a
*to* clause, a *that* clause, a bare clause, and a *-ing* clause as its
object, plus a particle. Six patterns, one word, and the meaning changes
with each.

**Verbs whose *-ing* form and *to* form are both transitive.**

> *She **stopped** smoking.* — the activity ended
> *She **stopped** him smoking.* — she prevented him
> *She **stopped** the car.* — the object of stop
> *She **stopped** to smoke.* — she paused in order to smoke

**Verbs that take a clause with its own subject.**

> *She **said** **it** was late.* — bare clause
> *She **said** **that it** was late.* — with *that*
> *She **said to him** **that** it was late.* — with a recipient
> *She **suggested that we** leave early.* — *that* + subject + base
> *She **suggested leaving** early.* — *-ing*

*Suggest* and *recommend* and *insist* take *that* + **subject + base
form**, with no *to* and no *-s*. This is one of the few places English
requires a bare infinitive.

> ✓ *She suggested **that we leave** early.*
> ✗ *She suggested **that we should leave** early.* — too wordy
> ✗ *She suggested **that we to leave** early.*
> ✓ *They insisted **that she be** present.* — with *be*, and this is
> formal; *insisted that she was present* is the spoken form

<DeepDive>
The pattern that gives learners the most trouble is the one where the verb
takes a **clause with its own subject**, because there is no way to tell
from the verb alone which words belong to the clause.

> *He **remembered to lock** the door.* — he remembered a duty
> *He **remembered locking** the door.* — he remembered doing it
> *He **remembered that he had locked** the door.* — the same event, said
> in a clause

The three sentences mean different things and the difference is entirely
in the pattern. This is why learning a verb by its meaning is not enough;
you have to learn its shape.

The rule that helps most: after a verb that takes a clause, find the
subject of that clause. If there is no subject in the following words, the
clause has not started, and what you are looking at is an *-ing* form or a
*to* form instead.

> *He admitted **the error**.* — a noun
> *He admitted **making** the error.* — an *-ing* clause
> *He admitted **that he had made** the error.* — a *that* clause with
> *he*

Three ways of saying one thing, and the learner who has only the first will
write the first forever.
</DeepDive>

## Nominalisation

Many verbs have a noun form with the same meaning, and English prefers the
noun in formal writing.

| verb | noun | example |
|---|---|---|
| *decide* | *decision* | *a decision was made* |
| *explain* | *explanation* | *an explanation of the rules* |
| *arrive* | *arrival* | *on arrival* |
| *depart* | *departure* | *the departure time* |
| *conclude* | *conclusion* | *the conclusion was reached* |
| *propose* | *proposal* | *a proposal was submitted* |
| *discuss* | *discussion* | *under discussion* |
| *analyse* | *analysis* | *an analysis of the data* |
| *permit* | *permission* | *permission was granted* |

<Warning>
Nominalisation makes writing sound formal and makes it harder to read,
because it hides the actor and the action behind a noun. It is worth
knowing because academic English uses it constantly, and worth avoiding
because it is a better sentence without it.

> *The **implementation** of the new policy **was** met with **resistance**
> from staff.*
> *Staff **resisted** the new policy.*

The second is a third of the length, it has a real subject, and it has a
real verb. A reader who has to unpack *the implementation of* and *was met
with* has done work the writer could have done.

The rule: if the sentence has *is*, *was* or *were* as its main verb, and
you can find a real verb hiding in a noun nearby, rewrite it. That single
check catches most of it.
</Warning>

## Four things learners get wrong about verb patterns

***-ing* where the verb wants *to*.** ✗ *I enjoy to read* → ✓ *I enjoy
reading*.

***To* where the verb wants *-ing*.** ✗ *I suggest to go* → ✓ *I suggest
going*.

**Missing the clause subject.** ✗ *She suggested me to leave* → ✓ *She
suggested **that I** leave* / *She suggested I leave* / *She suggested
that I should leave*.

**A past participle where a clause is needed.** ✗ *He admitted to make
the error* → ✓ *He admitted **making** it* / *He admitted **that he had
made** it*.
