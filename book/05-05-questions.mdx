# Questions and how to ask them

English has three question forms and about forty fixed patterns. The three
forms are easy. The patterns are where the fluency is.

## The three forms

**Yes or no.** Auxiliary before the subject.

> ***Do** you **speak** French?*
> ***Is** she **arriving** tomorrow?*
> ***Have** you **eaten** yet?*

**Wh-.** The question word in front, still with the auxiliary.

> ***What** did you **do**?*
> ***Where** does he **live**?*
> ***Why** was she **angry**?*

**Question tags.** A short form at the end that carries the real meaning.

> *You are coming, **aren't you**?*
> *She is a teacher, **isn't she**?*
> *You will help me, **won't you**?*
> *Nobody called, **did they**?*

## The question word forms

| word | asks about | do form | short form |
|---|---|---|---|
| *who* | a person | ***Who did** you call?* | *Who called?* |
| *what* | a thing, or an unspecified person | ***What did** you buy?* | *What happened?* |
| *which* | one of a known set | ***Which one** do you want?* | *Which do you prefer?* |
| *whose* | a possessor | ***Whose bag** is this?* | *Whose was it?* |
| *where* | a place | ***Where does** she live?* | *Where is it?* |
| *when* | a time | ***When did** it happen?* | *When?* |
| *why* | a reason | ***Why did** you leave?* | *Why?* |
| *how* | a manner | ***How do** you know?* | *How?* |

<Warning>
*How* combines with the others to make eight more question forms, and this
is where learners lose the thread. The rule is that *how* sits in front of
another question word and the rest of the question is normal.

> ***How many** books did you read?*
> ***How much** does it cost?*
> ***How long** did you wait?*
> ***How far** is the station?*
> ***How often** do you exercise?*
> ***How old** are you?*
> ***How soon** will you leave?*
> ***How come** you said that?

The last one is not a question at all in structure. *How come* is
informal for *why*, and it does **not** invert.

> ✗ *How come did you say that?*
> ✓ *How come you said that?*
> ✓ *Why **did** you say that?*

*How come* is worth learning because it is the only wh- question that
behaves differently, and every native speaker uses it.
</Warning>

## Question tags

Tags are where a learner's questions become natural, because almost every
informal question in English is a statement with a tag on the end.

The tag is built from the auxiliary.

| statement | tag | note |
|---|---|---|
| *You are coming* | ***aren't you**?* | positive → negative tag |
| *You aren't coming* | ***are you**?* | negative → positive tag |
| *She is a teacher* | ***isn't she**?* | |
| *They have gone* | ***haven't they**?* | |
| *He will help* | ***won't he**?* | |
| *She did it* | ***didn't she**?* | |
| *You can swim* | ***can't you**?* | |
| *Nobody called* | ***did they**?* | negative subject |
| *Let's go* | ***shall we**?* | |
| *Nobody told me* | ***did they**?* | |

**With a negative subject, the tag is positive**, which is a detail
learners get wrong constantly.

> *Nobody called, **did they**? ✓
> ✗ *Nobody called, didn't they?* — this means "they did not call", which
> is the opposite question
> ✗ *Nobody called, didn't they?* → *Nobody called, did they?*

*Nobody* is negative, so the tag agrees with *nobody* by being positive.

**Contracted tags in speech.**

> *You're coming, **aren'tcha**?* → *aren't you*
> *I'm late, **ain't I**?* → *am I not*
> *You are not going, **right**?* — a neutral tag that works with anything
> ***Right?*** and ***OK?*** are the most useful tags in informal English,
> because they ask for confirmation without asserting anything

<Warning>
A tag turns a statement into a question, and the surprise is in the tag,
not the statement. Putting a *surprised* intonation on the statement
defeats the point.

> *You are coming, **aren't you**?* — I assumed you were, and I am checking
> *You are coming, **aren't you**?* — genuinely astonished
> ✗

A learner who says *You are coming, aren't you?* with the tone of someone
discovering news has expressed the opposite of what the sentence asks.

The reliable habit: keep the statement flat and let the tag do the work. In
writing there is no tone at all, which is why tags read as more confident
than the statement does.
</Warning>

## Indirect questions

A wh- question embedded in a statement, and the rule is that it stops being
a question: no inversion, no *do*.

> *I wonder **where he lives**.* → *I wonder **where does he live*** ✗
> *Tell me **what you want**.* → *Tell me **what do you want*** ✗
> *Do you know **where the bank is**?* → *Do you know **where is the
> bank*** ✗

| direct | indirect |
|---|---|
| *Where is the bank?* | *I wonder where the bank is.* |
| *What time does it start?* | *I asked what time it starts.* |
| *Did she leave?* | *I wonder whether she left.* |
| *Is he coming?* | *I wonder if he is coming.* |
| *How much is it?* | *I asked how much it was.* |

**With *why*, the indirect form is different** and it is the one that
surprises people.

> ***Why** did you leave?* → *I asked **why** you **left**.* ✓
> *I asked **why you** **did** leave.* ✗

The reason is historical: *why* in an indirect question is followed by the
subject and the reason for the inversion disappeared with the rest of the
form. It is an exception and it is worth learning as one.

<DeepDive>
Four question forms are fixed phrases rather than constructions, and they
carry more politeness than their word order suggests. Learning them as
fixed phrases is the only way to get the tone right.

> ***How about** a walk?* — a suggestion, and the *-ing* is optional
> ***Why not** have a drink?*
> ***What if** we leave early?* — a suggestion hedged into a question
> ***Would you mind** closing the window?* — a request with *mind*
> ***Do you fancy** coming tonight?* — British, informal, and *fancy* here
> means *feel like*
> ***Would you like** to sit down?*
> ***Could I possibly** ask you something?* — three hedges in three words
> ***Is there any chance** you could help?*
> ***I don't suppose** you could help?*
> ***Would it be possible** to move the meeting?*

Notice that the most polite forms are all questions with no expectation of
a yes, which is the same mechanism as the tag: a question is a request for
confirmation, and a request that anticipates refusal is politer than one
that does not.

The order of politeness in English is roughly: *Could you possibly* >
*Would you mind* > *Can you* > *You can*.
</DeepDive>

## Echo questions

Repeating part of the question as a question, to ask for confirmation of
something surprising.

> *You're leaving.* — ***Leaving?***
> *He said Tuesday.* — ***Tuesday?***
> *She wants three.* — ***Three?***

The repeated word can be a noun, a verb, a number or a whole phrase, and
it usually carries rising intonation. Written, the question mark is the only
signal, so the convention in writing is to put the echoed part in italics.

## Four things learners get wrong about questions

**Inverting inside an indirect question.** ✗ *I wonder where does he
live.* → ✓ *I wonder where **he lives**.*

***How come* inverted.** ✗ *How come did you leave?* → ✓ *How come you
left?*

**A negative tag with a negative subject.** ✗ *Nobody called, didn't
they?* → ✓ *Nobody called, did they?*

**Inverting after *why* in an indirect question.** ✗ *I asked why you did
leave.* → ✓ *I asked why you left.*
