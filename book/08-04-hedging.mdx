# Hedging

A hedge reduces the strength of a claim. It is the most useful single
technique in careful writing, because most claims are slightly stronger
than the evidence supports, and the hedge is how you say so without
sounding unsure.

## The full range, weakest to strongest

| strength | form | example |
|---|---|---|
| weakest | *It could be argued that...* | |
| | *It may be the case that...* | |
| | *It seems likely that...* | |
| | *This appears to suggest that...* | |
| | *The data are consistent with...* | |
| | *The results suggest that...* | |
| | *The evidence indicates that...* | |
| | *The data show that...* | |
| strongest | *We demonstrate that...* | |

The point of the scale is that you can pick the exact strength your
evidence has. Most overclaiming is a hedge that was left out, and most
underclaiming is a hedge that was added to a sentence that did not need
it.

<Warning>
Overclaiming is the most criticised habit in research writing and the most
common habit in learner writing, and the reason is structural: a bare claim
is easier to write and easier to read.

> *The data prove that the treatment works.*
> — the sentence is short, clear, and probably wrong. A clinical trial
> almost never proves anything; the strongest available claim is that the
> results are consistent with an effect and unlikely to be chance.

> *The data suggest that the treatment works.*
> — the same information, calibrated, and this sentence survives review.

The test for whether a claim is overclaimed: **could someone find a case
that contradicts it?** If yes, hedge. *Prove* and *demonstrate* leave no
room, and leaving no room in a claim about the world is almost always a
mistake.
</Warning>

## Hedges by part of speech

**Adverbs.** *probably, possibly, perhaps, likely, apparently, seemingly,
presumably, arguably, admittedly, arguably, clearly, evidently, allegedly*

> *The results **apparently** contradict the model.* — something suggests
> this and the writer is not certain
> *The results **clearly** contradict the model.* — the writer is certain,
> and *clearly* says so out loud, which is a claim in itself

**Adjectives.** *possible, probable, likely, unlikely, potential, apparent,
evident, questionable, debatable, tentative, preliminary*

> *There is **a possible** explanation.*
> *The effect is **tentative**.*

**Verbs and whole constructions.** These are the useful ones, because they
carry the hedge without a separate word.

| construction | strength |
|---|---|
| *It seems that...* | weak |
| *It appears that...* | weak |
| *It is likely that...* | moderate |
| *It could be that...* | weak |
| *The evidence suggests that...* | moderate |
| *X tends to...* | a pattern, not a rule |
| *X is associated with...* | correlation, not cause |
| *X is related to...* | correlation |
| *X may reflect...* | a possible explanation |
| *One explanation is that...* | explicitly not the only one |

<DeepDive>
One hedge is not a hedge at all, and it is worth separating out because
learners use it as one: **it is thought that**, **it is believed that**,
**it has been reported that**.

These look like hedges and are actually **attributions**. They say that
somebody holds the claim, not that the claim is probably true. The
difference matters whenever you are evaluating the claim rather than
reporting it.

> ***It has been reported that** the treatment causes headaches.*
> — somebody reported it. It might be true, it might be false, and the
> source is unstated.

> ***Headaches have been reported** in patients taking the treatment.*
> — the headaches are real, the causal link is not established.

> *Studies **have reported** similar results.*
> — attribution, and the reader must go and find the studies

The distinction that matters: *the data suggest* commits the writer to
judging the evidence. *It has been suggested* does not. The first is
better writing, and the second is what a writer reaches for when they do not
want to take responsibility for a claim, which is sometimes honest and
sometimes not.

Where the attribution is named, it is at least traceable: *Smith et al.
suggest*, *the WHO reports*, *according to*. A bare *it is thought that*
is the weakest and most common form, and its weakness is that nobody can
check it.
</DeepDive>

## Hedging the number, not just the claim

Statistics and measurements are hedged too, and the hedging is in the
precision of the number.

> *about 30* / *approximately 30* / *around 30*
> *some 30* / *an estimated 30* / *a little under 30*
> *between 28 and 32*, with a confidence interval
> *30 ± 2*

The last two are stronger claims than the first three, because they say how
precise the estimate is, and a learner who writes *about 30* is losing
information the data contain.

> ✗ *There were **a lot** of errors.* — no quantity
> ✓ *There were **roughly 400** errors.*
> ✓ *Error **rate: 12 per cent (95% CI 11 to 13)**.* — the strongest form,
> because it states the uncertainty

A claim with no number and no range is the weakest thing in writing, and it
is very easy to fix.

## Hedges that do not work

Some things should not be hedged, and hedging them weakens a sentence
rather than protecting it.

> ✗ *We **may suggest that** the data show a trend.*
> — the hedge is doubled; *may suggest* is incoherent

> ✗ *It **might possibly** be true.* — two hedges, and the second is
> meaningless

> ✗ ***It is important to note that** the sample was contaminated.*
> — this is a fact about the sample, not a claim about the world

> ✗ ***It is important to note that** the method is sound.*
> — if it is important, say it: *The method is sound.*

> ✗ *The sample **was perhaps somewhat** contaminated.*
> — *perhaps* and *somewhat* do the same work twice

> ✗ *We **would tend to suggest that** one **might** possibly expect...*
> — four hedges and a verb per clause. This is the sound of a writer
> avoiding a claim.

<Warning>
**It is important to note that** deserves particular attention, because it
is the single most common piece of padding in learner academic writing, and
it usually marks a sentence that should be much simpler.

Every time you write *it is important to note that*, ask: **important to
whom?** If the answer is "the reader needs to know this to follow the
argument", then it is important, and the sentence should say the thing
without the frame.

> ✗ *It is important to note that the results cannot be generalised.*
> ✓ ***The results cannot be generalised.***
> ✓ *These results **apply only to** samples from this region.*

The second version is stronger, because it says what the limitation is
instead of announcing that a limitation exists.
</Warning>

## The test

Take a claim you have written and ask three questions.

1. **Could someone find a case that contradicts it?** If yes, hedge.
2. **How do I know?** If the answer is a report rather than a source, name
   the source or drop the claim.
3. **Is the number precise enough to be useful?** *About 30* → *28 to 32*,
   with the range.

A sentence that passes all three is a sentence someone can rely on, and
that is the whole aim.

## Four things learners get wrong about hedging

**No hedging at all.** *Prove* and *demonstrate* where *suggest* and
*indicate* were correct.

**Double hedging.** *May suggest, might possibly, could perhaps.*

**Hedging a fact.** *It is important to note that the sample was
contaminated.*

***It is thought that* instead of a source.** Untraceable, and it commits
the writer to nothing.
