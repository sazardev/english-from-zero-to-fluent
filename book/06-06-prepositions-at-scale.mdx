# Prepositions at scale

Part 1 covered the prepositions of place and part 3 the verbs that require
one. This chapter is the rest: the prepositions that do the work of
connecting an idea to a noun phrase, which is where most of a learner's
preposition errors actually live.

## The prepositions and what they do

| preposition | job | example |
|---|---|---|
| *of* | belonging, composition, measure | *a **of** bread*, *a friend **of** mine* |
| *in* | inside, during, within | *in** the room**, in 2020, in detail* |
| *on* | contact, support, a day, a topic | *on the wall, on Monday, on the radio* |
| *at* | a point, a place, an event | *at the door, at noon, at a concert* |
| *to* | direction, recipient, a limit | *to the station, to her, ten to six* |
| *for* | benefit, duration, exchange | *for you, for a week, £5 for the book* |
| *with* | accompaniment, instrument, means | *with a friend, with a pen, with care* |
| *by* | agent, means, route | *by post, by train, written by her* |
| *from* | origin, separation | *from Spain, from nine to five* |
| *about* | topic, approximation | *about money, about ten* |

Every error below is one of these ten used wrongly, and the reason it is
easy to get wrong is that the distinctions are about **meaning**, not about
place.

## *On* and *in* for surfaces

The most basic pair, and the most reversed by learners whose first language
uses one preposition for both.

| phrase | preposition |
|---|---|
| **on** the wall | a surface in contact |
| **on** the ceiling | a surface in contact |
| **on** the bus, **on** the train | a public transport vehicle |
| **on** the radio, **on** television | a medium |
| **in** the box, **in** the drawer | a container |
| **in** the car | a private vehicle, or the activity of being inside it |
| **in** the room | an enclosed space |
| **at** the door, **at** the window | a point |
| **at** the bus stop | a point |
| **in** the morning, **in** the evening | a period of the day |
| **at** night | a period, but *at* |
| **on** Monday, **on** 5 May | a day |
| **at** the weekend | British, and the period not the day |

<Warning>
The two that produce the most confident errors:

**In** the car, **on** the bus. A private vehicle you sit inside; public
transport you sit on. This is not a rule anyone decided, it is a fact about
the vehicles, and it is a fact about English.

> ✓ *I am **in** the car.*
> ✗ *I am on the car.*
> ✓ *I am **on** the bus.*
> ✗ *I am in the bus.* — except in some countries, where "in the bus" is
> used, and it is marked in British and American English

**In** the morning, **at** night, **on** Monday. Three different
prepositions for three spans of time.

> ✓ *in the morning*, *in the afternoon*, *in the evening*
> ✓ *at night*
> ✓ *on Monday*, *on Friday*, *on 1 January*
> ✓ *at the weekend* — British; *on the weekend* — American
> ✗ *on night* / ✗ *in Monday*

If you are writing and are not sure, *at* is the safe fallback for a point
in time: *at 6pm, at midnight, at Christmas, at the weekend.*
</Warning>

## *At*, *in* and *on* for places

*At* is a point. *In* is an area. *On* is a surface or a larger place used
as a surface.

> *I am **at** the door.* — the door as a point
> *I am **in** the kitchen.* — the room
> *I am **on** the roof.* — the surface
> *She lives **at** 14 Ash Grove.* — the number as a point
> *She lives **in** Ash Grove.* — the street as a place
> *She lives **on** Ash Grove.* — the street as a surface, and less common

*At* with an address, *in* with a town or a country, and this is stable:

> *at 14 Ash Grove, in Bristol, in England*
> *at 5 Main Street, in Springfield, in the United States*

## *For* and *since*, and *in* and *during*

| duration with an end | duration without | point |
|---|---|---|
| *for* two weeks | *for* two weeks | *in* July |
| *from* Monday **to** Friday | *for* four days | *at* nine o'clock |
| *over* the summer | *during* the summer | *on* Monday |
| *in* 2020 | *during* 2020 | *in* 2020 |

*In* versus *during* is a small distinction that native speakers are loose
about and that is worth being careful with in writing.

> *in 2020* — the year as a whole
> *during 2020* — a period inside the year, at some point
> *in the summer* — the season as a whole
> *during the summer* — at some point in it

## *Of* and possessive *'s*

Both mark belonging. The difference is length and formality.

> *the roof **of** the house* — of + longer
> *the house's roof* — 's + shorter, more common in speech
> *a friend **of** mine* — and here *of* means something *of* cannot be
> replaced with: one of my friends, but specifically a friend of mine
> *a book **of** photographs* — a book that consists of photographs

**A friend of mine** cannot be *my friend* in the same way, because it
emphasises that there are several and this is one of them.

> *a friend of mine* — one of my friends
> *my friend* — the person I am thinking of, possibly the only one

<DeepDive>
The pairs that invert completely, which is the reason this chapter exists
rather than a list of the ten prepositions with examples.

**Borrow from**, **lend to**. A learner who thinks in terms of direction
reverses these.

> *I borrowed £20 **from** my brother.*
> *My brother lent £20 **to** me.*
> ✗ *I borrowed £20 to my brother.* — that is a donation, or a different
> language's grammar
> ✗ *My brother lent £20 from me.* — he gave it and took it back, or
> nonsense

**Worry about**, **concerned about**, **involved in**.

> *I worry **about** the exam.*
> *I am concerned **about** the exam.*
> *I am involved **in** the exam.* — participating
> ✗ *I am concerned in the exam* — wrong
> ✗ *I am involved about the exam* — wrong

**Consist of**, **insist on**, **depend on**, **rely on**, **concentrate
on**. Part 3 has the full list, and *of* appears in exactly one of them,
which is what makes it memorable.

**Listen to**, **listen for**, **listen in**.

> *listen **to** music* — with the thing
> *listen **for** a sound* — waiting to hear it
> *listen **in*** — eavesdrop, and rare

**Depend on** versus *insist on* versus *concentrate on* — all *on*. And
*depend of*, *insist of*, *concentrate of* are all wrong, and all sound
plausible because *of* is the preposition English uses for nearly
everything else.

The general principle behind all of these: **the preposition is chosen by
the word in front of it, not by the word behind it.** That single sentence
is worth more than memorising two hundred phrases, because it tells you
what to do when you meet a phrase you have never seen: look it up, do not
derive it.
</DeepDive>

## Prepositional phrases of place

**Fixed points.**

> *at the top, at the bottom, at the centre, at the edge*
> *in the middle, in the corner, in the centre*

**Boundaries and areas.**

> *on the border, on the edge of, on the coast*
> *in the middle of, in the centre of, in the middle of nowhere*

**Side by side.**

> *by the window, by the door, by the river*
> *next to, beside, alongside, near, close to, opposite, opposite to*
> ✗ *next the window* — needs *to*
> ✗ *beside of the window* — wrong; *beside* is already a preposition

**Above and below.**

> *above, over, on top of, higher than*
> *under, underneath, below, beneath, lower than*
> ✗ *over of* ✗ *under of* ✗ *below of*

*Over* and *above* differ: *above* is a relation, *over* is a position.

> *the plane flew **above** the clouds.* — higher than
> *the plane flew **over** the clouds.* — physically on top, and not
> visible through them

## Common learner errors in a list

| wrong | right | why |
|---|---|---|
| *discuss about* | *discuss* | transitive verb |
| *contact with* | *contact* | transitive verb |
| *explain me* | *explain it to me* | two objects, one object needs *to* |
| *depend of* | *depend on* | the verb decides |
| *consist in* | *consist of* | |
| *consist from* | *consist of* | |
| *listen of* | *listen to* | |
| *married with* | *married to* | *marry* takes no preposition |
| *discuss* + *about* | *discuss* | |
| *arrive to* | *arrive at* / *arrive in* | |
| *enter to* | *enter* | transitive |
| *reach to* | *reach* | transitive |
| *according with me* | *according to me* | |
| *due to the fact that* | *because* | formal and clunky |
| *in front of* ✓ | *opposite to* | *opposite* is a preposition on its own |
| *beside of* | *beside* | |
| *next the* | *next to the* | |
| *across of* | *across* | |
| *through of* | *through* | |
| *despite of* | *despite* / *in spite of* | |
| *concern about* as a verb | *concern* | *concerns* takes no preposition |
| *phone to* | *phone* | transitive |
| *enter into* | *enter* | *enter into* is a different phrase and rare |
| *borrow from someone* ✓ | *lend to someone* | the direction reverses |

<Warning>
Three of these are so common that they are worth singling out.

***Discuss about*** and ***contact with*** are transitive verbs with no
preposition. The *-s* and *-t* endings are the clue, and so is the fact
that they are verbs.

***Married with***, ***born in*** is the contrast: *marry* takes no
preposition, *be born* takes *in*. A learner who applies one rule to both
gets one of them wrong.

***Arrive to*** and ***reach to*** and ***enter to*** are all wrong, and all
sound right because a language that marks direction on the verb expects a
preposition to appear.
</Warning>

## Four things learners get wrong about prepositions at scale

**Deriving the preposition from the noun.** *depend of, insist of,
concentrate of, listen of, married with.*

**Adding one to a transitive verb.** *discuss about, contact with,
explain me, phone to, enter to, reach to.*

**Reversing the direction.** *I borrowed **to** her* → *borrowed **from**
her*. *She lent **from** me* → *lent **to** me*.

**Using *of* where the verb names it.** *consist in* → *consist **of***.
*of* is the commonest preposition in English, which is exactly why it is
over-applied.
