# Auxiliaries

An auxiliary is a verb that does not act on its own. It supports a main
verb, and it is where the tense, the voice and the mood of an English
sentence actually live.

## The list

| auxiliary | carries |
|---|---|
| *be* | present passive, continuous, *-ing* forms |
| *have* | perfect tenses |
| *do* | emphatic, negative and question forms |

| modal | carries |
|---|---|
| *can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must* | ability, permission, obligation, possibility |

That is the whole inventory. Everything called a tense in English is one
of these seven verbs plus a main verb.

## *Do* as an auxiliary

*Do* does three jobs, and in all of them the main verb returns to its base
form.

**Negative.** The auxiliary takes the *-s* and the *not*.

> *Don't* forget. / *Doesn't* matter. / *Didn't* come.
> *I **don't** know* → not *I don't knows*
> *He **doesn't** know* → not *He don't know*

**Question.** Same pattern, and the *-s* sits on *do*.

> *Do* you know? / *Does* he know? / *Did* she go?

**Emphasis.** Here *do* carries meaning: it asserts that what follows is
true, and disputes the alternative.

> *I **do** think* it matters. *(I really think so)*
> *She **does** know* the answer. *(she knows it, contrary to what you
> said)*
> *I **did** tell* you. *(I told you, and you did not listen)*

<Warning>
The *-s* has exactly one home in an English sentence, and the rule is
worth having as a single sentence: **the *-s* goes on the first auxiliary
and nowhere else.**

> ✓ He *doesn't* work.
> ✗ He *don't* work.
> ✓ He *doesn't* **work**s. → ✗, and this is the one learners produce when
> they have learned *-s* as a fact about verbs rather than as a fact
> about the first auxiliary.

> ✓ *Doesn't* he **work**?
> ✗ *Doesn't* he **works**?
> ✓ *Has* she **finished**? / ✗ *Has* she **finishes**?

In the negative and the question, *-s* moves to the auxiliary and the main
verb goes bare. That is the same fact you already learned with *does* and
*doesn'* in the present chapter, and it applies to every auxiliary in
English: *has walked*, never *has walk*; *can read*, never *can reads*;
*was going*, never *was goings*.
</Warning>

## *Have* as an auxiliary

*Have* carries the perfect, and the main verb becomes the past participle.

> *have walked*, *have gone*, *have written*
> *had walked*, *had gone*
> *will have walked*

The form of *have* is fixed and depends only on the person:

| person | *have* | *had* | *having* |
|---|---|---|---|
| I, you, we, they | *have* | *had* | *having* |
| he, she, it | *has* | *had* | *having* |

Note that *had* and *having* do not change. The past perfect and the
present perfect continuous would otherwise need *he had had* and *he had
having*, and no language does that.

> ✓ *She has had* a car for a year.
> ✓ *She has had* the job since 2010.
> ✗ *She has have* a car.

## *Be* as an auxiliary

*Be* carries the continuous and the passive, and it is the only auxiliary
that changes form for the person.

| person | *be* | *been* | *being* |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | *am* | *have been* | *being* |
| you, we, they | *are* | *have been* | *being* |
| he, she, it | *is* | *has been* | *being* |

The irregularity that catches people: *I am*, not *I is*, and *she is*, not
*she am*. And the *-s* goes on *is*, *was* and *has* but not on *were* or
*am*.

> ✓ It *is* written. / ✗ It *are* written.
> ✓ They *are* written. / ✗ They *is* written.
> ✓ Nothing *was* lost. / ✗ Nothing *were* lost.

## Modals

A modal has no *-s*, no *to* and no past form. It is followed by a base
verb, and that is the whole grammar of the modal.

| modal | meaning |
|---|---|
| *can* | ability, permission |
| *could* | past ability, polite request |
| *may* | permission, possibility, hedging |
| *might* | possibility, past |
| *must* | strong obligation, deduction |
| *shall* | formal offer, suggestion |
| *should* | advice, expectation |
| *will* | prediction, decision, offer |
| *would* | past *will*, conditional, polite offer |
| *ought to* | moral or practical obligation |

| property | modals |
|---|---|
| form *-s* in the third person | **none** |
| *to* after the modal | **none** |
| past form | only *could, might, would*, and *must* in negatives |
| infinitive after | **none** |

> ✓ He *can* swim.  ✗ He *cans* swim.  ✗ He *can to* swim.
> ✓ She *will* come.  ✗ She *wills* come.  ✗ She *will to* come.

<Warning>
The lack of a *-s* on modals is the direct cause of two errors that every
learner makes and few are told about.

**Adding *-s*.** ✗ *He cans* do it. ✗ *The system works well* is fine, but
✗ *She works not* / ✗ *She can nots* work.

**Adding *to*.** ✗ *I must to go.* ✗ *You should to try harder.*

Both come from treating a modal as an ordinary verb. It is not. It is a
different class of word, and the difference is visible in three places:
no *-s*, no *to*, and the main verb always bare.
</Warning>

### Negating a modal

There is only one form, with *not* or *n't* inserted, and no *do* anywhere.

> *cannot* / *can't*
> *will not* / *won't*
> *shall not* / *shan't*
> *is not* is the only exception, and it is not a modal.

> ✓ I *can't* come.
> ✓ She *won't* be able to.
> ✗ I *don't can* come.  ✗ She *doesn't can* come.

### Asking about a modal

Invert, with no *do*.

> *Can* you help me? / *Have* you seen it? / *Will* you be there?
> *Should* we wait? / *Would* you mind?

## Two auxiliaries together

Some tenses need two. They appear in a fixed order, and the order is the
same every time.

| combination | order | example |
|---|---|---|
| perfect continuous | *have* + *been* + *-ing* | *I have been working.* |
| future perfect | *will* + *have* + participle | *I will have finished.* |
| future perfect continuous | *will* + *have* + *been* + *-ing* | *I will have been working.* |
| modal perfect | *will* + *have* + participle | *I will have seen it.* |
| modal perfect continuous | *will* + *have* + *been* + *-ing* | *I will have been working.* |

The order never varies. Modal, then *have*, then *been*, then the main
verb.

> *will have been working* ✓
> *have will been working* ✗
> *will been have working* ✗

<DeepDive>
A useful way to see the auxiliaries is as a stack, where each one sits on
top of the next and the topmost one carries the tense. Written out as a
sentence, a long form decomposes like this.

| step | what it adds | example |
|---|---|---|
| 4. main verb | the meaning, and the form | *working* |
| 3. *been* | the aspect, the process | *been working* |
| 2. *have* | the perfect, the result | *have been working* |
| 1. *will* | the future | *will have been working* |

Read from the bottom up and you get the tense; read from the top down and
you get the order to write it in. The stack is why the sequence is fixed:
it is a stack, and stacks do not reorder.

It also explains something that confuses learners. The tense of the whole
sentence is the *first* auxiliary, not the last one.

> *He **has** been working here since 2010.* — present perfect continuous
> *He **had** been working here before he moved.* — past perfect
> *He **will have** been working here for two years by June.* — future
> perfect continuous

*Had* is in all three sentences. Which tense the sentence is depends
entirely on whether the first auxiliary is *has*, *had* or *will have*.
</DeepDive>

## Four things learners get wrong about auxiliaries

**Adding *-s* to a modal.** ✗ *He cans*, ✗ *She wills*, ✗ *It musts*.

**Adding *to* after a modal.** ✗ *I must to go.*

**Putting *do* in a modal question.** ✗ *Do you can help?* → ✓ *Can you
help?*

**Stranding the *-s* on the main verb.** ✗ *She doesn't works* → ✓ *She
doesn't work*.
