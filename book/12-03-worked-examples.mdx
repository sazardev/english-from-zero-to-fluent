# Worked examples

Every chapter in parts 1 to 8 explains a rule and shows it. This chapter
shows a **whole text being built**, one decision at a time, with the
reasoning visible. The gap this chapter closes is between recognising a
rule and producing a sentence that needs it.

Read the finished text at the end first. Then read how it was made.

## The text we are going to build

A short paragraph, the kind you might actually write. It is deliberately
ordinary, because the point is the decisions and not the content.

> The report I wrote last month has been rewritten three times, and the
> results that were in the first version have been removed. The
> measurements that were taken by the department were never checked
> properly, so nobody knows whether the compound is stable in the acid
> that was used. It is being tested again now, and a new set of results
> should be available before the end of the month.

Every one of those choices is defensible and none of them is the only
choice. That is the point: the alternatives are given, so you can see
that this is a decision and not a formula.

## Step 1: the raw idea, in the order it arrived

Before any grammar, the content. Write it badly, in whatever order the
thoughts are in.

> *report, wrote, month, three times, results, first version, removed.
> measurements, department, checked, compound, stable, acid. test again,
> results, end of month.*

This is what a first draft actually is, and it is not wrong. It is
unformed. The next steps are the grammar, and the grammar is a series of
decisions about this list.

## Step 2: choose the tenses, and say why

| content | tense chosen | reason |
|---|---|---|
| I wrote the report | past simple | a finished event at a point in the past |
| has been rewritten | present perfect | **the rewriting is finished and the result matters now** |
| three times | — | the present perfect cannot be counted with a finished time, and "three times" is not finished, so it stays |
| have been removed | present perfect passive | the removal is finished and the absence is the current fact |
| were taken | past simple passive | the taking happened once, at a point, in the past |
| are never checked | present simple passive | a fact about the method, still true |
| nobody knows | present simple | the state of knowledge now |
| is stable | present simple | a property of the compound, a fact |
| is being tested | present continuous passive | in progress right now |
| should be available | modal plus passive | a prediction about the future, hedged |

<Warning>
The decision worth studying is the second row, because it is the one
learners get wrong most.

> *The report **has been rewritten** three times.* — the third rewriting
> is finished, and the fact that matters is that the current version is
> not the first one
> ✗ *The report **was rewritten** three times.* — this is also true English,
> but it closes the story: it tells the reader that rewriting happened and
> then stops caring
> ✗ *The report **is rewritten** three times.* — present simple passive is a
> fact about the method, not a count of events, so this is wrong

If you were unsure, both past simple and present perfect are defensible.
**Present perfect when the current state depends on the event. Past
simple when it does not.** Everything in part 2 follows from that.
</Warning>

## Step 3: choose the voice, and say why

Four passives, and each one is chosen.

| phrase | voice | reason |
|---|---|---|
| the measurements that **were taken** | passive | the doer is the department, which is not the point, and it is already given later |
| that **were never checked** | passive | no doer, and the absence of checking is the claim |
| is **being tested** | passive | the doer is unknown and irrelevant |
| should **be available** | passive | the availability is the point, the provider is nobody in particular |

And one active, deliberately:

> *nobody **knows** whether...* — active, because the doer is the whole
> point of the sentence. Making it passive would lose the information.

**Rule that emerges from the text:** passivise when the doer is unknown,
obvious, or going to be stated later. Keep the active when the doer is the
news. Four passives out of eight clauses is unremarkable in academic
English and would be excessive in a letter.

## Step 4: reduce the relative clauses

The first draft has four full clauses doing a job that a participle does
better.

| full | reduced | why |
|---|---|---|
| the report **that I wrote** last month | the report **I wrote** last month | the pronoun is the object, so it can go |
| the results **that were in** the first version | the results **in** the first version | reduced to a prepositional phrase |
| the measurements **that were taken by** the department | the measurements **taken by** the department | passive, so *-ed* |
| the compound **that is stable** | — | kept as a clause, because the subject of *is* is the compound and dropping the pronoun leaves no subject |
| the acid **that was used** | the acid **used** | passive, so *-ed* |
| the set of results **that should be available** | — | kept, same reason |

Two of the six could not be reduced, and knowing which two is the skill.
The rule from part 3: a pronoun can be dropped when it is the object, and
never when it is the subject.

## Step 5: add the hedges

The first draft is certain about everything. The claims are not certain,
and part 8.4 is about saying so at the right strength.

| plain | calibrated | why |
|---|---|---|
| *The results **show**...* | *It is **suggested that** the results show...* | only if somebody said so |
| *nobody knows* | *it **is not yet known** whether...* | the same claim, and the passive is more formal |

The text as written keeps *nobody knows*, and that is a deliberate choice:
it is a definite claim about the state of knowledge, and in this context it
is defensible. In a paper it would be *it has not yet been established
whether*, and part 7.3 explains why.

## Step 6: check the articles

| phrase | article | why |
|---|---|---|
| **the** report I wrote | the | already mentioned: you wrote it, so the reader knows which |
| **the** results in the first version | the | plural countable, specific |
| **the** measurements taken | the | specific set, already discussed |
| **the** department | the | unique within the organisation |
| **the** compound | the | specific, already discussed |
| **the** acid that was used | the | specific instance, already mentioned |
| **it** | — | pronoun, no article |
| **a** new set of results | a | a new one, not yet identified |
| **the** end of the month | the | the end is a single event |
| **nobody** | no article | a negative pronoun |
| **results** after *should be* | no article | abstract, plural, uncountable sense |

Eleven decisions, and nine of them would be wrong if you guessed.

## Step 7: check the word order and the modifiers

> *the measurements that were **never** checked **properly***

*Never* goes between the auxiliary and the main verb, which part 4.4
covers, and *properly* goes after the participle because it modifies
*checked* and not the whole clause.

> *a **new** set of results*

*New* is opinion, and opinion comes first. There is only one adjective
here, so the ordering rule is not tested, which is the recommended
default: one adjective, no order to get wrong.

## Step 8: read it aloud

The final test, and the one that catches what the checks missed.

Reading the text aloud: *"...and **a new set** of results should be
available before the end of the month."* The stress falls where a speaker
would put it. Nothing is a stumble.

If a sentence is hard to say, it is usually hard to understand, and the
reverse is also true often enough to be worth trusting. Part 0.3 makes the
recording a daily item for this reason.

<DeepDive>
What this chapter teaches, stated generally, is that grammar is a set of
**decisions taken in a particular order**, not a set of rules to be
recalled.

The order matters and it is the same every time:

1. **Content.** What is being said. No grammar yet.
2. **Time.** Where does each event sit relative to now, and relative to
   each other. This decides the tenses, and it must come before the voice
   because the tense and the voice are chosen together in English.
3. **Voice.** Is the doer the news, or is the receiver the news.
4. **Structure.** Can a clause be reduced, and which pronoun can go.
5. **Calibration.** How strong is the evidence, and therefore the claim.
6. **Detail.** Articles, word order, prepositions, spelling.
7. **The ear.** Read it aloud.

Two learners can both know every rule in this book and produce different
results, and the difference is usually that one of them starts at step 2
and the other starts at step 6. The one who starts at step 2 writes *the
results that were in the first version have been removed* and the one who
starts at step 6 writes *the results in the first version was removed*,
and both are aware of every rule involved.

The practical consequence for a learner is that when a sentence goes wrong,
the useful question is not "which rule did I break" but "which step did I
skip". The repair is to go back to the top of the list, not to look up the
rule again. Looking up the rule again is what produces the learner who
knows fourteen errors and makes them, which is the result in part 8.6.
</DeepDive>

## Try it yourself

Take a paragraph you wrote this week and put it through all eight steps,
in order. Write down the step where it broke. That step is the part of
English you are weakest at, and it is a specific answer rather than a
feeling.
