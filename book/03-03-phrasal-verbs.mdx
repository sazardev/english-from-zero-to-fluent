# Phrasal verbs

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle that together mean something
neither part means alone. They are not idioms and they are not slang. They
are the ordinary verb vocabulary of English, and a learner who avoids them
is stuck at the level of a textbook.

| verb | particle | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *give up* | | stop, abandon |
| *look after* | | take care of |
| *put off* | | postpone |
| *find out* | | discover |
| *turn down* | | refuse, or lower the volume |
| *get away with* | | escape a consequence |

## The three patterns

Every phrasal verb is one of three shapes, and the position of the object
depends on the shape.

| type | pattern | example |
|---|---|---|
| separable | verb + particle + object | *turn the lights **off*** |
| inseparable | verb + particle + object | *look **after** the dog* |
| verb + preposition | verb + preposition + object | *depend **on** the answer* |

**Separable** means the object can go between the two parts.

> *Turn off the lights.* / *Turn the lights off.* ✓
> *Look after your sister.* / *Look your sister after.* ✗

With a pronoun it must go between, because a pronoun cannot follow a
particle and still sound English.

> ✓ *Turn **it** off.* / ✗ *Turn off **it**.*
> ✓ *Look after **her**.* / ✗ *Look **her** after.*

With a noun, both positions work, and the difference is emphasis.

> *Turn **off the lights**.* — the action is the point
> *Turn **the lights off**.* — the lights are the point

**Inseparable** verbs are written as one item in a dictionary and always
keep the particle attached.

> *look after*, *look into*, *look up*, *come across*, *deal with*,
> *catch up with*, *stand out for*

**Verb + preposition** pairs take a preposition, and the preposition is
part of the verb. Nothing can come between.

> *depend on*, *rely on*, *insist on*, *apologise for*, *apply for*,
> *account for*, *blame somebody for*, *congratulate somebody on*

<Warning>
The rule that catches most learners is the pronoun one, and it is worth
treating as a reflex: **a phrasal verb and a pronoun do not go next to
each other.**

> ✓ *I need to turn **it** off.*
> ✗ *I need to turn off **it**.*

> ✓ *Please look after **him**.*
> ✗ *Please look **him** after.*

> ✓ *She gets on well with **them**.*
> ✗ *She gets well on **them**.*

This is not a stylistic preference. *Turn off it* is a construction that
does not exist in English, and a listener will hear it as a mistake before
the sentence ends.
</Warning>

## Position, and what it changes

With a separable verb, moving the particle changes the emphasis, and
sometimes the meaning.

> *Turn **off the lights**.* — the lights are what you are acting on
> *Turn **the lights** off.* — the same act, the lights as the topic
> *Turn off **the lights in the hall and the lights in the kitchen**.* —
> the scope is what follows

> *Put **the book down**.* — set it on a surface
> *Put down **the book**.* — the same act, the book as the topic
> *Put **the book down** and pick **it up** again.*

> *Look **the word up**.* — the word is the topic
> *Look up **the word**.* — the same act, the lookup is the topic

The practical rule: if the thing acted on is short and the particle
carries the meaning, put the object between. If the object is long, put it
after.

## Phrasal verbs that are not idioms

A large group are compositional: the parts mean what they say.

> *sit down* ← sit + down
> *stand up* ← stand + up
> *lie down* ← lie + down
> *pick up* ← pick + up
> *grow up* ← grow + up
> *come in* ← come + in
> *go out* ← go + out
> *move back* ← move + back
> *take off* ← take + off *(clothes)* / *(planes, meaning depart)*
> *put on* ← put + on
> *try on* ← try + on
> *take after* ← take + after *(a relative)*
> *come from* ← come + from

Learn these as their sum. They need no memorising and they transfer
immediately to compounds you have never seen: *sit down, lie down, stand
up, pick up, put on, take off, try on, turn on, turn off, switch on,
switch off, get in, get out, get on, get off, come in, go in, go out.*

<DeepDive>
A useful fact about English: a large number of phrasal verbs exist only
because the language had to fill a gap. A language that marks tense and
mood on the verb does not need *come in* for the plain present, because
*comes* is already marked. English has almost no inflection, so the tense
and the meaning are both carried by extra words, and the extra words
become phrasal verbs.

That is why so many of them are about position and state, where English
has no other way to say it.

> *in* and *out* — movement, because English verbs do not change
> *on* and *off* — activation, because English verbs do not change
> *up* and *down* — direction, same reason
> *over* and *under* — position, same reason

Once you see the pattern, the meaning of an unfamiliar phrasal verb is
guessable from its particle, which is a real gain when you meet one you
have never been taught.
</DeepDive>

## The phrasal verbs that are genuinely idiomatic

These do not decompose, and they have to be learned.

| phrasal verb | meaning | a common wrong guess |
|---|---|---|
| *put up with* | tolerate | ✗ tolerate up |
| *get away with* | escape a penalty | ✗ escape away |
| *look into* | investigate | ✗ look inside |
| *look forward to* | await with pleasure | ✗ look ahead |
| *come across* | meet or find by chance | ✗ come across to |
| *stand out for* | represent | ✗ be excellent |
| *take after* | resemble a parent | ✗ follow |
| *get on with* | have a good relationship | ✗ continue |
| *catch up with* | reach the same level | ✗ seize |
| *run out of* | have no more | ✗ run outside of |
| *make up* | invent, or become friends again | ✗ fabricate makeup |
| *make up for* | compensate | ✗ invent |
| *turn down* | refuse | ✗ reduce |
| *turn up* | arrive, or increase | ✗ discover |
| *give in* | surrender | ✗ hand inside |
| *bring up* | raise a child | ✗ lift |
| *work out* | solve, or exercise | ✗ calculate precisely |
| *turn out* | prove to be | ✗ extinguish |
| *come out* | be published, or result | ✗ exit |
| *rule out* | eliminate as impossible | ✗ impose a rule outside |

<Warning>
The dangerous ones are the pairs where both meanings exist and they
contrast.

> *He **turned down** the offer.* — refused
> *He **turned down** the radio.* — lowered the volume

> *She **turned up** at eight.* — arrived
> *She **turned up** the heat.* — increased it

> *It **turned out** to be a fraud.* — proved to be
> *She **turned out** the light.* — switched off

> *I **worked out** the answer.* — solved it
> *I **worked out** at the gym.* — exercised

> *The machine **turned off**.* — stopped running
> *He **turned off** the machine.* — stopped it

The pattern: one meaning is intransitive, the other transitive. The
sentence with a direct object is the "doing something to something"
meaning. That is a reliable guess for a phrasal verb you have not met.
</Warning>

## Learning them in a way that sticks

Phrasal verbs are stored badly by dictionaries, as an alphabetical list,
and stored well as a list of situations. Group them by what you do.

**Starting and stopping.** *start up, set up, shut down, wind down, break
down, wear out, burn out*

**Moving and arranging.** *get on, get off, get in, get out, get along,
pull off, pull up, drop off, drop in, drop out, check in, check out, sign
in, sign up, log in, log out*

**Obtaining and giving up.** *give up, give in, give away, hand over, take
over, pass on, turn down, back out, opt out*

**Dealing with.** *deal with, come across, run into, get away with, put
up with, look into, sort out, work out, iron out, get round to, see to,
account for, answer for*

**Becoming and stopping being.** *take on, take off, put on, put off, turn
on, turn off, bring up, grow up, wake up, cheer up, calm down, cool off*

That is a few dozen verbs organised by the situation in which you would
need them, which is the only order in which you will ever use them.

## Four things learners get wrong about phrasal verbs

**Separable verbs with a pronoun.** ✗ *Turn off it.* → ✓ *Turn it off.*

**Inventing a meaning from the particles.** ✗ *I look forward to see
you.* → ✓ *I look forward to seeing you.* ✗ *Put up with is tolerate.* →
✓ it is, but only because that is what the dictionary says.

**Confusing the phrasal verb with the phrasal preposition.** *across from*,
*instead of*, *because of*, *next to* are prepositions, not verbs. There
is no verb in them.

> ✓ *I sat **next to** her.*
> ✗ *I sat next her.*

**Dropping the particle in formal writing.** It is not informal. *Find out*
is more standard than *determine*, not less.
