# False friends

A false friend is a word that looks familiar, sounds familiar, and means
something else. Most of them come from Latin, because English borrowed
heavily from Latin and French and then kept the form while the meaning
moved.

## The ones that cause the most damage

| word | looks like it means | actually means |
|---|---|---|
| *actually* | currently, right now | **really**; and in speech, *actually* often means *by the way* |
| *eventually* | at some time | **finally**, and definitely not soon |
| *sensible* | sensitive | **sane, showing good judgement** |
| *sensitive* | sensible | **easily affected, easily offended** |
| *familiar* | well known | **known from experience** |
| *library* | books | **a place that lends books** |
| *novel* | new | **a long story** |
| *carpet* | a small rug | **a floor covering for a whole room** |
| *gossip* | idle talk | **talk about other people's private lives** |
| *historic* | from long ago | **so important it will be remembered** |
| *classic* | typical of a bad situation | **of recognised quality** |
| *guess* | an inaccurate estimate | **to work something out correctly** |
| *resume* | begin again | **to start again after an interruption** |
| *convict* | find guilty | **to find guilty of a crime**; the *convict* is the person |
| *confront* | face a meeting | **to challenge, or to bring face to face with** |
| *ignore* | be unaware of | **deliberately pay no attention** |
| *discover* | invent | **to find something that already exists** |
| *manufacture* | make by hand | **to produce, and to fabricate a claim** |
| *modest* | small, humble | **not large; also, reluctant to boast** |
| *obvious* | a plain fact | **easy to see or understand** |
| *patient* | a doctor's client | **able to wait calmly** |
| *poll* | a survey | **a count of votes** |

<Warning>
**Eventually** and **finally** are the pair that causes the most real
damage, because using *eventually* when you mean *at some time* is
understood by everybody as *eventually* in its strict sense, and the
sentence says something much worse.

> *The bus **eventually** arrived.* — it arrived, after a long wait
> *The bus **eventually arrived**.* — ✓ same

> *I will **eventually** visit you.* — ambiguous in writing, and a reader
> will take "at some point in the distant future", which can sound like a
> brush-off
> *I will **visit you one day**.* — no implication of delay
> *I will **eventually get around to visiting you**.* — honest, and it
> admits the delay

A learner who says *I will eventually finish this* meaning *I will finish
this* has told the listener not to expect it soon, in a sentence that
looked entirely neutral.

**Actually** is the second one, and it has drifted a long way.

> *It is **actually** quite good.* — really
> *Actually*, it was John who called. — by the way
> *I have **actually** finished it.* — really, and with some emphasis
> ✗ *I have actually finished at four o'clock.* — meaning "currently", which
> the word does not mean
</Warning>

## Latinate pairs that invert

Several pairs work in the opposite direction from what the forms suggest,
and they are the ones a learner is most confident about.

| word | the trap | the truth |
|---|---|---|
| *deceive* | looks like *receive* | it is the opposite: **to mislead** |
| *enquire* | looks like *acquire* | it is to **ask** |
| *imagine* | looks like *manage* | to **picture in the mind** |
| *resemble* | looks like *assemble* | to **look like** |
| *elicit* | looks like *illicit* | to **draw out**; *illicit* is illegal |
| *disburse* | looks like *disburse* | to **pay out money**; *disburse* vs *disperse* |
| *peruse* | looks like *pursue* | to **read carefully**, and it is rare |
| *cavetto* | — | *cavetto* is an architectural feature; *caveat* is a warning |

The first two are the ones that actually cause mistakes, and both are the
result of Latin prefixes having lost their force in English. *De-* still
means reversal in *descend, decrease, depress*, but in *deceive* it is
gone entirely, and a learner who reasons from the prefix will get it
exactly backwards.

## The *-ful* and *-less* inversions

Part 3 covered this, and it is repeated here because the false friends
in this group are the ones a learner meets as ordinary vocabulary.

| word | looks like | actually |
|---|---|---|
| *fearless* | unable to fear | not afraid. ✓ |
| *fearsome* | full of fear | **frightening**, and often of a thing that inspires fear |
| *thoughtless* | without thought | **careless**. ✗ |
| *thankful* | full of thanks | **grateful**. ✓ |
| *grateful* | full of thanks | ✓ |
| *useless* | without use | ✓ |
| *hopeful* | full of hope | ✓ |
| *helpful* | full of help | ✓ |

The one to watch is *fearsome*, which in American English can mean
*excellent*, as in *a fearsome guitarist*. The senses have drifted far
enough that using it at all is a choice.

## Words that are more limited than they look

**Decide.** Not *decide about* or *decide of*.

> ✓ *I decided **to leave**.* / ✓ *I decided **against** it.* / ✓ *I
> decided **on** the blue one.*
> ✗ *I decided **of** leaving.* → ✓ *I decided **on** leaving.*
> ✗ *I decided **for** leaving.* → ✓ *I decided **to** leave.*

**Discuss.** Not *discuss about*.

> ✓ *We discussed **the plan**.* / ✓ *We discussed **how to do it**.*
> ✗ *We discussed **about** the plan.*

This one is extremely frequent in learner English and is always marked,
because *discuss* is a transitive verb and takes no preposition.

**Contact.** As a verb it is transitive.

> ✓ *I contacted* her. ✓ *I got in touch with* her. ✓
> ✗ *I contacted **with** her.* — spoken, informal, marked in writing

**Explain.** Not *explain me*.

> ✓ *Could you explain **it** to me?* ✓ *Could you explain **what** it means?*
> ✗ *Could you explain **me** how it works?* — common in Spanish-speaking
> learners and always marked

<DeepDive>
Why false friends exist at all, and why the ones from Latin are the
dangerous ones.

English took about sixty per cent of its vocabulary from French and Latin
between the eleventh and the sixteenth centuries, mostly to write law,
theology and science, and the borrowing was done by people who already
knew Latin. The English words they brought in carried their Latin forms
into a language that had a completely different system for building
compounds, so the *de-* of *deceive* stopped meaning anything to an
English speaker even while the form survived.

The result is that the *forms* look systematic to a learner who knows
Latin and are arbitrary to a speaker of English, and the learner's
instinct to reason from the prefix is actively wrong in these cases.

The practical defence is worth knowing in general form: **if a word feels
logically predictable, check it in a dictionary before relying on the
logic.** The cases where a prefix is productive are the many, and they
deceive reliably; the cases where the prefix is fossilised are few, and
they are the ones that matter, because they are where being confidently
wrong costs most.

The list in this chapter is not exhaustive and cannot be. New borrowings
arrive constantly, and the ones that will trap a future learner are being
invented right now, by speakers who are not thinking about prefix
semantics at all.
</DeepDive>

## Four things learners get wrong with false friends

**A preposition added to a transitive verb.** ✗ *discuss about*, ✗
*contact with*, ✗ *explain me*.

***Eventually* for "at some time".** It means *finally*, and it implies a
delay.

**Reasoning from a prefix.** *deceive* is not *receive*; *enquire* is not
*acquire*.

**Expecting a new word to be predictable.** Novel, library, classic, guess
and manufacture all mean something narrower or wider than their forms
suggest.
