# Prepositions of place

English uses three prepositions where Spanish uses one, and the choice
carries meaning that Spanish leaves to context.

## in, at, on

| | place | time | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| in | enclosed space | long period | in the room, in Spain, **in** 2020, **in** the morning |
| at | a point | short period | at the door, at the station, **at** 3 o'clock, **at** night |
| on | a surface | a day | on the table, on the bus, **on** Monday, **on** 5 May |

<Warning>
The same preposition covers space and time, and the pattern is identical.
In a room and in 2020. At the door and at midnight. On the wall and on
Tuesday. Learn them as one idea, not two.
</Warning>

## The place rules

### in: enclosed, and countries and cities

> in the kitchen, in a box, in a car, in Spain, in Madrid, in the
> European Union

### at: a point, a building, an address

> at the bus stop, at the door, at work, at university, at 14 Calle Mayor

<DeepDive>
There is a real inconsistency here and there is no rule that covers it.
<br><br>
<i>in</i> the car but <i>at</i> the wheel.
<br><br>
<i>in</i> the building but <i>at</i> school.
<br><br>
<i>in</i> the country but <i>at</i> home.
<br><br>
<i>in</i> a city but <i>at</i> a village.
<br><br>
What is true is that <i>at</i> points at a specific location and <i>in</i>
surrounds you. "At the school" means at that address; "in the school" means
inside the building. The exceptions are conventional and have to be
learned, but the principle holds often enough to be a useful default.
</DeepDive>

### on: a surface, and anything with a surface

> on the wall, on the ceiling, on the floor, on a page, on the bus, on the
> train, on a ship

<Warning>
Public transport is <i>on</i> and the small vehicles are <i>in</i>.
<i>on the bus, train, plane, ship, bike</i> but <i>in the car, taxi, van</i>.
Learners get this exactly backwards in both directions and it sounds
markedly foreign.
</Warning>

<Warning>
The error Spanish produces most often is <em>among</em> and <em>between</em>.
<i>Between</i> is for two, always. <i>Between you and me</i>, <i>between
the two buildings</i>, <i>between 2 and 4</i>. <i>Among</i> is for three or
more, or for an open group: <i>among friends</i>, <i>among the results</i>.
</Warning>

## Prepositions of movement

| preposition | direction | from | to |
|---|---|---|---|
| into | in | out of | to inside |
| onto | on | off | to on top of |
| to | neutral | — | the place |
| from | neutral | — | away |
| through | within | — | the far side |
| across | over | — | the far side |
| past | by | — | beyond |
| toward(s) | towards | — | in the direction of |

> He walked **into** the room. *(in)*
> He jumped **onto** the table. *(on)*
> He walked **through** the room. *(stayed in it)*
> He walked **across** the room. *(crossed it)*

<DeepDive>
<em>Through</em> and <em>across</em> are the pair learners confuse most,
and the difference is about space rather than movement.
<br><br>
<i>Through the park</i> — the park is the medium; you were inside it
throughout.
<br><br>
<i>Across the park</i> — the park is the obstacle; you went from one side
to the other.
<br><br>
You swim <b>through</b> water. You walk <b>across</b> the water. The same
word cannot be used for both.
</DeepDive>

## Two prepositions in a row

Some phrases take two. They are learned as single units, because the
second one carries the meaning.

| phrase | meaning |
|---|---|
| depend **on** | rely |
| consist **of** | be made from |
| belong **to** | be owned by |
| happen **to** | occur, by chance |
| refer **to** | mention |
| object **to** | disagree formally |
| lead **to** | cause |
| be used **to** + -ing | be accustomed to |
| look forward **to** + -ing | anticipate |
| get used **to** + -ing | become accustomed |
| be opposed **to** | disagree with |
| in addition **to** | as well as |
| as opposed **to** | by contrast with |
| account **for** | explain |

<Warning>
The first three are not interchangeable with a simple verb. <i>It
depends on</i> and <i>it consists of</i> and <i>it belongs to</i> are
fixed. A learner who says <i>I depend of you</i> has produced a sentence
that no English speaker would recognise.
</Warning>

## The gerund trap

Some verbs take a preposition before a gerund, and the pair is fixed.

| verb + preposition | + gerund |
|---|---|
| look forward to | looking |
| object to | objecting |
| insist on | insisting |
| be used to | being used |
| get used to | getting used |
| be opposed to | being opposed |
| apologise for | apologising |
| be responsible for | being responsible |
| confess to | confessing |
| admit to | admitting |

> I look forward to **seeing** you. ✓
> I look forward to **see** you. ✗

<Warning>
The reason is structural. <em>To</em> in <em>look forward to</em> is a
preposition, not the infinitive marker, and a preposition is always
followed by a gerund.
<br><br>
This single error is in the top three for Spanish speakers, because
Spanish <em>estoy listo para verte</em> looks identical in structure and
<em>para</em> here really is an infinitive marker.
</Warning>
