# Relative clauses

A relative clause modifies a noun. It is introduced by a relative pronoun,
and the pronoun's job is to stand in for the noun without repeating it.

## The six pronouns

| pronoun | stands for | used after a preposition | possessive |
|---|---|---|---|
| *who* | people | ✓ | |
| *which* | things | ✓ | |
| *that* | people and things | ✗ | |
| *whom* | people, formal | ✓ | |
| *whose* | people and things | ✗ | ✓ |
| *where* | places | ✓ | |
| *when* | times | ✓ | |

The distinction is by **person, thing, place, time and possession**, not by
animacy, formality or meaning. Nothing more subtle is involved, and a
learner who looks for depth in *who* and *which* will not find it.

> *the man **who** lives next door* — a person
> *the book **which** I bought* — a thing
> *the city **where** I was born* — a place
> *the day **when** we met* — a time
> *the woman **whose** car was stolen* — possession
> *the man **whom** I met* — a person, formal, and only after a preposition

<Warning>
The rule that catches people is *that*. **A relative pronoun cannot
follow a preposition, and *that* is never used after one.**

> ✓ *the man **whom** I spoke **to***
> ✗ *the man **that** I spoke to* — in careful English
> ✓ *the car **which** I drove **in***
> ✗ *the car **that** I drove in*

*That* is the most useful relative pronoun in informal English because it
takes people and things, and it is the one a learner reaches for
everywhere. The one place it does not go is after a preposition, and
because the preposition must come last, the clause has to be split.

> *the film **which** I was watching* — fine
> *the film **that** I was watching* — fine
> *the film **in which** I appeared* — fine, and required here
> *the film **that** I appeared in* — acceptable informally
> ✗ *the film **that** I appeared in* — marked in writing

Note the movement: the preposition goes to the front, so the clause reads
*in which* and the sentence has to be built around it.
</Warning>

## Defining and non-defining

The distinction is not about which pronoun you use. It is about commas and
about what the clause is doing.

**Defining.** The clause says which noun you mean. No commas.

> *the man **who lives next door***
> *the book **that I borrowed***

Without the clause, the noun is not identified.

> *I spoke to the man.* — which man?
> *I spoke to the man **who lives next door**.* — now you know.

**Non-defining.** The clause adds extra information about a noun already
identified. Commas on both sides.

> *My brother, **who lives in Berlin**, is visiting next week.*
> *The book, **which I finished last week**, was excellent.*

<Warning>
A non-defining clause is set off by commas, and a defining clause is not.
Getting this wrong changes the meaning in a way that can be embarrassing.

> *The students **who had studied** passed the exam.*
> — only the students who had studied passed. The rest did not.

> *The students, **who had studied**, passed the exam.*
> — all the students had studied, and all of them passed.

The comma turns a restriction into a statement about everyone. This is one
of the few places in English where punctuation changes the logical content
of a sentence, and it is worth being careful with in writing.
</Warning>

## *That* or no pronoun

In formal English the pronoun can be omitted when it is the object of the
clause.

> *the book **(that) I borrowed***
> *the man **(whom) I met***

It cannot be omitted when it is the subject, because then the clause has
no subject at all.

> ✓ *the book **which** I borrowed* → can be *the book I borrowed*
> ✗ *the book I borrowed* is fine, because *I* is the subject
> ✓ *the book **that** was borrowed last week*
> ✗ *the book was borrowed last week by someone* — the clause has no
> subject

| position of the noun | can the pronoun be dropped |
|---|---|
| object of the clause | ✓ yes |
| subject of the clause | ✗ no |
| after a preposition | ✗ no |

## *Who* and *which* as subjects and objects

| position | who | which | that |
|---|---|---|---|
| subject | *the man **who** came* | *the thing **which** happened* | ✓ |
| object | *the man **whom** I met* | *the thing **which** I saw* | ✓ |
| possessive | *the man **whose** car* | *the thing **whose** cover* | ✓ |

*Whom* is the object form and it is vanishingly rare in speech. Almost
every native speaker says *who* for the object.

> *the man **who** I met* — spoken English, universal
> *the man **whom** I met* — formal, and correct

<Warning>
*Whom* after a preposition is the one place the distinction still holds in
writing, and it is worth using there because the alternative is worse.

> ✓ *To **whom** should I send the report?*
> ✗ *To who should I send the report?* — spoken, informal
> ✓ ***To** whom it may concern* — fixed phrase
> ✗ ***To** who it may concern* — heard, and it is a cliché of carelessness

The test for *whom*: if the pronoun is the object of a preposition or a
verb, and you are writing, use *whom*. If you are not sure, restructure so
the pronoun is not needed at all: *Who should I send the report to?*
</Warning>

## *Where* and *when*, and the *which* alternative

*Where* for places and *when* for times, followed by a clause.

> *the city **where** I was born*
> *the year **when** it happened*

An alternative with *which* plus a preposition, and the two are not always
interchangeable.

| form | example | difference |
|---|---|---|
| *where* | the city *where* I was born | identifies by event |
| *which* + preposition | the city *which* I was born **in** | identifies by location |
| *when* | the year *when* it happened | identifies by time |
| *which* + preposition | the year *which* it happened **in** | identifies by period |

> *This is the house **where** I grew up.* — the event
> *This is the house **which** I grew up **in**.* — the location
> *This is the year **when** the war ended.* — the event
> *This is the year **which** the war ended **in**.* — the period

The pair with *which* is slightly more precise and slightly more formal,
and in a 6x9 book the difference is worth having.

## Possessives with *whose*

*Whose* replaces a possessive noun phrase, and it applies to things as well
as people, which surprises people who expect it to be for people only.

> *the woman **whose** car was stolen* — a person
> *the book **whose** cover I designed* — a thing
> *the man **whose** father was famous* — a person
> *the theory **whose** implications are unclear* — a thing

*Whose* can be followed by a clause as well as a noun.

> *an author **whose** books I have never read*
> *a decision **whose** consequences nobody predicted*

<DeepDive>
There is a subject ambiguity in *whose* that no punctuation can fix, and
it is worth knowing about because it appears in academic prose.

> *The author **that** the critics praised **was** controversial.*

Two readings: the critics were controversial, or the author was. When the
relative pronoun is the subject and the main verb can attach to either
noun, the sentence is ambiguous.

> *The book **that** he mentioned **was** excellent.* — the book was
> excellent, or he was?

The fix is to make one of the two subjects unmistakable, usually by using
*which* for the thing.

> *The book, **which** he mentioned, **was** excellent.* — now the book is
> the subject of *was*.

*That* after a noun is the ambiguous case, and *which* resolves it because
*which* refers only to things. In careful writing, the rule is: use *that*
for people, *which* for things, and reserve *that* for things only when
there is no ambiguity.
</DeepDive>

## Chains of clauses

A noun can take more than one clause, and the order is fixed: the shorter,
more defining clause comes first.

> *the man **who lives next door** **whose car was stolen** yesterday*

The second clause is relative to *the man*, not to *next door*, and the
order puts the identifying information first.

<Warning>
**A relative clause cannot follow an indefinite pronoun.** The noun it
refers to has to be identifiable, and *a*, *some*, *any* and *every* make
it impossible.

> ✗ *A book **which I bought yesterday** is on the table.*
> ✓ ***The** book **which I bought yesterday** is on the table.*

> ✗ *Someone **who was late** left.*
> ✓ ***Somebody* else**, or *Someone **that** was late* — and even that is
> better avoided.

> ✗ *Every student **who studies hard** succeeds.*
> ✓ ***Every student who studies hard succeeds.*** ✓ — *every* is fine,
> because it identifies the group

The rule is not about *every* and *all*. It is about whether the noun
identifies someone in particular. *A man who was tall walked in* is broken
because *a* could be anyone. *Every man who was tall walked in* is fine
because the set is already fixed.
</Warning>

## Four things learners get wrong

**Two coordinators.** ✗ *I was tired but however I went.* → *I was tired,
however, I went.*

**A comma in a defining clause.** ✗ *The students, who had studied,
passed.* → this says they all had studied.

**A dropped pronoun in subject position.** ✗ *The book was bought last
week.* → *The book **that was** bought last week.*

**A relative clause after *a* or *some*.** → *the*, or restructure.
