# The future

English has no future tense. What it has is a set of ways to point at a
time that has not happened yet, and the choice between them is a choice
about commitment, evidence and distance, not about the calendar.

## Five future forms

| form | example | what it commits to |
|---|---|---|
| *will* | I *will call* you. | a decision now |
| *be going to* | I *am going to call* you. | a decision already made |
| present simple | The train *leaves* at six. | a schedule |
| present continuous | I *am meeting* her at six. | a fixed arrangement |
| *be about to* | I *am about to leave*. | imminence |

Choosing between them is the whole skill.

## *Will* and intention

**A decision formed in the moment of speaking.** The speaker had not
decided before now.

> *I'll* have the coffee, please.
> I think it *will* rain.
> The door *will* open at nine.

**A prediction**, with no claim about anyone's plans.

> She *will* be forty next year.
> Most people *will* forget this in a year.

**An offer, a promise, a refusal, a request.**

> *I'll* carry that for you.
> I *won't* forget.
> *Will* you help me?
> I *won't* do that again.

<Warning>
*Will* is a decision at the moment of speaking. If the decision exists
already, it is not *will*.

> I've already decided. **I am going to** study medicine.
> ✗ I've already decided. I will study medicine. *(sounds like you are
> deciding while you say it)*

> The government has announced the change. It **will** take effect in June.
> ✓ *(announced, so the decision is public and future to the listener)*

The test: could you have made this decision yesterday? If yes, it is not a
decision now, and *will* misrepresents it.
</Warning>

### *Will* has no *-s*

*Will* is one of the very few verbs whose third person is unmarked.

> He *will* come. / She *will* come. / They *will* come. ✓
> ✗ She *wills* come.

*Would* is the same: *he would, she would, they would*.

This matters because a learner who has spent a year putting *-s* on third
person verbs will reach for *she wills*, and the error is instantly
recognisable.

## *Be going to*

**A plan formed before now.**

> I *am going to* run a marathon.
> We *are going to* move to Lisbon.

**Evidence for a prediction.** Something already visible makes the future
likely.

> Look at those clouds. It *is going to* rain.
> She *is going to* have a baby in June.

**A prediction based on present facts, with no evidence in the scene.**

> It *is going to* be a long week.
> You *are going to* be sorry.

The evidence reading is the one worth drilling, because the difference
from *will* is invisible otherwise. The test: is there something you can
point at, right now, that makes it more likely?

| situation | tense | example |
|---|---|---|
| clouds outside, darkening | *is going to* | *It is going to rain.* |
| no evidence, opinion | *will* | *It will rain tomorrow.* |
| a promise made now | *will* | *I will help you.* |
| a promise made weeks ago | *is going to* | *I am going to help you.* |
| an intention, decided | *am going to* | *She is going to resign.* |
| a reaction to news | *will* | *She will be furious.* |

<DeepDive>
*Be going to* is built on *be* + *going to* + base, so its questions and
negatives put the auxiliary first. That is why the third person singular
looks the same as the first person, which is not true of *will* either:
both are frozen.

> I *am going to* leave. / She *is going to* leave.
> *Am* I going to leave? / *Is* she going to leave?
> I *am not going to* leave. / She *is not going to* leave.

The *-s* lands on *is* and nowhere else. The main verb never takes *-s*
after a modal or an auxiliary, which is worth stating as its own rule
because it is the same rule that keeps *doesn't work* and not *doesn't
works*.
</DeepDive>

## Timetables and arrangements

When the time is fixed before you speak, the present does the work.

**Present simple** for timetables, departures, schedules, recurring
published times.

> The train *leaves* at 6:40.
> The shop *opens* at nine on weekdays.
> The film *starts* at half past seven.

**Present continuous** for personal arrangements, appointments, meetings
someone has agreed to.

> I *am meeting* the dentist at eleven.
> We *are having* dinner with them on Saturday.
> She *is coming* to the party.

<Warning>
This is a real distinction and it is worth getting right, because the same
sentence can be true of two different situations.

> I *see* the doctor on Friday. — the appointment is in the diary
> I *am seeing* the doctor on Friday. — the appointment is in the diary
> and I have a personal stake in it

When you are not sure, the present continuous is safer in speech, because
it sounds like a personal arrangement rather than a public timetable.
</Warning>

## *Be about to*

Something is about to happen, usually within seconds.

> I *am about to* leave. / *I'm about to* order.
> Careful, the film *is about to* start.

It takes *to* + base, and it is nearly always about the immediate present,
never a distant one.

> ✓ I am about to fall. ✓ I am about to call him.
> ✗ I am about to retire. *(too distant; this is a plan, not imminence)*

## The future in the past

Everything above is available in the past, and the meanings shift
accordingly.

| present | past | example |
|---|---|---|
| *will* | *would* | I thought I *would* be there. |
| *is going to* | *was going to* | I *was going to* call, but I forgot. |
| present simple | past simple | The train *left* at six. |
| present continuous | past continuous | I *was meeting* him at eleven. |

The past *going to* is almost always followed by something that went
wrong, because the plan did not survive contact with reality.

> I *was going to* study abroad, but I got a job.
> She *was going to* say something, then she changed her mind.

That is a genre in itself, and it is a favourite of spoken English.

<Warning>
*Would* is not a tense. It is a conditional auxiliary that also carries
past *will*, and the two uses overlap enough to cause errors.

**Past *will*, habitual.** *would* for a repeated past action.

> When I was a child, we *would* go to the beach every August.
> ✓ *(used to and would both work)*

**Conditional.** *would* for a hypothetical.

> If I *were* taller, I *would* be a model. ✗ *If I was taller* — for
> unreal situations it is *were*.

**Polite offer.** *Would* is softer than *will*.

> *Would* you mind closing the window? *(polite)*
> *Will* you mind closing the window? *(less polite, sharper)*

The test that separates them: if the sentence is about a repeated past
situation with no *if* and no unreal condition, it is past *will*.
</Warning>

## Future forms, side by side

> **I will call** you. *(decided now)*
> **I am going to call** you. *(decided earlier)*

> **I am meeting** her at six. *(arrangement, in the diary)*
> **The train leaves** at six. *(timetable)*

> **It is going to rain.** *(dark clouds, evidence)*
> **It will rain** tomorrow. *(prediction, no evidence)*

> **I am about to leave.** *(within seconds)*

> **I would call** you. *(past habit, or a polite offer, or a condition)*

## Choosing, in order

Ask four questions and stop at the first yes.

1. **Is the time fixed in advance and published?** Present simple.
2. **Is it a personal arrangement with a time?** Present continuous.
3. **Is something visible or already known making it likely?** *Be going
   to.*
4. **Is the decision being made as I speak, or is it a prediction, an
   offer, a promise or a refusal?** *Will.*

If none of them fits, *will* is the default. When in doubt in speech,
*will* is almost never wrong.
