# Participles

A participle is a verb form doing a job no other form can do. There are
two of them, the *-ing* form and the *-ed* form, and between them they
cover almost every continuous and perfect tense in English.

## The two participles

| form | name | example |
|---|---|---|
| *-ing* | present participle | *the **running** water* |
| *-ed* | past participle | *the **broken** window* |

*Running* is both the present participle of *run* and an adjective. That
ambiguity is not a defect; it is why English can build sentences this
economically.

## The participle as an adjective

A participle before a noun describes the noun. A participle after a verb
describes the subject.

> the *running* water → the water is running
> a *broken* window → the window is broken
> *I saw a **running** train.* → the train was running
> *I saw a **broken** window.* → the window was broken

<Warning>
The two participles are not interchangeable, and the difference is
finished versus unfinished, exactly as in the tenses.

> ✗ the *broken* car → ✓ the *breaking* car *(it is in the process)*
> ✗ the *running* water → ✓ the *run* water *(it has been run)*

For a small number of verbs both participles describe a resulting state
with no time in them at all, and there the choice is idiomatic rather than
logical.

> *A **broken** leg* — the leg is broken now
> *A **breaking** sound* — the sound is happening now
> *A **grown** adult* — an adult who has grown
> *A **growing** child* — a child who is growing
> *A **fallen** angel* / *a **falling** leaf*
> *A **hidden** camera* / *a **hiding** place*
> *A **lost** cause* / *a **losing** streak*
</Warning>

## Reduced relatives

A participle phrase can replace a relative clause, and this is the single
most useful thing about participles in writing.

| full | reduced |
|---|---|
| the woman *who is standing* there | the woman *standing* there |
| the man *who was injured* in the crash | the man *injured* in the crash |
| the book *which was written* in 1954 | the book *written* in 1954 |
| the road *that leads* to town | the road *leading* to town |

The rule that decides *-ing* against *-ed* is active against passive.

**Active → *-ing*.** The person named is doing the action.

> the *students **taking** the exam* — the students are taking it
> the *people **waiting** outside* — the people are waiting
> the *engineers **responsible** for the design* — the engineers are
> responsible

**Passive → *-ed*.** The person or thing named is being acted on.

> the *question **asked** by the interviewer* — it was asked
> the *report **written** in 2019* — it was written
> the *damage **caused** by the storm* — it was caused

<Warning>
The passive reading is the one learners get wrong, and the error produces
a sentence that says the opposite of what was meant.

> the *man **killed** in the accident* → he died
> ✗ the man *killing* in the accident → he was doing the killing

> the *photos **taken** by my father* → my father took them
> ✗ the photos *taking* by my father → nonsense, and a native reader
> assumes the writer meant something obscene

> the *crime **committed** in 1994* → someone committed it
> ✗ the crime *committing* in 1994 → the crime was committing something

*Take*, *commit*, *kill*, *break* and *build* are the verbs where this
matters most, because the participles sound like active words.
</Warning>

## Participles and the perfect

A past participle after *have* or *be* is doing the tense work. This is
the whole mechanism.

> *is **written*** → present simple passive
> *was **written*** → past simple passive
> *has been **written*** → present perfect passive
> *was being **written*** → past continuous passive
> *is being **written*** → present continuous passive

The participle never changes. Only the auxiliary moves. That is why the
passive is easy once the auxiliary is understood and hard while it is not.

## Participles as adverbs

A participle phrase can replace an adverb phrase, and it does so more
concisely.

> He spoke *very quietly*. → He spoke *in a low voice*.
> She left *in a hurry*. → She left *hurriedly*.
> They listened *with great care*. → They listened *attentively*.

> *Not knowing what to say, he said nothing.* — he said nothing because he
> did not know what to say
> *Having finished the report, she went home.* — she went home after
> finishing it

### The dangling participle

The subject of a participle phrase has to be the subject of the main
clause. When it is not, the phrase dangles, and the result is either
nonsense or a change of meaning.

> ✓ **Walking** to the station, **she** dropped her keys.
> ✗ **Walking** to the station, **her keys** dropped. *(keys do not walk)*

> ✓ **Having reviewed** the data, **the model** produced better results.
> ✗ **Having reviewed** the data, **better results** were produced. *(the
> results did not review)*

> ✗ *Looking at the requirements carefully, **they** seem straightforward.
> — what seems straightforward: the requirements, or they? The reader
> cannot tell.

<Warning>
A dangling participle is the most common error in edited academic
prose and it survives publication, because the sentence still parses. Fix
it by making the subject of the participle the subject of the main verb,
or by turning the participle phrase into a full clause.

> *Looking at the requirements carefully*, they seem straightforward.
> → *When you look at the requirements carefully*, they seem
> straightforward.
> → *The requirements seem straightforward **when looked at** carefully.*

Both fixes are longer. That is the price, and it is worth paying, because
the second version is the one that survives review.
</Warning>

## Verb + -ing, and why the form matters

Many verbs are followed by a noun. When that noun is a gerund, the
sentence can mean two different things depending on who is doing the
action.

> *I saw **the man** **shooting** a gun.* — I watched him shoot
> *I saw **the man** **being shot**.* — I watched him be shot
> *I saw **the gun** **shooting** a man.* — nonsense; the gun cannot shoot

> *I heard **her** **playing** the piano.* — she played
> *I heard **her piano being played**.* — someone or something else played

> *I had **the car** **repaired**.* — I arranged it
> *I had **the car** **breaking down**.* — the car broke down

The *-ing* form puts the subject in control. The *-ed* form puts the
subject in the hands of someone else. The difference is who is acting, and
it is worth mastering because it is the difference between arranging
something and suffering it.

## Absolute constructions

A participle phrase can have its own subject, detached from the main
clause. The subject is then a noun phrase, not a pronoun, and the whole
thing is called absolute.

> *The weather **permitting**, we will sail on Sunday.*
> *Her son **living abroad**, she visited him twice a year.*
> *The task **finished**, we went for a walk.*
> *The door **open**, he walked straight in.*

This is a formal and literary structure. It is common in journalism and
rare in speech, and it is worth recognising when reading rather than
producing.

<DeepDive>
The three participles you meet most often in serious writing are *having*,
*being* and *being* used absolutely, and each carries a different logical
relation to the main clause.

> *Having **finished**, she left.* — she finished, then she left. Cause
> precedes effect.

> *Being **careless**, he lost the file.* — because he was careless, he
> lost it. Character precedes result.

> *The file, **having been lost**, was replaced.* — the file was lost
> first. This is the absolute, because the subject is *the file* and the
> participle is *having been lost*, and the two are separated by a comma.

The pattern to notice: *having* + participle points backwards, and *being*
+ adjective points forwards. Once that is automatic, the absolute
construction stops looking like a foreign trick and starts looking like
what it is: a way of putting two facts in the right order without a
subordinate clause.
</DeepDive>
