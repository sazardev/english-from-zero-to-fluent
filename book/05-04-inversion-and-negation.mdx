# Inversion and negation

Inversion puts the verb before the subject. It is the oldest construction
in the language, it survives in about thirty fixed forms, and it is the
mark of formal and literary English.

## Normal and inverted order

English has a default order and a marked one.

> **Subject + verb.** *She had never seen such a thing.*

> **Verb + subject.** *Never had she seen such a thing.*

Inversion is not available for every sentence. It needs a particular
element at the front, and that element has to be a negative or restrictive
word, or a phrase that is not the subject.

<Warning>
The learner version of this rule is almost always wrong in the same way:
fronting a negative adverb without inverting the verb.

> ✗ *Never I had seen such a thing.*
> ✓ *Never **had I** seen such a thing.*

> ✗ *Not only she was late, but she was rude.*
> ✓ *Not only **was she** late, but she was rude.*

> ✗ *Rarely I have seen such courage.*
> ✓ *Rarely **have I** seen such courage.*

The fronted word is not the subject, so the verb has to come before the
subject to keep the clause grammatical. This is not a stylistic flourish;
it is required once the word is at the front.
</Warning>

## What can be inverted

**Negative and restrictive adverbs.** *never, rarely, seldom, hardly,
scarcely, barely, not only, no sooner, nowhere, not until*

> ***Rarely** does** she complain.*
> ***Hardly had** I arrived **when** the phone rang.*
> ***Nowhere** are** the stars brighter.*
> ***Seldom** have** I seen such a mess.*

***No sooner* and *not until*.** These take *than* and *when*.

> ***No sooner had** I sat down **than** the phone rang.*
> ***Not until** the meeting ended **did** he speak.*

**Questions with *who*, *what*, *which*, etc.** Inversion is normal in
questions, so this is the form every learner already knows.

> *Who **wrote** this?* / *What **happened**?*

**Conditional clauses without *if*.** An alternative to *if*.

> ***Were** I you, I would decline.*
> ***Should** you need help, ring me.*
> ***Had** I known, I would have come.*

**Adverbials moved to the front for emphasis.** Formal, and increasingly
rare.

> *On the table **lay** a book.*
> *Beyond the wall **stood** a tower.*
> *Into the room **walked** a stranger.*

## The *no sooner* pattern

One pattern is worth learning as a whole, because it is fixed and the
learner's version is always wrong in the same way.

> ***No sooner had** I sat down **than** the phone rang.*
> ***No sooner** had **the** plane **landed than** they **applauded**.

Four elements: *no sooner*, the past perfect, *than*, the past simple. And
the two halves cannot be swapped: *no sooner* takes *than*, and *not until*
takes *until* or a clause.

> ✓ No sooner had he arrived than he phoned.
> ✗ No sooner had he arrived when he phoned.
> ✓ Not until he arrived did he phone.
> ✗ Not until he arrived he phoned.

<Warning>
*No sooner* and *hardly* are fixed phrases that take inversion. Learners
write them as ordinary adverbs and the sentence collapses.

> ✗ *Hardly I had arrived than the phone rang.* — two errors: no
> inversion, and *hardly* takes *when*
> ✓ *Hardly **had I** arrived **when** the phone rang.*

> ✗ *No sooner he arrived than he left.*
> ✓ *No sooner **had he** arrived **than** he left.*
</Warning>

## Negation

English has one true negating word, *not*, and a set of words that negate
by carrying their own negative meaning.

### *Not*

*Not* goes before the word it negates, and it can go in several places.

> *I **do not** know.* — the verb
> *I know **him not**.* — old-fashioned, and formal
> *He is **not** here.*
> ***Not** everyone came.* — the negative is on the quantifier
> *It is **not** very cold.*

The last two are the interesting ones. A negative quantifier is stronger
than a negated verb, and a negated adverb is a hedge.

> ***Not many** people came.* — few came
> ✗ *Many people did not come.* — the same fact, and a much weaker claim,
> because it is compatible with most people coming
> ***Not very** cold.* — mild
> *Not cold at all.* — strong

### Negative words that are not *not*

| word | affirmative equivalent |
|---|---|
| *none* | zero, not any |
| *nobody, no one* | nobody, not anyone |
| *nothing* | zero, not anything |
| *nowhere* | not in any place |
| *never* | at no time, not ever |
| *neither, nor* | not either |
| *few, little* | not many, not much |
| *rarely, seldom* | often |
| *hardly, barely, scarcely* | almost |
| *cannot* | can not |

<DeepDive>
*Few* and *little* are the pair that changes a claim's strength, and it is
the single most useful thing to know about English negation.

> ***Few** students passed.* — almost none. A strong claim.
> ***A few** students passed.* — some. A weak claim, and it contradicts the
> previous sentence.
> ***Only a few** students passed.* — some, and the surprise is the point.

> ***Little** progress was made.* — almost none. Strong.
> ***A little** progress was made.* — some. Weak.

The four-word pattern is a complete system for expressing quantity with
precision, and it is more than most native speakers use.

| | almost none | some | all |
|---|---|---|---|
| count nouns | *few* | *a few* | *many* |
| mass nouns | *little* | *a little* | *much* |
| with a noun | *few students* | *a few students* | — |
| adding emphasis | *very few* | *only a few* | — |

Three errors follow from not knowing it, and all three are common in
learner writing.

> ✗ *Few people came to the party, it was very boring.*
> — This is ambiguous. It can mean "few came, and separately the party was
> boring", which is probably not what was meant.
> ✓ *Only a few people came to the party; it was very boring.*
> ✓ ***Not many** people came to the party; it was very boring.*

The *only a few* version says the party was boring **and** attendance was
low, which is the intended meaning, unambiguously, in seven words.
</DeepDive>

## Negative questions

English forms a negative question by putting *not* in the auxiliary, which
carries surprise, doubt or a request for confirmation.

> *You **are not** coming?* — surprise
> *You **didn't** bring it?* — surprise
> *Why **aren't** you coming?* — a real question, and it sounds
> disappointed

There is a form with no *do* at all, which is a request rather than a
question.

> ***Why not** take a taxi?*
> ***Why not** sit down?*
> ***How about** a coffee?*

These are among the most useful constructions in spoken English, and they
are almost impossible to produce from a grammar book. *Why not* plus the
base verb is a complete sentence.

> *It's cold in here.* — ***Why not** close the window?*
> *I've worked all day.* — ***Why not** take a break?*

## Four things learners get wrong about inversion and negation

**Fronting a negative without inversion.** ✗ *Never I had seen.* → ✓
*Never **had I** seen.*

***Hardly* with *than*.** → *when*: *hardly had I arrived when it rang.*

**Saying *few* when you mean *a few*.** → *only a few*, and the meaning
changes from almost none to some.

**Two negatives that do not cancel.** ✗ *I don't know nothing.* → ✓ *I
don't know anything.* / ✓ *I know nothing.*
