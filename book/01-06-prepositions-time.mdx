# Prepositions of time

English uses three prepositions for time, on the same pattern as place,
and the differences are sharper for time than for space.

## in, at, on for time

| preposition | used with | example |
|---|---|---|
| in | long periods, years, months, seasons, decades | in an hour, in 2020, in July, in summer, in the 1990s |
| at | clock times, nights, festivals, expressions of time | at 3 o'clock, at midnight, at night, at Christmas, at the weekend |
| on | days and dates | on Monday, on 5 May, on a cold day, on Christmas Day |

<Warning>
Three that catch people out every time.
<br><br>
<em>in the morning, in the afternoon, in the evening</em> — but
<em><b>at</b> night</em>. There is no <em>in the night</em>.
<br><br>
<em>in a week</em> but <em>on Monday</em> and <em>at the weekend</em>.
<br><br>
Dates take <em>in</em> before the day, <em>on</em> before the date and
nothing between the two: <em>in</em> June <em>on</em> the 5th <em>at</em>
7 a.m.
</Warning>

## No preposition at all

Some time expressions take none, and putting one in is an error.

| with a preposition | without |
|---|---|
| on Monday | ✗ in Monday |
| on 5 May | ✗ in 5 May |
| in July | ✗ on July |
| in 2020 | ✗ at 2020 |
| at Christmas | ✗ in Christmas |
| at night | ✗ in the night |
| last week | ✗ in the last week |
| next year | ✗ in the next year |
| this morning | ✗ in this morning |

## The comparative timeline

| | past | present | future |
|---|---|---|---|
| | yesterday | today | tomorrow |
| | last week | this week | next week |
| | last month | this month | next month |
| | last year | this year | next year |
| | two days ago | | in two days |
| | in 2020 | | in 2025 |

<Warning>
<em>Two days ago</em> takes <em>ago</em> and no preposition. <em>In two
days</em> takes <em>in</em> and no preposition. <em>Ago</em> already means
"before now", so an extra preposition is redundant, and Spanish learners
add one by reflex: <i>en dos días atrás</i>.
</Warning>

## Duration: for, in, since

These are three different words with three different meanings, and mixing
them up is common.

| | length of time | from a point | complete or not |
|---|---|---|---|
| **for** | ✓ | ✗ | duration only |
| **in** | sometimes | sometimes | tendency |
| **since** | ✗ | ✓ | duration from a start point |
| **during** | ✓ | ✓ | a period, often with a known event |

> I have lived here **for** six years. *(how long)*
> I have lived here **since** 2020. *(from when)*
> I studied **during** the pandemic. *(a period, with context)*
> I will finish **in** two hours. *(after the start)*

<DeepDive>
<em>For</em> and <em>since</em> are the pair that matters most, and they
are not interchangeable.
<br><br>
<em>For</em> takes a length: two days, three months, ten years.
<br><br>
<em>Since</em> takes a starting point: Monday, 2019, I was a child.
<br><br>
<i>For 2019</i> is wrong. <i>Since two years</i> is wrong. Spanish
<i>durante</i> covers both and blurs the distinction, which is why it is
worth learning as two separate items.
<br><br>
<i>During</i> is different again: it does not need a present perfect. *I
studied <b>during</b> the pandemic</i> is past and complete, so no perfect.
</DeepDive>

## Specific versus general times

| specific: with a number or a day | general: without |
|---|---|
| for **two** hours | for hours |
| in **three** days | in days |
| on **Monday** | on Mondays |
| at **seven** o'clock | at night |

<Warning>
<em>In two days</em> means a specific point. <em>In days</em> means
repeatedly, repeatedly, over a period. Spanish often uses a plural in
both places, so <i>en dos días</i> and <i>en días</i> are hard to tell
apart at first, and the difference is worth noticing.
</Warning>

## Ordering with time words

Position in the sentence depends on the word.

| word | position | example |
|---|---|---|
| at, on, in + a point | end of the clause | I arrived **on Monday**. |
| by | before a deadline | Finish **by Friday**. |
| until, till | up to a point | Wait **until tomorrow**. |
| since, for | with perfect, after the verb | I have worked here **since 2019**. |
| during | before a noun | **During** the meeting |
| while | before a clause | **While** I was out |
| as soon as, once | before a clause | **As soon as** you arrive |
| by the time | before a clause | **By the time** I woke up |

<DeepDive>
<em>Until</em> and <em>by</em> are confused constantly and the difference
is about direction.
<br><br>
<em>Until</em> marks the end of a period and takes the time phrase
<b>after</b> it: I waited <b>until 6 o'clock</b>.
<br><br>
<em>By</em> marks a deadline and takes the phrase <b>before</b> it: I will
finish <b>by 6 o'clock</b>.
<br><br>
Same words, opposite order, opposite meaning. Read <i>until</i> as "up to"
and <i>by</i> as "before the deadline" and the order follows.
</DeepDive>

## Time clauses and tense

A clause of time with *when*, *after*, *before*, *as soon as*, *until*
takes the **present simple** for the future, never *will*.

> I will call you **when** I **arrive**. ✓
> I will call you when I **will arrive**. ✗

| | present simple | not will |
|---|---|---|
| when | when I arrive | ✗ when I will arrive |
| before | before I leave | ✗ before I will leave |
| as soon as | as soon as I know | ✗ as soon as I will know |
| until | until I finish | ✗ until I will finish |

<Warning>
This is a direct interference from any language that has a subjunctive
tense form. A form that looks like a future, and a future form after
<i>when</i>, both exist in many first languages. In English the future is
already in the main clause, so the subordinate stays in the present.
<br><br>
The one exception is <em>when</em> meaning "at the time when", a habit
in the past: <i>Every time I went there, it rained</i>. That is past
because the whole situation is past, not because <em>when</em> demands it.
</Warning>
