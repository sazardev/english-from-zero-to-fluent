# -ing or to

Two forms of the same verb, and choosing between them is the most common
decision a learner of English makes. It is also the one most likely to be
decided by feel, which is why it goes wrong so consistently.

## The two forms

| form | name | example |
|---|---|---|
| *-ing* | gerund, participle | *I enjoy **reading**.* |
| *to* + base | infinitive | *I want **to read**.* |

They are not interchangeable, and no rule involving meaning alone will
tell you. You have to learn which verbs prefer which, and which accept
both.

## Verbs that take only *-ing*

These never take *to* + base. The list is short, and it is worth learning
by heart.

| verb | example |
|---|---|
| enjoy | *I enjoy **reading**.* |
| avoid | *She avoided **answering**.* |
| finish | *I have finished **writing**.* |
| mind | *Do you mind **waiting**?* |
| risk | *Don't risk **being** late.* |
| admit | *He admitted **lying**.* |
| deny | *They denied **stealing** it.* |
| suggest | *She suggested **going** instead.* |
| consider | *I am considering **moving**.* |
| imagine | *Can't you imagine **winning**?* |
| postpone | *They postponed **leaving**.* |
| practise | *I practised **swimming**.* |
| miss | *I miss **living** there.* |
| quit | *He quit **studying**.* |

<Warning>
The rule behind the list is worth understanding, because it tells you
which verbs to expect. These are the verbs whose English form is built on
a noun: *enjoy* is originally a noun meaning pleasure, *avoid* a noun
meaning a flight, *miss* a noun meaning loss, *risk* a noun. A noun takes
another noun, so the complement is a gerund.

This is why the set feels arbitrary when you learn it as a list and
obvious when you learn it as a fact about English history. It is also why
new verbs invented in English are far more often *-ing* verbs than *to*
verbs, because the productive pattern is the *-ing* one.
</Warning>

## Verbs that take only *to* + base

| verb | example |
|---|---|
| want | *I want **to go**.* |
| decide | *She decided **to leave**.* |
| hope | *We hope **to see** you.* |
| plan | *They plan **to move**.* |
| offer | *He offered **to drive**.* |
| refuse | *She refused **to sign**.* |
| promise | *I promise **to be* early.** |
| agree | *We agreed **to meet** at eight.* |
| manage | *He managed **to finish**.* |
| afford | *I can't afford **to eat** out.* |
| pretend | *She pretended **to understand**.* |
| seem | *It seems **to be** raining.* |
| appear | *She appears **to be** tired.* |
| fail | *He failed **to arrive**.* |
| choose | *I chose **to stay**.* |

The two sets do not overlap. If you are unsure, the safe default is *to*,
because the *to* verbs are far more common in speech.

## Verbs that take both

These are the interesting ones, because the choice carries meaning.

| verb | *-ing* | *to* + base | the difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| remember | *I remember **locking** it.* | *I remember **to lock** it.* | past vs future duty |
| forget | *I forgot **closing** it.* | *I forgot **to close** it.* | did it vs failed to do it |
| stop | *I stopped **tiring**.* | *I stopped **to rest**.* | ceased vs purpose |
| try | *I tried **swimming**.* | *I tried **to swim**.* | experiment vs attempt |
| regret | *I regret **saying** that.* | *I regret **to say** that.* | what happened vs what is about to |
| go on | *He went on **talking**.* | *He went on **to talk** about it. | same activity vs new topic |
| mean | *This means **leaving** early.* | *I mean **to leave** early.* | consequence vs intention |
| object | *They objected **to being** searched.* | *They objected **to being** searched.* | preposition, so *-ing* |

### Remember and forget

The clearest pair in English, and worth learning as a pair.

> *I remember **locking** the door.* — I did it, and I remember doing it
> *I remember **to lock** the door.* — I have a duty to do it, and I have
> not forgotten about the duty

> *I forgot **taking** my keys.* — I did it, and I did not notice
> *I forgot **to take** my keys.* — I meant to, and I did not

<Warning>
This pair is a reliable explanation for a mistake learners make without
being able to say why. *I forgot to take my keys* means the keys are
still on the table. *I forgot taking my keys* means the keys are in your
pocket and you have no idea where they are.

The two sentences describe completely different situations, and choosing
the wrong one does not merely sound odd, it tells the listener the wrong
thing about your morning.
</Warning>

### Stop

> *He stopped **smoking**.* — the smoking ended
> *He stopped **to smoke**.* — he paused in order to smoke

> *The rain stopped **falling**.* — the rain came to an end
> *The rain stopped **to fall**.* — nonsense; but *He stopped **to
> complain**.*

The *-ing* version says the activity finished. The *to* version says
something else started, and that something else was the reason for
stopping.

### Try

> *I tried **swimming**.* — I gave it a go to see what happens
> *I tried **to swim**.* — I made an effort, and it may have failed

> *I tried **opening** the door.* — experimenting
> *I tried **to open** the door.* — attempting, with effort, probably
> failed

*Try* + *-ing* sounds relaxed. *Try* + *to* sounds like a report of an
effort, and the effort usually did not succeed.

## Verbs that take a noun or a gerund

A handful accept both, and the noun is the older meaning.

> *Thank you **for helping** me.* / *Thank you **for your help**.*
> *I mind **your smoking**.* / *I mind **you smoking**.*
> *There is no point **in arguing**.* / *There is no point **in the
> argument**.*
> *He was accused **of lying**.* / *He was accused **of a lie**.*

The pattern is the same each time: the gerund is more direct and more
common in speech, and the noun is slightly more formal.

<DeepDive>
The gerund is the only English form that can be a noun, an adjective and a
verb at once, and that three-way ambiguity is the source of a family of
errors.

> *His **swimming** is excellent.* — noun
> *a **swimming** pool* — adjective, modifying a noun
> *He is **swimming**.* — verb

When the form follows a preposition it is always a noun, even if the
sentence is about an action.

> *By **swimming** every morning, he built his stamina.*
> *The best time for **swimming** is early morning.*

There is no way to avoid this. *Swimming* is a noun in both, and a
learner who insists on calling it a verb will produce sentences that are
grammatical and wrong.
</DeepDive>

## The decision procedure

Ask three questions, in this order.

**Is the verb in the first list?** Use *-ing*. Always.

**Is the verb in the second list?** Use *to*. Always.

**Is the verb in the third list?** Ask what happens next. If the meaning
is about the past, use *-ing*. If it is about a future intention or duty,
use *to*.

If the verb is not in any list, and it is followed by a noun, it probably
takes *-ing*. If it is followed by a clause starting with a subject, it
probably takes *to*.

> *She suggested **going***. — a noun
> *She suggested **that we go***. — a clause

> *He explained **why the door was locked***. — a clause, so *to* if the
> verb allows it
> *He explained **the problem with the door***. — a noun, so *-ing*
