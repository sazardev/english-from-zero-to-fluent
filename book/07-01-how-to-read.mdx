# How to read

Reading is the phase most learners do badly, and it is the phase with the
highest return. Twenty-five minutes a day of good reading moves you faster
than two hours of grammar exercises, because reading is the only way to
acquire speed, and speed is what makes reading sustainable.

The difference between good and bad reading is not effort. It is whether
you look words up immediately or guess first.

## The two-minute rule

<Warning>
Never look up a word the first time you meet it. Read past it, guess from
the context, and only look it up if you still cannot get through the
sentence.
</Warning>

Looking up immediately teaches you a word. Guessing first teaches you the
word *and* the skill that lets you meet ten thousand unfamiliar words for
the rest of your life: inferring meaning from context.

The two minutes is a floor, not a ceiling. On a good day you will read for
forty minutes without stopping.

## How to guess from context

Six things in a sentence tell you what a word means. In rough order of
reliability.

### 1. The grammar around it

The single most reliable clue, and the one learners use least.

| you see | it probably means |
|---|---|
| *I **___ed** to work* | past, and probably finished |
| *They are **___ing*** | a verb in the -ing form |
| *a very **___** thing* | an adjective |
| *to **___** something* | a verb |
| *the **___** of the problem* | a noun, probably abstract |
| ***___ly*** | almost always an adverb from an adjective |

### 2. Prefixes and suffixes

| part | usually means | examples |
|---|---|---|
| un-, in-, dis-, mis- | not, or wrongly | unhappy, incorrect, dislike |
| re- | again | rewrite, return, rebuild |
| pre-, pro- | before | preview, produce |
| over-, under- | too much, too little | overeat, underestimate |
| -tion, -ment, -ness, -ity | a noun | decision, movement, darkness |
| -ful, -less, -able, -ive | an adjective | useful, careless, comfortable |
| -ly | an adverb | slowly, carefully |

<DeepDive>
Suffixes are more reliable than prefixes and are badly underused. If you
see *-ment* or *-tion*, the word is a noun and you can usually guess the
rest of the sentence's grammar. If you see *-able* or *-less*, it is an
adjective, so it modifies a noun and is probably describing a quality.
<br><br>
This works even when you know none of the individual words in the phrase.
<b>international incomprehensible</b> is a phrase, and you can use it.
</DeepDive>

### 3. The words around it

> The committee rejected the proposal on the grounds of cost.

You do not need to know *proposal* to know the sentence is about a
decision made for financial reasons. *committee*, *rejected*, *grounds*,
*cost* are enough, and three of those four are easy.

### 4. The category

> Put the **tools** back in the **drawer**.

If you know *drawer* and *tools* go together, you have *put* from context
even if you have never been told.

### 5. The contrast

> It was not the **cheapest** option, but the **best** one.

*Cheapest* and *best* are contrasted, and both are about choosing. If you
do not know the first, the second tells you the first is about price.

### 6. The definition in the sentence itself

English explains a lot of things in passing.

> A **habit**, that is, something you do without thinking about it.

You do not need the dictionary.

## The five per cent

<Warning>
A page of graded English should be roughly ninety-five per cent known. If
you understand less than that, the book is too hard and you should drop a
level.
</Warning>

The ninety-five figure sounds severe, and it is the test that matters.
Understanding seventy per cent of a page means you stop every three lines,
and stopping every three lines is not reading, it is decoding.

The practical version: if you have to look up more than one word per
paragraph, stop. Get an easier book.

## Reading speed

| level | comfortable speed | notes |
|---|---|---|
| A2 | 100 to 150 words a minute | every word is work |
| B1 | 150 to 200 | the target |
| B2 | 200 to 300 | comfortable, and the usual exam pace |
| C1 | 300 to 450 | a first reading, not a study reading |
| C2 | 400 to 600 | literary English |

These are *first-read* speeds for material at your level. Speed is not
something to push through, it is a consequence of familiarity: the same
page read ten times becomes fast.

<DeepDive>
The reason a second read is faster is that on the first read you are
decoding, and on the second you are recognising. Almost all of the
apparent difficulty of a text is the first-read cost, and you pay it once.
<br><br>
This is the argument for rereading. A text you struggle with for forty
minutes on Monday and read in eight minutes on Tuesday has not become
easier. You have stopped decoding it.
</DeepDive>

## The three-pass method

For anything difficult, read it three times with three different jobs.

| pass | job | time | result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | skim | 2 minutes | the shape, the topic, whether it is worth it |
| 2 | read properly | 20 minutes | the content, looking up as needed |
| 3 | reread | 8 minutes | the parts you flagged, now fast |

The third pass is the one people skip, and it is where vocabulary becomes
yours rather than borrowed.

## What to do with a word you looked up

<DeepDive>
Three things, in this order:
<br><br>
<b>1. Write the word in a sentence of your own.</b> Not a translation. A
sentence. A word learned in a sentence is available; a word learned in a
word list is not.
<br><br>
<b>2. Decide if it is worth keeping.</b> Most words you look up, you never
need again. A test: would you expect to need it in a book you would
actually read? If not, let it go. Collecting every word you meet is
collecting words you will not use.
<br><br>
<b>3. If it is worth keeping, learn the collocation, not the word.</b>
*Make* and *do* are the same idea and behave differently: *make a
decision*, *do the washing*. Learn them as pairs.
</DeepDive>

## What not to do

| don't | why |
|---|---|
| look up every unknown word | you stop reading, and you learn nothing |
| read a book far above your level | you will not finish it, and finishing is what teaches |
| read only on the phone | long text needs long lines and a quiet setting |
| read the same easy book forty times | you plateau, and you will not notice |
| translate as you read | you read Spanish all day and acquire Spanish |

That last one is the most common and the most damaging. Every word you
translate is a word you could have inferred, and the skill you practise
is the wrong one.

## The habit that works

Twenty-five minutes, at the same time, on a text you chose because you
wanted to read it. Not a textbook, unless you want a textbook. Fiction
travels further, because you will come back to it, and coming back is
the whole mechanism.
