# Verbs and prepositions

A verb followed by a preposition is a single unit. The preposition is part
of the verb, and it is chosen by the verb, not by the noun that follows.

## The pattern

| verb + preposition | example |
|---|---|
| *depend on* | *It depends **on** the answer.* |
| *rely on* | *Don't rely **on** him.* |
| *insist on* | *She insisted **on** paying.* |
| *apologise for* | *I apologised **for** being late.* |
| *account for* | *That accounts **for** the delay.* |
| *apply for* | *He applied **for** the job.* |
| *blame somebody for* | *Don't blame me **for** it.* |
| *congratulate somebody on* | *They congratulated her **on** the result.* |
| *remind somebody of* | *It reminds me **of** my childhood.* |
| *provide somebody with* | *They provided us **with** the data.* |

The four prepositions that do most of the work: *for* (blame, apologise,
account, pay, search, wait), *on* (insist, depend, rely, count, agree,
concentrate), *to* (belong, refer, respond, contribute, object, listen),
*with* (deal, agree, provide, cope, familiar).

<Warning>
The preposition is not chosen by the noun. That is the assumption behind
almost every error in this area, and it is always wrong.

> *It depends **on** the weather.* — not *depends of*, not *depends to*
> *It consists **of** three parts.* — not *consists in*
> *She apologised **for** the mistake.* — not *apologised of*
> *He suffers **from** asthma.* — not *suffers with*
> *She objected **to** the proposal.* — not *objected the proposal*

You cannot derive the preposition from the meaning of the noun. Learn
them as whole verbs, the way you would learn a word in any language.
</Warning>

## The ones that get mixed up

| verb | preposition | a different verb with another preposition |
|---|---|---|
| *depend* | on | *insist* on, *concentrate* on |
| *consist* | of | *result* in |
| *concentrate* | on | *congratulate* on |
| *apologise* | for | *apply* for, *wait* for, *search* for |
| *suffer* | from | *suffer* under |
| *recover* | from | *depend* on |
| *refer* | to | *prefer* to, *object* to |
| *belong* | to | *contribute* to |
| *listen* | to | *listen for*, *listen in* |
| *account* | for | *account* to |
| *blame* | somebody for | *blame* something on somebody |
| *provide* | somebody with | *provide* something for somebody |
| *remind* | somebody of | *remind* somebody about |
| *result* | in | *result* from |
| *substitute* | A for B | *replace* A with B |

<DeepDive>
The pair that causes most damage is *blame* and *provide*, because the
two objects swap places and the two prepositions swap with them, and the
result is a sentence that is grammatical, idiomatic, and about something
else entirely.

> *Don't **blame** me **for** the mistake.* — I made the mistake
> *Don't **blame** the mistake **on** me.* — also correct, and the
> mistake is the subject now

> *They **provided** us **with** the data.* — we received the data
> *They **provided** the data **for** us.* — also correct, and the data is
> the subject now

Both orders work for both verbs, and the difference is only which noun is
in the subject slot. What does not work is mixing them up.

> ✗ *Don't blame the mistake on me being careless.*
> ✓ *Don't blame me for making a careless mistake.*
> ✓ *Don't put the mistake on me being careless.*

Notice that the correct third sentence replaces the prepositional
construction with a gerund, which is the general escape route when a
preposition has no obvious home: **turn the phrase into an *-ing* phrase
and the preposition problem disappears.**
</DeepDive>

## Adjective + preposition

The same phenomenon with adjectives, and here the preposition is even
harder because there is no verb to attach it to.

| adjective | preposition | example |
|---|---|---|
| *good* | at | *She's good **at** maths.* |
| *bad* | at | *He's bad **at** spelling.* |
| *interested* | in | *I'm interested **in** history.* |
| *bored* | with / by | *I'm bored **with** waiting.* |
| *worried* | about | *She's worried **about** the exam.* |
| *surprised* | at / by | *I'm surprised **at** how fast he reads.* |
| *proud* | of | *They're proud **of** their son.* |
| *afraid* | of | *I'm afraid **of** dogs.* |
| *capable* | of | *She's capable **of** much more.* |
| *conscious* | of | *He was conscious **of** the risk.* |
| *responsible* | for | *Who's responsible **for** this?* |
| *famous* | for | *He's famous **for** his cooking.* |
| *similar* | to | *Their views are similar **to** mine.* |
| | | *different* **from** |
| | | *similar* **to** |

<Warning>
Two pairs are reversed in a way that produces confident, fluent errors,
so they are worth memorising as pairs.

**Similar to** and **different from.**

> ✓ *Her view is similar **to** mine.*
> ✗ *Her view is similar **from** mine.*
> ✓ *Her view is different **from** mine.*
> ✗ *Her view is different **to** mine.*

**Interested in** and **good at**. The noun after the preposition changes
meaning, and the confusion is between the activity and the subject.

> ✓ *I'm interested **in** photography.* — the activity
> ✗ *I'm interested on photography.*
> ✓ *She's good **at** arguing.* — the skill
> ✗ *She's good in arguing.*
> ✓ *She's good **with** children.* — a general suitability
> ✓ *She's good **at** maths.* — a specific skill
> ✓ *She's good **for** something.* — usefulness
> ✓ *She's good **to** somebody.* — kind

Four prepositions, four different senses of *good*, and no way to guess
which is which from the meaning of *good*.
</Warning>

## Prepositions after *whether*

*Whether* takes *or* or *if*, and no preposition before *or not*.

> *I wonder **whether or not** he agrees.*
> ✓ *I wonder **if** he agrees.*
> ✗ *I wonder whether he agrees **or not** is true.* *(ungrammatical)*

*If* for a concession takes no *whether*: *Although it is expensive, if
you like it, buy it.*

<DeepDive>
## When the preposition is missing

Some sentences are correct with the preposition and correct without, and
the two are not interchangeable.

> *I agree **with** you.* / *I agree.* ✓
> ✗ *I agree you.* *(informal dialect, and wrong in writing)*

> *She arrived **on** Monday.* / *She arrived.* ✓
> ✗ *She arrived Monday.*

> *He married **her**.* / *He married her friend.* ✓
> ✗ *He married with her.*

> *I waited **for** two hours.* ✓
> ✗ *I waited two hours.* *(this means I remained for that time, which is
> a different claim)*

> *It depends **on** you.* / *It depends.* ✓
> ✗ *It depends you.*

The rule: the bare form is allowed only when the sentence still has a
complete meaning without the preposition. If removing it removes the
object, the preposition stays.
</DeepDive>

## Four things learners get wrong about prepositions

**Choosing the preposition from the noun.** ✗ *It depends of the answer.*
→ *It depends **on** the answer.*

**Similar to, different from.** Reversed, and the error is confident.

**Four senses of *good*.** *good at* (skill), *good with* (general
suitability), *good for* (usefulness), *good to* (kindness).

**Dropping a preposition in formal writing.** *I agree you* is spoken
dialect and will be marked wrong in anything you write.
