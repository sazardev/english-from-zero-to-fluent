# Word formation

English builds most of its vocabulary from about seventy affixes. Learning
the affixes is learning several thousand words at once, and it is the
highest-yield vocabulary work available to a learner.

## The four processes

| process | adds | example |
|---|---|---|
| affixation | a prefix, a suffix, an infix | *un-happy, quick-ly, un-repeat-able* |
| compounding | two words | *bookshelf, decide* |
| conversion | no affix at all, a new part of speech | *water* → *to water* |
| clipping | a shortened form | *photograph* → *photo* |

## Prefixes

Prefixes are negations, intensifiers, or names of a quality.

### Prefixes that negate

| prefix | meaning | example |
|---|---|---|
| *un-* | not | *unhappy, unlikely, unfair* |
| *in-* | not | *invisible, incorrect, impossible* |
| *im-* | not, before *p*, *m* | *impossible, immobile* |
| *il-* | not, before *l* | *illegal, illogical* |
| *ir-* | not, before *r* | *irregular, irrelevant* |
| *dis-* | not, or the opposite of | *disagree, dislike, disable* |
| *non-* | not, neutral | *non-smoker, non-profit* |
| *mis-* | wrongly | *mistake, misunderstand, misspell* |
| *re-* | back, again | *return, rewrite, rebuild* |

The *in-* family changes its first letter before a labial and an *l* or
*r*. This is phonological, not orthographic, and it applies to
*in-*, *im-*, *il-*, *ir-* and also to the negative prefix on *-able*.

> *impossible, immobile, illegal, irrational*
> *unable, unclear, unrealistic* — *un-* also does this before *-able*

<Warning>
*Un-* has two meanings, and they are opposites in practice.

**Reversing a word that already had an opposite.** *untie* means the
opposite of *tie*, so untying a knot means **opening** it, not closing it
again. *unzip* means opening. *unwind* means the opposite of winding.

**Forming a new word for something that did not have one.** *unhappy* is
not the opposite of *happy*, which is *sad*. It is a new word meaning
"not happy in a way worth naming separately". *unlikely*, *unfair*,
*unwilling*.

The trap is in the first group, and it produces the opposite of the
intended meaning.

> ✓ *He untied the rope.* — he loosened it
> ✗ *He untied the rope* — understood as "he tied it again"
> ✓ *She unwrapped the sandwich.*
> ✓ *They undid the damage.*
> ✓ *He unpacked the suitcase.*

If the base word means a reversible action, *un-* reverses it. If you are
unsure, ask what the reverse of the action would be and check that *un-*
gives that answer.
</Warning>

### Other prefixes

| prefix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *re-* | again | *rewrite, rebuild, repay* |
| *pre-* | before | *preview, prewar, predisposed* |
| *post-* | after | *postwar, postgraduate, postpone* |
| *over-* | too much | *oversleep, overreact, overestimate* |
| *under-* | too little | *underestimate, undercook, understate* |
| *sub-* | under, secondary | *subway, subordinate, subscript* |
| *super-* | above, better | *supermarket, superb, supernatural* |
| *anti-* | against | *antivirus, antisocial* |
| *co-* | together | *cooperate, coexist, coincide* |
| *de-* | remove, reverse | *defrost, deactivate, depose* |
| *dis-* | apart | *disconnect, disperse, discard* |

*Over-* and *under-* are a matched pair, and mixing them up reverses a
claim.

> *overestimate* — to say too high / *underestimate* — to say too low
> *overreact* — too strong / *underreact* — too weak
> *overcook* — too long / *undercook* — not long enough
> *overweight* — above the usual weight / *underweight* — below it

The direction is obvious once stated and inverted constantly, which is why
it is worth a list rather than a rule.

## Suffixes

### Nouns

| suffix | makes | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-tion, -sion* | an action or a state | *action, decision, revision* |
| *-ment* | a result or an action | *movement, government, argument* |
| *-ness* | a quality | *happiness, darkness, willingness* |
| *-ity, -ty* | a quality | *ability, honesty, curiosity* |
| *-ance, -ence* | a state | *importance, absence, independence* |
| *-ship* | a state or a role | *friendship, championship* |
| *-hood* | a state | *childhood, adulthood* |
| *-dom* | a state or a domain | *freedom, kingdom* |
| *-er, -or* | a person or a thing | *teacher, actor, computer* |
| *-ist* | a person | *dentist, novelist, scientist* |
| *-ee* | a person acted upon | *employee, trainee* |
| *-age* | an action, a collection | *passage, mileage, shortage* |
| *-ure* | an action or a result | *failure, pressure, closure* |

### Verbs

| suffix | makes | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-ise, -ize* | to make or to become | *realise, criticise, organize* |
| *-ify* | to make | *simplify, verify, notify* |
| *-en* | to make or to become | *widen, shorten, strengthen* |
| *-ate* | to act on | *create, calculate, operate* |
| *-fy* | informal, to make | *beautify, simplify, notify* |

### Adjectives

| suffix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-ful* | full of | *useful, careful, beautiful* |
| *-less* | without | *useless, careless, endless* |
| *-ous* | full of, having the quality of | *famous, dangerous, curious* |
| *-ive* | having the quality of | *active, creative, expensive* |
| *-able, -ible* | can be | *readable, possible, comfortable* |
| *-al* | relating to | *national, musical, personal* |
| *-ic* | relating to | *basic, dramatic, economic* |
| *-ish* | slightly, or belonging to | *reddish, childish, British* |
| *-y* | having the quality of | *rainy, healthy, noisy* |
| *-like* | resembling | *childlike, lifelike* |
| *-worthy* | deserving | *trustworthy, worthwhile* |

<DeepDive>
The suffixes are not interchangeable, and a learner who picks by
sound produces words that are wrong in a way no speaker can guess at.
The trap pairs are all *-ful* against *-less* and *-ous* against *-ive*.

> **useful** — full of use → **useless** — without use
> **careful** — full of care → **careless** — without care
> **fearful** — afraid → **fearless** — not afraid

> **expensive** — having the quality of costing a lot → **expensively**
> **famous** — full of fame → **fame** *(no adjectival partner)*
> **curious** — having curiosity → **curiosity** *(noun, not adjective)*
> **various** — having variety → **variety** *(noun)*

The second trap is the noun suffixes. The same root makes a different
part of speech with each one, and picking the wrong one changes the
meaning rather than the grammar.

> *decide* → *decision* — the act
> *decide* → *decisive* — the quality
> *decide* → *decided* — the state

> *inform* → *information* — what you are given
> *inform* → *informative* — full of information
> *inform* → *informed* — having been told

Notice also that many *-tion* nouns drop the *-e* or the *-d* of the base:
*decide* → *decision*, *inform* → *information*, *describe* → *description*.
The *-tion* suffix does not simply get attached; it changes the spelling
of the root, and the change is not predictable from the suffix alone.
</DeepDive>

## Compounds

Two nouns, or a noun and a verb, joined. English is built on these.

| type | example | examples |
|---|---|---|
| noun + noun | *bookshelf* | *bookcase, bedroom, smartphone* |
| noun + verb | *sunrise* | *sunset, earthquake, bookshelf* |
| verb + noun | *washroom* | *breakfast, cupboard* |
| adjective + noun | *blackboard* | *newcomer, grandson* |
| adverb + noun | *underground* | *overview, upbringing* |

The stress tells you which element is the head, and the head is the part
that keeps its meaning.

> *book*·*case* — the case is for books. Head: *case*
> *book*·*shelf* — the shelf is for books. Head: *shelf*
> *green*·*house* — the house is green. Head: *house*
> *house*·*work* — the work is at the house. Head: *work*

<Warning>
A compound is one word if the elements form a single unit, two if they
stay separate. Getting this wrong changes the parsing, not just the
spacing.

> *a **school**·*teacher* — a teacher at a school ✓
> ✗ *a **schoolteacher*** — rare, and sounds archaic
> *a **school** **teacher** — a teacher, whose job is teaching at a school

> *some**one** — a person
> *some **one** — some person in particular
> *every**thing*** — everything
> *every **thing** — every individual object
> *any**thing*** vs *any **thing***

> *a **book**·*case*
> *a **book** **case** — a legal case about a book

A learner who writes *some one* or *any thing* has not made a spelling
error; they have made four words where the reader expects one, and the
meaning is genuinely different.
</Warning>

## Conversion

Changing a word's part of speech with no change to its form. English does
this constantly and it is a large part of why the language feels fast.

| noun to verb | example |
|---|---|
| *water* | *to water* the plants |
| *book* | *to book* a table |
| *name* | *to name* someone |
| *hand* | *to hand* someone a pen |
| *answer* | *to answer* |
| *control* | *to control* |
| *photograph* | *to photograph* |

| verb to noun | example |
|---|---|
| *to run* | *a **run*** |
| *to walk* | *a **walk*** |
| *to fail* | *a **failure*** — this one takes a suffix |
| *to succeed* | *a **success*** — and *-cc-* |
| *to sing* | *a **singing*** |
| *to build* | *a **building*** |

| adjective to noun | example |
|---|---|
| *kind* | *her **kindness*** |
| *happy* | *her **happiness*** |
| *strong* | *its **strength*** |
| *wide* | *its **width*** |
| *long* | *its **length*** |

*Length, width, height, strength, depth* are irregular, and a learner who
expects *longness* will meet it in dictionaries and not in speech.

## Four things learners get wrong

***Un-* on a reversible verb, meaning the wrong direction.** *untie*,
*unzip*, *unwind*, *undo*, *unpack*.

**The wrong suffix for the part of speech.** *decision* for the act,
*decisive* for the quality, *decided* for the state.

**Spelling a compound as two words.** *some one*, *any thing*, *with out*
are different claims from *someone*, *anything*, *without*.

**Adding two intensifiers.** ✗ *quite totally empty* → ✓ *quite empty* /
✓ *totally empty*.
