# The guided track

Parts 0 to 11 are a reference that happens to be ordered. Part 12 is
something else: a route through it, one day at a time, with the exercises
already chosen and the order already decided.

Read this chapter first if you have never used this book. Then read
12.1, the twelve-week programme, and start on the week you are on rather
than the week you should be on.

## Why a route is needed

A thousand-page grammar book has a specific failure mode. A learner opens
it, reads forty pages, feels that they have learned something, does not
know what to do next, and stops. Three weeks later they open it again,
read a different forty pages, and stop again. A year later they own a
grammar book and cannot write a letter.

The failure is not laziness. It is that grammar books are organised by
**topic** and learners need to be organised by **sequence**. Everything in
English depends on something earlier, and a reader who jumps to the
present perfect because it looks useful has to understand the past simple
first, and the past simple is 200 pages back.

Part 12 removes the decisions. Every day has one job, the job is small
enough to finish, and the exercises for it are named. The only thing left
to decide is whether to do it, which is the only decision that matters.

<Warning>
The most common way to fail with a guided track is to skip the daily work
because the reference chapters are more interesting. The reference
chapters are more interesting. That is not an argument for reading them
first, it is an argument for reading them **later**, when the daily work
has given them something to attach to.

A grammar rule you meet while trying to write something is a rule you
remember. A grammar rule you meet while reading about grammar is a rule
you recognise once and never use.

The test: after any reading session, you should be able to say what you
produced. Not what you learned. What you produced.
</Warning>

## What a day looks like

Thirty minutes, four blocks, the same four blocks every day. Part 0 sets
out the method; this is what it looks like when it is a specific half
hour.

| minutes | block | what you do |
|---|---|---|
| 25 | read | the day's chapter, or 10 pages of a reader |
| 15 | write | 100 to 150 words on anything, then correct it |
| 15 | listen | 20 minutes with delayed subtitles |
| 10 | review | only what failed today |

That is 65 minutes, which is more than the plan allows. The real
arrangement, and the one the programme uses, is:

| minutes | block | what you do |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | review | the day's due cards, nothing else |
| 15 | read | one section, 3 to 5 pages |
| 15 | write | 100 words |
| 10 | listen | delayed subtitles |

Forty minutes. On a bad day, do the ten minutes of review and nothing else.
The streak is worth more than any single session, and the ten minutes is
the part that has to survive a bad day.

## How to correct what you write

This is the step everybody skips and it is the one that makes writing
work. You do not need a person.

1. **Read it aloud.** Errors of *-s*, missing articles and wrong verb
   forms are audible. Your ear catches what your eye skips.
2. **Search for your five errors.** Part 8.6 lists the fourteen that cost
   the most. Look for those five first, every time, in that order.
3. **Check the verb of every sentence.** The tense has to match the time
   relation in your head, and part 2 is the reference.
4. **Look up three words**, no more.
5. **Write every error in the log**, in your own words, with the rule in
   one line.

That takes ten minutes on 150 words, and it is worth more than an hour of
writing more words without it. Part 8.6 gives the proofreading order and
explains why it is that order.

<DeepDive>
There is a reason the daily work is written rather than read, and it is
the whole architecture of this book.

Reading builds recognition. Recognition is fast, feels like progress, and
is what a grammar book optimises for, because a grammar book is written by
someone who already knows the language and cannot remember what it was
like not to.

Writing builds production. Production is slow, feels like failure, and is
what actually works, because a learner who cannot produce a sentence has
not learned it no matter how many rules they can state.

The consequence for how you use this book is specific. **Read the
explanation, then immediately write something that needs it.** Not later
today. Immediately, while the rule is in working memory and the connection
between the rule and a sentence can still be made.

A chapter read without writing leaves you able to talk about the third
person *-s* and unable to produce it. That is not a knowledge problem, it
is a transfer problem, and the transfer only happens through the act of
writing. Part 12.3 is a bank of worked examples for exactly this: every
chapter has one, so that the gap between recognising a rule and producing
it is as short as a page.
</DeepDive>

## What to do when you fall behind

You will. The plan assumes you will, and this is the rule.

**Never make it up.** Do not double up on a day. If you are three days
behind, you are three days behind, and the next session is the next
session in the programme. This sounds obvious and almost nobody does it,
because the guilt of being behind is stronger than the discipline to
ignore it, and the result is a weekend spent reading six chapters and
remembering none of them.

**Halve it instead of doubling it.** If you genuinely have ninety minutes
one day, do two short sessions rather than one long one, spaced apart.
Spacing beats duration: the same ninety minutes in one block gives you
one consolidation, and in two blocks gives you two, and the second one
retrieval is worth more than the extra forty minutes.

**Drop the writing block, not the reading.** If you can only manage
twenty minutes a day, read. The writing is what converts the reading, so
it is the first casualty, and dropping the reading instead means the
writing has nothing to produce from.

<Warning>
The one thing that ends a learning programme is treating a missed day as
evidence that the programme is not working.

A missed day is a missed day. It says nothing about the method, and it
says everything about the day you had. The reasoning that gets people to
quit is always the same shape: *I missed Tuesday, so I have already failed,
so starting again on Monday is the sensible thing.* But the Tuesday you
missed has not been banked against Monday. Monday is a fresh day with the
same content, and the version of you that shows up on Monday has lost
nothing.

The measurement that matters is the trend over a month, not the streak
today. One bad week in twelve is not a failure of the method, it is what
using the method looks like.
</Warning>

## How to know it is working

Four measurements, taken monthly, in writing. Nothing else counts.

| what to record | why |
|---|---|
| days completed out of 30 | the habit, which is the base |
| words written | the production, which is the point |
| errors in the log | the accuracy, which is the product |
| stops per minute when speaking | the fluency, which is the result |

The fourth one is the one people skip and it is the only one that measures
the thing the book is for. Part 0.4 has the tasks; pick one you have not
prepared for, record yourself for two minutes, and count the stops. Do
that in week 1 and again at the end, and the difference is the number that
tells you whether to continue.
