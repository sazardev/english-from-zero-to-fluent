# The present

English has three present tenses and they are not interchangeable. The
distinction between them is not about time. It is about how much of the
sentence you are asserting.

## The three presents

| tense | form | what it asserts |
|---|---|---|
| present simple | *I work* | a fact, a habit, a state |
| present continuous | *I am working* | unfinished, right now, changing |
| present perfect | *I have worked* | finished, with a result now |

That is the whole system. Everything else in this chapter is detail.

## Present simple

Three uses, and only three.

**Facts and states.** Something true regardless of when you say it.

> Water *boils* at 100 degrees.
> The shop *opens* at nine.
> She *lives* in Seville.

**Habits.** Something repeated, and the repetition is the point.

> I *drink* coffee every morning.
> They *go* to the gym on Mondays.

**Fixed future arrangements.** A time in the future is agreed and written
down. This use is why the present simple is the tense of timetables.

> The train *leaves* at 6:40 tomorrow.
> I *see* the doctor on Friday.

<Warning>
The third use is the one that causes errors. A present simple sentence
with a future time is not an error, it is a *schedule*. What is an error
is using it for a decision you have just made.

> ✗ I *will meet* you tomorrow at eight. *(a plan, not a timetable)*
> ✓ I *meet* you tomorrow at eight. *(it is in the diary)*
> ✓ I *will meet* you tomorrow at eight. *(you just agreed to it now)*

If the time was fixed before this conversation, present simple. If this
conversation is what fixed it, *will*.
</Warning>

### The third person *-s*

The single most common error in written English at every level above
beginner, because the ending is easy to forget when the subject is far
away.

> She *writes* every day. / She *write* every day.
> He *runs* to work. / He *run* to work.
> It *rains* in November. / It *rain* in November.

Three things make it hard. The subject is often a long noun phrase
separated from the verb by other words. The negative adds an auxiliary
that pushes *-s* onto the wrong word. And the third person singular covers
*he*, *she*, *it* and singular *they*, but not *I*, *you*, *we*, *they*.

| subject | positive | negative | question |
|---|---|---|---|
| I / you / we / they | *I work* | *I don't work* | *Do I work?* |
| he / she / it | *She works* | *She doesn't work* | *Does she work?* |

The *-s* moves to the auxiliary when there is one, and only to the
auxiliary.

> ✓ He *doesn't work* on Sundays.
> ✗ He *don't work* on Sundays.
> ✗ He *doesn't works* on Sundays.

<DeepDive>
When the verb is irregular, the *-s* is the only thing marking the third
person, so the form is the base form plus *-s* and nothing else.

> *go* → *goes*
> *have* → *has*
> *do* → *does*

This is why *does* is irregular in the negative and the question. If *did*
carried the past and *-s*, English would have to remember three endings at
once. Instead the *-s* is frozen into the auxiliary and the main verb
returns to its base form.

> ✓ She *doesn't go* to work on Fridays.
> ✓ *Does* she *go* to work on Fridays?

Every *-s* question in English works this way. Learn it once, here.
</DeepDive>

## Present continuous

**Now, or around now.** The action is unfinished at the moment of
speaking.

> I *am reading* your email right now.
> Look, it *is raining*.
> What *are you doing* this weekend?

**A period around now**, not a moment. The action may have started before
this conversation and may continue after it.

> She *is working* on a book about verbs.
> They *are living* in Berlin until September.

**A changing state**, contrasted with a fixed fact. This is the use that
carries the contrast.

> The world *is warming*. / The world *is* warm.
> Prices *are rising*. / Prices *rise* every year.
> I *am understanding* it now. / I *understand* it.

<Warning>
Some verbs cannot be continuous. They describe a state, not an action, so
putting them in the continuous either is wrong or means something else.

| verb | state, simple only | continuous means something else |
|---|---|---|
| know | I *know* the answer. | ✗ I am knowing ✗ |
| like | I *like* it. | ✗ I am liking ✗ |
| want | I *want* tea. | ✗ I am wanting ✗ |
| believe | I *believe* you. | ✗ I am believing ✗ |
| belong | It *belongs* to me. | ✗ is belonging ✗ |
| seem | It *seems* fine. | ✗ is seeming ✗ |
| own | She *owns* a car. | ✗ is owning ✗ |
| consist | It *consists* of water. | ✗ is consisting ✗ |

The right-hand column is not a list of near-misses. *I am wanting* means
*begin to want*, and *is belonging* is not English at all.
</Warning>

### *Be* as the main verb

When *be* is the main verb, the continuous adds nothing and is wrong.

> ✗ I *am being* tired. → ✓ I *am tired*.
> ✗ She *is being* at home. → ✓ She *is at home*.

*Be* continuous appears only when *be* is an auxiliary: *You are working
too hard*, *The children were being noisy*.

## Present perfect, and the one rule that governs it

The present perfect is the tense people avoid, and avoiding it is the
single largest cause of a learner sounding unfinished.

It has two halves. The **past** part says when. The **present** part says
whether it still matters. If it still matters, present perfect.

> I *have read* that book. *(and I remember it, it is relevant now)*
> I *read* that book in 2019. *(finished, closed, irrelevant now)*

> She *has left*. *(she is not here, and that is the current fact)*
> ✗ She *has left* in 2019.

> I *have lost* my keys. *(I still cannot get in)*
> ✗ I *lost* my keys. *(story over, keys found, move on)*

<Warning>
The present perfect cannot have a finished time expression. Not *when*,
not *yesterday*, not a year, not *in 2019*, not *when I was a child*.
The reason is structural: the perfect is built on the present, and a
finished time puts the sentence in the past.

> ✓ I *have finished* the report.
> ✗ I *have finished* the report yesterday.
> ✓ I *finished* the report yesterday.

The moment you name a finished time, the present perfect is impossible.
Use the past simple.

This is the opposite of what many learners expect. A present perfect feels
to them like "the distant past", and it is in fact "the past that still
counts".
</Warning>

### Since and for

The present perfect is the tense these two words require.

> *for* + a period → *I have lived here **for** six years.*
> *since* + a point → *I have lived here **since** 2019.*

Both mean the situation started in the past and is still true. Neither
takes a finished time.

> ✗ I have lived here for six years **ago**.
> ✓ I lived there six years ago. *(a period, not a continuing situation)*

### Three states, not two

English marks three distances from the present, and picking the wrong one
is a meaning error, not a grammar error.

| distance | tense | example |
|---|---|---|
| unfinished now | present continuous | *I am working on it.* |
| finished, still relevant | present perfect | *I have worked on it.* |
| finished, closed | past simple | *I worked on it last week.* |

Notice that all three of these are true at the same moment. The speaker
is choosing how much of the past is still alive.

## Stative verbs

Some verbs describe a state and take the simple, never the continuous:
*know, believe, like, love, hate, want, need, prefer, own, belong, seem,
cost, resemble, contain, consist, include, exist, matter, depend*.

> ✓ I *prefer* tea.  ✗ I am preferring tea.
> ✓ That *cost* ten pounds.  ✗ That is costing ten pounds.

They are called stative, from the Latin for "standing". They name a
position rather than an action, so there is nothing to be in the middle
of.

<DeepDive>
A few of them have a continuous form with a genuinely different meaning,
and it is worth knowing because the change of meaning is regular: the
continuous means the beginning or the end of the state.

> *I think* it is true. → *I am thinking* about it. *(the process)*
> *I know* the answer. → *I am knowing* him better. *(gradual change)*
> *He lives* in Berlin. → *He is living* in Berlin. *(temporarily)*
> *The shop sells* bread. → *The shop is selling* bread. *(now, at a profit)*

The pattern: the continuous means the state is *developing or in force at
this moment*, not that it holds. Where the continuous sounds odd in your
mouth, the simple is right.
</DeepDive>

## The present, all three, side by side

> **I am writing** a book. *(right now)*
> **I have written** a book. *(finished, the book exists)*
> **I write** books. *(this is what I do)*

> **She is living** in Berlin. *(at this moment, possibly temporary)*
> **She has lived** in Berlin. *(at some point, she is there now)*
> **She lives** in Berlin. *(her home)*

> **They are thinking** about it. *(in progress)*
> **They have thought** about it. *(considered, done with it)*
> **They think** about it a lot. *(a habit)*

## A decision procedure

When you are unsure, ask two questions in this order.

**Is it finished, with a result now?** Use the present perfect.

> I *have* just *finished*. You can see the paint.

**Is it unfinished, or in progress, or temporary?** Use the present
continuous.

> I *am painting*. The room is not ready.

**Otherwise?** Use the present simple.

> I *paint* on weekends.

If a finished time is named anywhere in the sentence, the answer is
automatically the past simple, whatever the verb.

> I *finished* it **last night**.
> I *have finished* it. ✓
> ✗ I have finished it last night.

<Warning>
Three errors account for most of what is wrong with the present tense in
learner writing. Learn them by their names and you will recognise them in
your own work.

**The missing third person *-s*.** *She write* → *She writes*.

**The continuous for a state.** *I am knowing* → *I know*.

**The present perfect with a finished time.** *I have seen him yesterday*
→ *I saw him yesterday*.

The first is a missing letter. The second is a wrong tense. The third is
two tenses fighting each other, and it is the one that survives longest,
because the sentence inside your head is right even when the English is
not.
</Warning>
